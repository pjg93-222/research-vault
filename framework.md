# Paul's research framework

## 1. What the record contains

Paul's annotation corpus has three layers, and each one is a different kind of evidence.

- **Twelve bibliography documents** sit under the *Annotated Bibliographies & Literature Synthesis* zip. Only seven of them carry Paul's own prose: Curated Annotations, Lobbying, Machine Learning & Sentiment, Social Network Analysis, Path-Finding (two of its nine entries), the Trey report, and the Adaptive Influence Maximization addendum. The other five (Adjacent, Campaign Politics, Causal & Methodological, Neural Networks, Optimization) are bare citation lists or bare links. Several prose entries recur across folders word for word: Birkenmaier & Lechner 2025, Kim J. et al. 2025, Huneeus & Kim 2018, Furnas et al. 2022, Fink et al. 2023, Kim & Kunisky 2020, Malaterre & Lareau 2024, and Rongkuan et al. 2024. Curated Annotations is the distilled core, built from those recurring entries.
- **The PDF highlight digest** covers about 60 PDFs. It holds thousands of highlights but only about ten real margin notes. They are short and pointed, for example "How does this translate into an edge?" (Gaynor 2022), "Important! Coalition" (Ardekani 2026), "Most important part" (the D-NOMINATE utility function in Poole & Rosenthal 1999), and "Very interesting" (D* dynamic replanning in Lawande 2022). Two notes carry substance: the complex-contagion note ("What about the impact of high ranking members? Not diminishing.") and the note on Bonica's contribution model (both sides must meet, sunk cost, a short-term theory of engagement). Several PDFs Paul highlighted heavily never entered any bibliography: Sotoudeh et al. 2024, Box-Steffensmeier et al. 2018, Merkley & Stecula, Bakker et al., Jacoby et al. 2025, Azaiez & Mandel 2025, Bonica 2013, the Kim/Stuckatz/Wolters working paper, and Chen et al. 2021/2023.
- **Working memos** from the extraction set are not literature. They include the sector-growth research plan, the contact-inference design and evaluation plan, the staffer-network plan of record, the forecast rechecks, the telecom and cross-industry reports, and the approach-brief handoff. They show the method Paul now applies to the literature: preregistration, single looks, circularity bans, and "verify against the source PDF".

The bibliography therefore records an earlier stage of thinking, from roughly July 2026 (the `bib-20260705` version) to its October re-export. The systems as built have since gone well past it in several places.

## 2. The causal story

Read across the files, Paul's annotations tell one composite story in three linked chains, with a contextual layer around them. The chains are Paul's reading. Where the papers' own designs are observational, the links below are stated as associations.

### Chain A: money leads to access, which leads to influence, and that sets allocation

The lobbying literature, as Paul reads it, says that political spending pays the firm and costs the economy. Alexander et al. 2009 report a 22,000% return, which Paul calls "an outlier" but "a dramatic illustration". That figure is about 220:1, meaning $62bn of estimated tax savings on $282.7M of LDA-reported lobbying. It covers the 93 lobbying firms among 476 repatriating firms under the one-time 2004 AJCA repatriation deduction, with 10-Ks searched for 2004–2006. The per-company median is 172:1. Huneeus & Kim 2018 use an instrumental-variable design on US public firms in Compustat matched to LDA filings from 1999–2015. Their headline is that 10% more lobbying brings 3% more revenue. That is the full-sample column (IV elasticity 0.319, regression N 15,332 firm-years). The paper's stated preferred estimate is the post-2007 column, 0.2% more revenue per 1% more lobbying (0.227, N 8,796). The first-stage F is 13–14. The paper also finds misallocation across the economy. Bickmore et al. 2025 is observational. Using Compustat with CRP data for 1998–2016 (116,242 firm-years with full controls), it finds that a lobbying firm's share of its industry's assets is 7.4 percentage points higher (0.0743; 0.0907 without controls). Paul's source text gives this as "7.5–10.5% higher market concentration".

Paul then asks how money becomes access. Kim, Stuckatz & Wolters (working paper 2020; later version 2023, cited by Paul as JOP 2026) study US public manufacturing firms from 2008–2018 (2008–2017 in the 2020 draft). After a first-time donation to a member, the donor firm is more likely to lobby bills that member sponsored, cosponsored or handled in committee. In the later version's matched difference-in-differences, the gap is about 8 pp in the year of the donation and about 11 pp by t+3 in the Senate (3,778 first-time-donation instances), and 8.6 to 12.7 pp in the House (5,497 instances). The base rate is about 0.07. The association is strongest for committee-related lobbying. This is an association, not an effect: the authors say their estimates "should not be interpreted as causal" (pp.3, 29), and the outcome is firm lobbying on member-linked bills, not access to the member. Furnas, LaPira & Brock 2025 add that lobbying and PAC giving rarely coincide. Pooled over 1998–2018, 3,427 of 54,517 organizations (6.3%) did both; that denominator includes inactive organizations. The yearly overlap is 10–15% of politically active organizations. Furnas et al. 2022 (the "More than mere access" staffer experiment) supply the information channel: "epistemic closure among partisan staffers", and a preference for evidence from ideologically aligned think tanks. The vault holds the 2019 APSA version of that experiment (`furnas-2019-moneyed-interests-information`), a stated-intentions survey from 2017 of N=441 staff. Split by party, it puts the penalty for non-aligned evidence at about twice the benefit of aligned evidence, and the pooled estimate is null.

Paul draws a practical conclusion. Huneeus & Kim become a way to set fundraising targets, and Furnas implies that allocation should include funding industry-aligned think tanks. In his reading, ideological infrastructure "cultivates" champions rather than recruiting them. His stated gaps are that donations are not separated from events and meetings, and that representatives are not differentiated. He proposes a "state layer" built on industry presence to fill the second gap.

### Chain B: networks give paths, and paths carry persuasion

The SNA and Path-Finding folders argue that influence travels along observable relationships. If you can build the graph, you can route through it. Haythornthwaite 1996 is the theoretical root (Paul tags it "Priority – Medium – Theory"). Malaterre & Lareau 2024 ("Very High – Foundational") turns text into edges. Fink et al. 2023 (`fink-2023-congressional-social-network-dataset`) gives a Congressional Twitter network dataset that quantifies what it calls the pairwise "probability of influence". That quantity is built from Twitter interactions and is not a validated influence estimate. Kim & Kunisky 2020 gives bipartite lobbying communities and "champions". Aref & Neal 2021 gives signed coalitions and a hidden, highly effective third bloc. Porter et al. 2005 gives committee structure. Gaynor 2022 gives LPAC donations as directed edges with DW-NOMINATE node attributes and Cook PVI vulnerability. Ardekani 2026 gives NER/IE and Louvain communities.

Path-finding enters through two A* variants. Oropeza-Martínez 2024 ("Priority – Structure – Basis") adjusts routes for OCEAN traits. Rongkuan 2024 ("Priority – Structure – Augmentation") learns the A* heuristic under unstable information. The addendum then turns routing into a staged decision architecture:

1. one-shot greedy (Kempe 2003; Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher 1978), static and submodular, with the 63% bound;
2. adaptive greedy (Golovin & Krause 2011 + CELF, Leskovec 2007) as the within-campaign "operational layer", mapped to D* Lite / LPA* replanning;
3. an RL envelope (Sutton & Barto; Ng et al. 1999 shaping; Watkins & Dayan; Khalil 2017 → GCOMB 2020 → Kamarthi 2020 → Chen 2021 → Chen 2023) for cross-campaign compounding, mapped to MCTS and orienteering.

The theoretical claim Paul cares about most is that congressional coalition formation is "plausibly" a complex contagion (Chen et al. 2023). If so, Melange's core problem is non-submodular, and that is "where the durable competitive moat lives". RL4CCIM shows non-submodularity within a single campaign only, not the cross-campaign claim.

### Chain C: text gives traits and stance, which give receptivity

The ML & Sentiment folder treats public text as the place to measure actors. Ramey et al. (Big Five from floor speech) is the older foundation. Birkenmaier & Lechner 2025 (agency/communion, with LLMs beating SVMs) is the modern method. Peng et al. 2022 surveys emotion models (Paul admits he needs a second read). Kim J. et al. 2025 gives LLM+GNN bill positions for interest groups. Oropeza supplies the bridge: traits should alter path costs. Paul's insight is that traits belong on **edges**, not nodes.

The Adjacent folder is unannotated but highlighted, and it supplies the behavioral priors the receptivity layer later used. Linde & Vis 2016 shows that MPs display the reflection effect. In the paid lottery, 39.13% chose the risky option under a gain frame and 76.09% under a loss frame (n=46 Dutch MPs, 2014–2015). In the hypothetical scenarios, reflection appears in only two of four. The paper found **no** loss aversion in the MPs or in the student comparison sample. Fouirnaies & Hall 2022 shows that lame ducks shirk on observable effort in US state legislatures with binding term limits: the productivity index falls 0.24 SD in the final term. Their ideological positions do not shift, and the paper does not test seat competitiveness.

### Context layer: salience, cues and persuasion

Several highlighted papers that Paul did not annotate describe the environment the chains run in. Noble & Turner 2024 cover going public (rally, retreat, backfire). Merkley & Stecula and Bakker et al. cover out-party cue backlash. In Bakker's 2016–2018 online panels (study 1, N=747), out-party cues lower mass-public policy support by about .06–.07 on a 0–1 scale. Box-Steffensmeier et al. 2018 show that one well-connected group's endorsement predicts cosponsors. Broockman & Kalla 2026 show moderation pays only issue by issue. Kalla & Broockman 2020 show durable persuasion through perspective-getting. The highlights cluster on the door-canvass protocol, which is directly relevant to Terrier.

**In one sentence:** money goes with later lobbying of the recipient's bills, in an observational link whose time course is unsettled (the 2020 draft has it fading within two to three years, while the later version has it growing through t+3); information filtered through ideology turns access into agenda; and agenda spreads through a relationship graph whose edges are shaped by personality and party, so the optimal play is a budgeted, replanned seeding policy on that graph.

## 4. Recurring critiques and methodological commitments

Paul applies the same few tests to every paper.

1. **The conversion gap.** His most repeated critique is that a method's output cannot yet become a node or edge. It appears in Birkenmaier (trait to node), Peng (emotion to vector), Ramey (pipeline for other venues), Kim & Kunisky (bipartite to path-findable graph), Porter (members and committees on one graph), Gaynor ("How does this translate into an edge?"), and Oropeza (traits as edge parameters).
2. **Temporal weighting.** Fink, Malaterre (office closeness), Aref & Neal (no tracking against national mood), and Kim/Stuckatz/Wolters (decay) all prompt him to ask whether relationships drift.
3. **Sign and negativity.** He asks whether a reply or mention counts as negative, and how to score agreement when actors share hostility toward a third member (Fink). Kim J. 2025 does not show conflict between industries, and his margin note there says "I may need to take into account the presence of conflicting industries".
4. **Reactive versus predictive.** LDA filings lag behind current positions (Kim J. 2025), and Paul wants political intelligence that looks ahead.
5. **Disaggregation.** He wants donations separated from events and meetings, and representatives treated as individuals (Huneeus & Kim).
6. **Pipeline-first appraisal.** Paul most often praises a "pre-built pipeline", "pre-built codebooks" or a large database (LobbyView, Fink, Kim J. 2025). He grades papers on whether he can reuse them, and labels them with his Priority / Structure / Basis-or-Augmentation tags.
7. **Compute pragmatism.** He looks for computational efficiency in Aref & Neal (30,000 edges, as the authors report), Malaterre (slimmed texts), Fink (Viral Centrality instead of Monte Carlo), Rongkuan (limited compute), and GCOMB (150× faster than IMM on the authors' benchmarks).

The systems as built add commitments the bibliography does not yet show. These include single-look preregistered holdouts, gold sets, zero-rank-delta gates, circularity bans, exclusion only on contradiction, and trend before significance. The annotations mostly accept reported effects at face value: the 22,000% return, and the "6 months" decay, which neither version of the Kim paper contains. The engineering record is far more skeptical, so the bibliography sits below the evidence standard Paul now enforces.

## 5. How the bibliography categories connect

- **Lobbying** and the **Trey report** supply Chain A (returns, access, information). Trey is the client-facing distillation, and its Kalla & Broockman 2015 citation actually carries Furnas text.
- **SNA** supplies the graph substrate.
- **Path-Finding** supplies routing over that graph.
- The **Adaptive Influence Maximization addendum** sits above both. It recasts routing as sequential seeding under a budget, and it is the only document that explicitly states Melange's architecture.
- **ML & Sentiment** supplies node and edge attributes (traits, positions, stance), and feeds both the SNA graph and the receptivity layer.
- **Causal & Methodological** (Imai & Kim; Keele; Blackwell & Yamauchi; Fouirnaies & Hall; Poole & Rosenthal) is unannotated. Its methods were later used in the sector-growth study (2FE caution, cluster inference) and the receptivity layer (Fouirnaies & Hall). Its only note is a to-do: "Look at Washington University at St. Louis and UCSD".
- **Campaign Politics** (Bonica & Grumbach; Bouton; Kim et al. 2026; Nickerson & Rogers; Ceron) is unannotated. It bears on Mentat (donor structure) and Terrier (voter-file targeting).
- **Adjacent** collects behavioral and contextual priors: Linde & Vis, Noble & Turner, Broockman & Kalla, Kalla & Broockman, and Weimer et al. (Monte Carlo as a negative control). The system later wired several of these directly into the engine, in some cases (LOSSFRAME, cpf_pressure, the competitiveness term) citing them for constants they do not support.
- **Optimization** and **Neural Networks** are placeholders with bare links only. The Neural Networks link to *Information Processing & Management* 2026 may be Zhou & Elejalde, the stance-dynamics source.
- **Curated Annotations** is Paul's shortlist of nine works that span Chains A–C. These are the papers he treats as load-bearing.

## 6. Where the notes are stale relative to the systems

1. **"Identify champions" from Kim & Kunisky's lobbying network.** The systems found that the lobbied-member link is circular by construction (P(joined | lobbied) = 1), because LD-2 filings never name members. Champion identification moved to contact inference (an abstention-first, preregistered design) and to action-based honors. Paul's notes do not reflect this refutation.
2. **A* with a learned heuristic (Rongkuan) and D* Lite replanning (addendum).** The built pathfinders use Dijkstra on −log p, Yen alternatives and Pareto greedy, with heuristic 0 labelled A*. No incremental replanner, MCTS, orienteering formulation or RL policy exists. The operational and strategic layers in the addendum are aspirational, while its Tier-1/Tier-2 content (CELF, the Golovin–Krause bound, contingent seeding) is built.
3. **The complex-contagion hypothesis.** Still untested. The engine runs competitive independent cascade plus linear-threshold priming. No cosponsorship data has been used to test whether joining probability jumps once enough neighbors have joined.
4. **Traits on edges (Oropeza).** This is partly realized. The receptivity layer applies `edge_mult` [0.80, 1.20] and `susc_mult` on the receiver side. But the Big Five validity record is mostly MISS (no N susceptibility signal, H5 null), so the premise that measured personality predicts response is weaker than the notes assume.
5. **Peng's emotion models and "third-generation AI" for stance.** These were superseded by DEBATE NLI, Lexicoder LSD and a two-hop stance×valence registry. The registry answers Fink's third-party sentiment question with an owner-curated valence table.
6. **Think-tank allocation (Furnas).** It never entered any allocator. The system instead took Furnas as the basis for a source-alignment constant (cpf_pressure), but encoded a gain larger than the penalty, the reverse of Furnas's party-split estimate. It also capped money at access (WM 0.35 vs WP 0.65).
7. **The Huneeus & Kim "state layer" for targeting.** It became Demographic Potential v3.0 (CU footprint). That score does not feed the Demo% pillar, and no fundraising-target model was built.
8. **Fink's temporal-weighting gap.** It is still open. The media blend applies half-lives of 2 years for press and 0.5 years for social, but those values have no empirical basis. Sotoudeh's 6-month figure is one of three half-lives (6, 12, 24 months) applied to cosponsorship capital and House passage (2009–2019), with discrimination flat from 6 to 24 months. It is not a measured decay of relationships, and it does not ground the blend. Viral Centrality was not adopted; Monte Carlo with common random numbers was used instead.
9. **Aref & Neal's lack of path-finding.** The system now builds signed coalitions and a multi-target coalition mode on top of them.
10. **Bacik's "news/press/social sentiment as determinants of pathway change".** This is half-built. The stance stack exists, but it was never joined to LobbyView dynamics. The FEC→LDA bridge was a member × industry × cycle rank test over 2023–2026, covering 22 financial-sector clients. It closed UNCORROBORATED: raw ρ +0.153, four-control partial −0.0002. Its estimand differs from Kim's within-pair design, so it neither refutes nor confirms Kim.
11. **Data presumptions.** The bibliography treats LobbyView and Kim's database as the data foundation. The systems found coverage reliable only through about 2021, and they rebuilt LDA directly.
12. **Behavioral priors cited for constants they do not support.** Linde & Vis is cited for LOSSFRAME 1.10, but it reports no loss aversion. Fouirnaies & Hall is cited for a competitiveness term and for "responsiveness", but it estimates effort, finds no position shift and does not test competitiveness. Both constants stand as owner judgment.

## 7. Defects in the notes themselves

- The Trey report's Kalla & Broockman 2015 entry carries the Furnas et al. annotation.
- The Kim (2026) note says donation favor lasts "**6 months**". Neither version of the paper contains a six-month figure. The 2020 working-paper text Paul highlighted says the effect disappears "two or three years after", although its Figure 5 still shows about 3.2 pp at t+3 (Senate). The later 2023 version shows the link growing through t+3 (Senate about 8 to 11 pp, House 8.6 to 12.7 pp). Six months is the shortest of Sotoudeh's three tested half-lives for cosponsorship capital, so Paul may have conflated the two papers.
- The Kim, Jiseon Lobbying entry is truncated mid-sentence.
- Fink's annotation announces "two main weaknesses" but lists three.
- The addendum says the "four anchored entries" are sufficient, but it anchors seven.
- Raychaudhuri 2008 is cited, but the PDF on disk is Harrison 2010.
- The Ramey year conflicts across sources: 2015 SSRN in the bibliography, 2016 and 2019 in the repo.
- Kunisky is spelled "Kuinsky" and Louvain is spelled "Louvian" in Paul's prose.
- Paul's citation of Furnas et al. 2022 ("More than mere access") corresponds on disk to the 2019 APSA version of the experiment.

## Open questions

1. Is congressional coalition formation a complex contagion? Does a member's probability of cosponsoring jump once K close allies have joined? This is Paul's stated moat hypothesis (Chen et al. 2023), and it has never been tested on cosponsorship timing data.
2. Do high-ranking members exert non-diminishing pressure, as Paul's margin note on RL4CCIM puts it? If so, which form should replace submodular marginal gains?
3. How should personality traits enter path costs as edge parameters rather than node attributes (Oropeza)? Given the weak Big Five validity record, is any trait signal strong enough to justify it?
4. Can a learned or adaptive heuristic (Rongkuan's ML-augmented A*; D* Lite/LPA* replanning) beat the current Dijkstra and Pareto routers on real introduction or recruitment outcomes? No outreach log exists yet to score routes.
5. How should a bipartite interest-group-to-legislator model become a path-findable one-mode graph without recreating the circular lobbied-member link?
6. How should negative or hostile interactions be signed, and how should agreement be scored when two actors share hostility toward a third (Fink)? Does the two-hop valence registry generalize beyond credit-union targets?
7. What temporal decay fits relationship edges? No source in the vault measures it. Sotoudeh's 6-month figure is one of three half-lives (6, 12, 24 months) applied to cosponsorship capital (House, 2009–2019), with discrimination flat across them. The "two or three years" fade comes from the 2020 Kim, Stuckatz & Wolters draft. The later 2023 version (cited by Paul as JOP 2026) shows the donation–lobbying association growing through t+3 (manufacturing firms, 2008–2018). Paul's '6 months' reading of Kim et al. 2026 is not found in either version.
8. Can position data be made predictive rather than reactive, given the lag in LDA filings, and can it show conflict between industries over a specific bill (Kim J. 2025)? Should the contested-bills registry be wired into the engine to answer this?
9. Should allocation models include funding ideologically aligned think tanks (Furnas et al. 2022)? What evidence would show that this cultivates champions better than direct contributions?
10. Can campaign donations be separated from events, meetings and other access activities in return estimates, and can representatives be differentiated (Huneeus & Kim gaps)?
11. Do news cycles, social feeds and press sentiment predict changes in lobbying pathways in LobbyView data (Bacik augmentation)?
12. Does the third, ideologically fluid and highly effective coalition (Aref & Neal) persist over time and respond to national mood? Do its members map onto the system's persuadable tiers?
13. Can members and committees be represented on one graph, and does partisanship drive committee selection or the reverse (Porter et al.)?
14. How does an LPAC donation, or a shared caucus membership, translate into an edge weight (Paul's Gaynor margin note)? Should leadership insulation enter as a covariate?
15. Is US legislation available in machine-readable form, so that Ardekani-style NER/IE plus Louvain can be applied to a focal CU statute ('Same for the US?')?
16. Which annotation was intended for the Trey report's Kalla & Broockman 2015 entry, which currently carries Furnas et al. text? Should the Kalla & Broockman access experiment get its own annotation?
17. What did Paul intend by the unexplained to-do 'Look at Washington University at St. Louis and UCSD' (Causal & Methodological), and what are the four bare Neural Networks links and the Optimization link (FAIA 397 p.583)?
18. Should the five citation-only folders (Adjacent, Campaign Politics, Causal & Methodological, Neural Networks, Optimization) be annotated, given that the engine now relies directly on several of their works (Linde & Vis, Noble & Turner, Fouirnaies & Hall)? Should the code's citations of Linde & Vis (LOSSFRAME 1.10), Furnas/Bakker (cpf_pressure 1.7/0.5) and Fouirnaies & Hall (competitiveness term) be re-labelled as owner judgment? That is Paul's call.
19. Inventory: the Fink et al. 2023 PDF (sha12 445139e681c1) now has the paper slug `fink-2023-congressional-social-network-dataset`, but no system hub carries an evidence row for it yet. The Ringe, Victor & Gross 2013 BJPS 'Keeping Your Friends Close and Your Enemies Closer?' PDF is paper `ringe-2013-pinpointing-powerful-voting` (VERIFIED). It is being renamed `ringe-2013-pinpointing-powerful-voting`, but that slug reuses the title words of the separate Ringe & Wilson 'Pinpointing the Powerful' appendix (`ringe-nd-pinpointing-powerful-voting`), so check it before applying. Several heavily highlighted PDFs (Sotoudeh, Box-Steffensmeier, Merkley & Stecula, Bakker, Jacoby, Azaiez & Mandel) never entered any bibliography.
