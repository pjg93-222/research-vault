# Influence maximization in social networks: a survey of behaviour-aware methods

**Ahmad Zareie, Rizos Sakellariou (2023).** *Social Network Analysis and Mining, 13:78 (published online 25 April 2023; received 23 May 2022, revised 12 March 2023, accepted 27 March 2023).* [10.1007/s13278-023-01078-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13278-023-01078-9). Theme: [[themes/adaptive-influence-maximization|Adaptive Influence Maximization]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Zareie and Sakellariou (2023, SNAM 13:78) is a narrative survey with no data. It proposes a two-level taxonomy of influence maximization (IM). Level 1 separates behaviour-agnostic methods, which use structure only, from behaviour-aware methods. Level 2 splits behaviour-aware methods into four families: interest-aware (target, label, topic), opinion-aware (trust-agnostic vs signed friend/foe), money-aware (cost-aware budgeted IM vs profit-aware) and physical-world-aware. Tables 1-4 code each surveyed method by which features it uses: message (AM), user (BV) or relationship (BE). They also code how it detects influence (centrality, simulation, RIS or MIA) and whether spreading is simple or complex. The useful content is qualitative. Topic-aware edge probabilities are judged more realistic than binary target or label methods, but they need rich history. Trust-agnostic models treat every tie as positive, whereas reality may also include negative relationships. Profit-aware IM needs per-user valuations that may not always be readily available, so cost-aware IM may be more practical. The authors also warn that changing the diffusion model can change accuracy a lot, and that many users are silent.
>
> For Melange the paper is mainly a lens on design choices that were already made. The code cites it directly: receptivity_layer.py:29-31 and outcome_contract.py:17 ('Chen/SNAM') use it to back contingency-aware seeding. But the survey gives no estimate of seed take-up and no magnitudes, so it supports only the general idea that member features belong in IM. Against the built engine, the survey exposes three choices. First, one topic-independent cascade kernel serves all 18 CU categories. Second, the kernel is trust-agnostic: partisan factors change edge magnitude, never sign, and the signed reference layer is display-only. Third, the optimizer has a profit-aware shape, weighting members by owner-set values (PERS, IMP), which is the kind of valuation data the survey says may not always be readily available. All of these are design-level bearings. Nothing in the paper validates or refutes any constant in the system.

## Study

**Question:** No empirical question is posed. The paper asks what behaviour-aware methods for influence maximization (IM) exist in social networks, and proposes to classify and review them as a separate family from behaviour-agnostic, purely structural methods (Sec. 1, pp. 1-3). It also states that previous surveys covered behaviour-agnostic approaches or touched behaviour-aware ones only briefly (p. 2).

**Theory:** No formal social or economic theory is tested. The framing is IM as discrete optimisation (Eq. 1, p. 4): select a seed set S of size k or under budget B that maximises spread phi(S), with query Q = (M, AM, B) and graph G = (V, E, BV, BE). Behaviour-aware methods use user features BV, relationship features BE and message features AM. Diffusion models are Independent Cascade and Linear Threshold (Kempe et al. 2003; Granovetter 1978), with voter and opinion-formation models used for opinion dynamics.

**Data:** None. This is a narrative review with no sample, unit of analysis, N or data period. Cited works span roughly 1977 to 2023, but the paper states no explicit inclusion window or search protocol.

**Method:** Narrative classification and review. The authors propose a two-level taxonomy (pp. 2-5, Fig. 2). Level 1 splits IM methods into behaviour-agnostic and behaviour-aware. Level 2 splits behaviour-aware methods into four families: interest-aware (target-aware, label-aware, topic-aware; Sec. 3.1), opinion-aware (trust-agnostic, trust-aware; Sec. 3.2), money-aware (cost-aware, profit-aware; Sec. 3.3) and physical-world-aware (physical relationship-aware, location-aware; Sec. 3.4). Summary tables code each surveyed paper by behavioural features (AM, BV, BE), influence-detection method (centrality measure CM, spreading simulation SS, reverse influence sampling RIS, maximum influence arborescence MIA) and spreading process type (simple or complex). Section 2.2 ranks influence-detection families by time complexity: simulation-based, then MIA, then RIS, then centrality (p. 4).

**Identification:** Not applicable. The paper makes no causal or statistical identification claim. It classifies published methods and does not estimate effects.

**Scope:** Population: social-network IM research, covering online graphs (friendship, follow, trust) and, for physical-world-aware methods, check-in and location-based social networks (Sec. 3.4). No institution or era restriction beyond the cited literature. Behaviour-agnostic methods are explicitly excluded as already surveyed elsewhere (pp. 3, 5).

## Key findings

- Behaviour-agnostic methods dominate current IM work because they have a longer history, but behaviour-aware formulations are argued to be more realistic. No previous survey had treated behaviour-aware methods as a class of their own. (None; qualitative claim; Sec. 1, pp. 2-3)
- Interest-aware methods split into target-aware (binary interest), label-aware (label sets) and topic-aware (continuous topic vectors). Topic-aware models are judged to capture preferences more realistically than binary target or label methods but need good historical records. Partly offline topic-aware methods trade offline storage and precomputation for faster online queries. (Table 1 codes the interest-aware papers; no numeric results; Sec. 3.1.3-3.1.4, pp. 6-8; Table 1 on p. 8)
- Opinion-aware methods split into trust-agnostic (all ties positive) and trust-aware (signed friend or foe ties). Trust-agnostic models assume all relationships are positive, whereas reality may also include negative relationships (Sec. 3.2.3, p. 11); opinion dynamics under friend and foe influence need more work. (None; Table 2 codes the opinion-aware papers; Sec. 3.2.1-3.2.3, pp. 9-11; Table 2 on p. 12)
- Cost-aware (budgeted) IM assumes every activated user yields the same benefit. Profit-aware methods model per-user benefit and cost, but data on users' valuations may not always be readily available, so the authors suggest cost-aware methods may be more practical. (Nguyen and Zheng (2013) introduced budgeted IM; Table 3 codes the money-aware papers; Sec. 3.3.1-3.3.3, pp. 11-14; Table 3 on p. 14)
- Physical-world-aware methods add offline ties (co-location, check-in) to the online graph, or use location and attendance probability as the target. Binary physical-tie models and fixed distance thresholds miss the dynamics of user location, and tracking locations over time adds processing and storage cost. (None; qualitative comparison; Sec. 3.4.1-3.4.3, pp. 14-16; Table 4 on p. 16)
- Five open challenge areas are identified: data availability, data processing, data modelling (numeric encodings of attitude and friend or foe ties), data dynamicity, and user reaction modelling. Research directions cover network size and time complexity, network diversity (temporal, multilayer, dynamic), data preparation, and adaptive diffusion models that feed behaviour changes back into spread. (None; Sec. 4, pp. 17-18)

## Limitations

- The paper states no methodological limitations of its own review: no search protocol, inclusion rule, or count of surveyed papers.
- Stated scope limit: it reviews behaviour-aware methods only and defers behaviour-agnostic methods to earlier surveys (pp. 3, 5).
- Stated challenge: behaviour data may not exist, may be incomplete or noisy, or may be governed by privacy rules (Sec. 4, p. 17).
- Stated challenge: many behaviour-aware models encode attitudes and friend or foe ties as numbers, which may not capture personality or attitude (Sec. 4, p. 17).
- Stated challenge: most methods use conventional IC or LT diffusion models that may not emulate real spreading; changing the model can significantly change accuracy (Sec. 4, p. 18).
- The paper classifies methods and does not benchmark or compare their accuracy.

## Short quotes

- "behaviour-aware methods have not been considered in any previous survey as a class on their own" (Sec. 1, p. 2)

## Gaps and flags

- No topic-conditioned edge probabilities: one cosponsor kernel serves all 18 CU categories (per_topic_coalition.py:37), whereas topic-aware IM treats topic-dependent influence as the realistic case.
- The cascade has no signed (foe) edges. The LLM-valence reference layer and the signed-coalition partition exist but stay outside the kernel, and neither has a validation that it predicts reduced cosponsorship.
- Per-member benefit valuations in the allocator (PERS, IMP, delivery_mult) are owner tables. The survey names unavailable valuations as the main weakness of profit-aware IM, and no uniform-benefit sensitivity run is recorded.
- No adaptive-diffusion feedback: the survey's research direction feeds behaviour changes back into spread, while stance dynamics stay a separate proposals-only module whose co-movement test was null.
- No empirical check that IC or LT diffusion fits observed cosponsor join sequences, which the survey flags as a source of large accuracy changes.

- Citation overreach in code: receptivity_layer.py:29-31, budget_frontier.py:73 and outcome_contract.py:17 ('Chen/SNAM') cite this survey as grounds for contingency-aware seed activation. The survey is a taxonomy with no take-up estimates, and its four families do not include contingency-aware seeding as such. It supports only including member features, not the seed_prob magnitudes. Suggested wording: 'framing per Zareie & Sakellariou 2023; magnitudes are design priors, UNVERIFIED pending H1'.
- Name collision: Paul's position for melange-sna.co-appearance-and-references cites 'Zareie & Sakellariou 2020' (similarity-based link prediction), which is a different paper from this 2023 IM survey. The two should not be conflated in notes or citations.
- Any move to trust-aware (signed) cascade edges based on this survey would be new agent analysis. The survey itself warns that numeric friend/foe encodings may not capture attitudes.
- The survey's 'influence' and 'spread' are model outputs of IC or LT on online graphs. Using this vocabulary for member ranks or route scores must follow D-025: heuristic outputs, never access or introduction probabilities.
- The paper does not argue for either rejected SNA proposal (dropping regular cosponsorship edges; per-edge volume normalization), so there is nothing to flag on that front.

Paul has no annotations on this paper, and it is not cited in his component positions; his co-appearance note cites the 2020 Zareie link-prediction paper instead. The survey still fits his stated direction. It supports behaviour-aware seeding, which is Paul's mapping of Chen's contingency-aware IM to legislators who may not move when approached. It also matches his call to model rival industries and sign, and his emphasis on directionality. It qualifies his receptivity ambitions without contradicting them. The survey's own challenges (silent users, crude numeric attitude encodings, model sensitivity) echo the system's poor validity record for trait-based receptivity, which his notes underweight. His GCOMB and learned-policy expectations sit in the behaviour-agnostic tier, which this survey deliberately excludes. The system cites this paper more strongly than its content supports: in code it stands as a basis for seed_prob, which is a design prior.
