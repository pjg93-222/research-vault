# The Effect of Politicians' Personality on Their Media Visibility

**Eran Amsalem, Alon Zoizner, Tamir Sheafer, Stefaan Walgrave, Peter John Loewen (2018).** *Communication Research (Original Research Article).* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650218758084). Theme: [[themes/adjacent|Adjacent]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Amsalem, Zoizner, Sheafer, Walgrave and Loewen (2018) linked a 2015 elite survey of 339 legislators in Belgium, Canada (federal and Ontario) and Israel to counts of how often each was named in national newspapers, news websites and TV. Personality was measured with the 10-item TIPI. Window: media counted over country-specific periods between 2011 and 2015. Denominator: n = 306 in the pooled model.
> 
> In a pooled cross-sectional OLS with country dummies, four traits predict visibility, each with a standardized beta of about 0.1:
> - extraversion: positive (beta .117)
> - agreeableness: negative (-.116)
> - neuroticism: negative (-.111)
> - openness: positive (.098)
> 
> Conscientiousness is null in the pooled model and splits by country. Office matters more: being a minister has beta .369, about three times the trait betas, while being a party leader (.195) is only slightly stronger than the personality effects; R² = .483. The measures are weak. Agreeableness has alpha .15 and its two items correlate at only r = .09 (not significant). Response rates are 26-57%. The paper does not identify a causal effect, so its "effect" is a covariate-adjusted association.
> 
> For Melange the paper matters in three places:
> 1. The behavioural visibility disposition is counted from interviews and labelled extraversion-sociability. The paper's extraversion result supports that label, but the paper also shows visibility loads on low agreeableness, emotional stability and, mostly, office.
> 2. That disposition raises seed_prob in the receptivity layer, whose default basis is behavioral. On that path susc_trait is built from cooperation and affective N, and visible MPs in the paper have lower agreeableness and lower neuroticism, so the tension is real but indirect.
> 3. Interview-derived traits, including affective N, exist only for members who get airtime. Everyone else is set to neutral. If airtime selects on temperament, as the paper's earned-coverage result suggests, that missingness may not be random; this is untested.
> 
> None of this has been tested on US Congress data.

## Study

**Question:** Do politicians' Big Five personality traits predict how often they are covered in national news media, beyond institutional power and other individual-level explanations (political power, activity, communication skill, demographics)? The paper focuses on agreeableness and extraversion as interpersonal traits, and also tests openness, neuroticism and conscientiousness (pp. 2-3, lines 74-78 and 146-156).

**Theory:** Two mechanisms link traits to visibility (pp. 5-6). (1) A motivational mechanism: traits shape need for interaction and attention, so some politicians seek coverage more (cites Costa & McCrae 1988; Mondak 2010; Cohen et al. 2008; Sheafer 2001). (2) A news-values mechanism: traits shape behaviours that fit journalistic selection criteria (camera performance, sound bites, surprising initiatives) and the ability to cultivate journalist relationships. The authors argue newsworthiness matters more than journalist relationships when the two conflict (p. 7; Helfer & Van Aelst 2016). Agreeableness and extraversion are the most interpersonal traits and voters rely on them (pp. 5-6; Caprara et al. 2002). Hypotheses: H1 agreeableness negative; H2 extraversion positive; H3 openness positive; H4 neuroticism negative; H5 conscientiousness positive (pp. 8-10).

**Data:** Unit: incumbent member of parliament (MP). Survey sample N = 339 MPs in three countries, fielded March to August 2015 (p. 10). Belgium: 95 federal MPs (54th federal parliament) and 139 regional MPs, 57% response rate. Canada: 45 federal MPs (41st parliament) and 28 Ontario provincial MPs, 26% response rate (278 contacted). Israel: 32 MPs (20th Knesset), 27% response rate (p. 10 and note 1, p. 20). Media counting windows (note 3, p. 21): Canada federal June 2, 2011 to Aug 2, 2015; Ontario July 2, 2014 to Dec 31, 2015; Belgium June 19, 2014 to Nov 30, 2015; Israel Mar 31 to Dec 31, 2015. Media outlets (p. 10): Belgium 6 newspapers, 2 news websites, 2 TV channels; Canada 3 newspapers, 2 websites, 1 TV channel (CTV); Israel 2 newspapers, 2 websites, 2 TV channels. Regression N: Model 1 n = 306; Model 2 (newspapers) n = 296; Model 3 (websites) n = 286; Model 4 (TV) n = 174 (Table 1, p. 14). Note 11 says log-transforming visibility drops MPs with zero mentions, but the paper does not report the per-model count dropped, so the drop from 339 to 306 is not fully explained.

**Method:** Dependent variable: count of mentions of each MP's full name in each country's major outlets, rescaled per country and medium to 0-1 (min-max, note 4), summed and rescaled again to 0-1 (M = .08, SD = .14, skewness 3.77). Log-transformed for OLS (p. 13); note 11 gives an alternative that adds half the smallest nonzero value. Personality: 10-item TIPI (Gosling et al. 2003), 7-point scale, two items per trait, reverse-coded where needed, averaged per trait (pp. 11-12). Main model: multivariate OLS with standardized betas on all five traits plus controls. Robustness: separate OLS models by medium (Models 2-4); a parsimonious demographic-only model and per-country parsimonious models (Online Appendix E); a randomization inference test (Malesky et al. 2014) for joint significance (pp. 16-17). Controls (pp. 12-13): gender, age, parliamentary experience, coalition membership, minister, party leader, house speaker, conservatism (binary), federal vs regional MP, parliamentary activity (standardized counts of bills, questions, etc.), Facebook posts (standardized), country dummies. Note 10: VIF below 1.63.

**Identification:** None beyond covariate adjustment. The design is a single-wave cross-sectional survey linked to media counts, analysed with OLS controlling for institutional power, activity, communication effort, demographics, ideology and country. The paper does not use an instrument, panel, experiment or natural variation. The title's "effect" is the authors' framing of conditional associations. Robustness is by medium, by country, by a parsimonious specification, and by randomization inference.

**Scope:** Population: incumbent MPs and provincial/regional members in Belgium, Canada (federal and Ontario) and Israel, sampled during the 2011-2015 legislatures. Institutions: three developed parliamentary democracies with different electoral systems (Canada single-member plurality; Belgium multimember with 11 districts; Israel one national proportional district) (pp. 9-10). Era: media coverage counted over roughly 2011-2015, depending on country. The authors say findings may extend to other developed democracies, but they do not claim generalization to presidential or nondemocratic systems (p. 19).

## Key findings

- H1 supported: agreeableness is negatively associated with national media visibility (less agreeable MPs covered more). (b = -0.184 (SE 0.075), standardized beta = -.116, p < .05; Results p. 13; Table 1 Model 1 p. 14; Discussion p. 17)
- H2 supported: extraversion is positively associated with national media visibility. (b = 0.129 (SE 0.052), beta = .117, p < .05; Results p. 13; Table 1 Model 1 p. 14)
- H3 supported: openness to experience is positively associated with visibility. (b = 0.153 (SE 0.070), beta = .098, p < .05; Results p. 15; Table 1 Model 1 p. 14)
- H4 supported: neuroticism (emotional instability) is negatively associated with visibility; more emotionally stable MPs are covered more. (b = -0.157 (SE 0.068), beta = -.111, p < .05; Results p. 15; Table 1 Model 1 p. 14)
- H5 not supported in the pooled model: conscientiousness has a near-zero, insignificant association with visibility. (b = -0.008 (SE 0.066), beta = -.006, p = .91; Results p. 15; Table 1 Model 1 p. 14)
- Institutional power dominates. Being a minister has the largest standardized effect, about three times the trait betas. A party leader's effect is only slightly larger than the personality effects. (Minister beta = .369 (b = 3.122, p < .001); party leader beta = .195 (b = 1.836, p < .001); house speaker not significant; overall R2 = .483; Results pp. 16-17 (lines 819-829))
- Medium-specific robustness. Newspapers: agreeableness b = -0.243 (p < .01), extraversion and neuroticism significant at p < .1, openness and conscientiousness not significant. Websites: extraversion (b = 0.117, p < .05), openness (b = 0.230, p < .01) and neuroticism (b = -0.191, p < .01) significant; agreeableness negative but not significant (-0.102); conscientiousness not significant. TV (n = 174): agreeableness (-0.188) and extraversion (0.128) at p < .1; other traits not significant. (Model 2 R2 = .450 (n = 296); Model 3 R2 = .478 (n = 286); Model 4 R2 = .546 (n = 174); Robustness Checks pp. 15-16; Table 1 Models 2-4 p. 14)
- Conscientiousness is positive and significant in Canada and Israel but not in Belgium. The authors attribute the Belgian null partly to TIPI measurement error (attenuation). Among Belgian regional MPs (n = 137) conscientiousness significantly decreases visibility; the federal Belgian effect (n = 95) is not significant. (Per-country samples: Israel n = 32, Belgium n = 234 (95 federal + 139 regional), Canada n = 73 (45 federal + 28 Ontario); the country-level coefficients are not reported in the main text; Robustness pp. 17-18; Discussion pp. 18 (lines 917-935); per-country and Belgian-parliament detail in Online Appendix E)
- Authors read the results as personality having a substantial effect beyond institutional explanations, with traits that fit news values (extraversion) giving a long-lasting advantage. (Four of five traits significant in the pooled model; one trait (conscientiousness) partly predicts (abstract p. 1; discussion p. 18); Discussion pp. 18-19 (lines 936-962))

## Limitations

- Social desirability: self-reported personality from office holders may be biased, e.g. understated irritability and overstated dependability (p. 19).
- Brief TIPI measure with low reliability, especially agreeableness (alpha .15; r = .09, ns). The authors say this likely underestimates true effects (pp. 11-12, 19; note 7 p. 21). Facet-level effects cannot be separated (p. 19).
- Only three countries, so generalization to presidential or nondemocratic systems is not established (p. 19-20).
- Small national subsamples (Israel n = 32) limit the country-level conclusions, particularly for conscientiousness (pp. 17-18).
- Only visibility (volume) is measured, not tone of coverage (p. 20).
- The two proposed mechanisms (motivation and newsworthiness) are not measured directly and cannot be disentangled (p. 20).
- Low response rates (Canada 26%, Israel 27%; Belgium 57%) and a drop from N = 339 to n = 306 in Model 1 that note 11 only partly explains (pp. 10, 13-14).
- Cross-sectional design with media counts over different windows across countries, so no causal or temporal ordering is established beyond the covariate-adjusted association (note 4, p. 21).

## Gaps and flags

- No earned-media volume measure: the system counts own-domain statements and interviews, and drops third-party coverage, so how often the press actually names a member (the paper's dependent variable) is absent.
- visibility_z is not adjusted for office (chair, ranking member, party leadership), which the paper shows is the largest driver of coverage. Whether visibility_z and dominance_z share leadership variance as a result is an untested inference.
- No audit of whether interview-corpus coverage (the source of affective_n and lexical traits) is selected on temperament or office. The missing-to-neutral rule assumes absence is uninformative.
- No tone-of-coverage measure for third-party media. The paper flags tone as its own unmeasured limitation; the system's LSD tone applies only to own-domain releases.
- No check of the sign of corr(visibility_z, coop_v2) in this roster. That check would show whether the +.04 visibility term in seed_prob works against the cooperation term.
- The title's 'effect' is causal framing for a single-wave, cross-sectional, covariate-adjusted OLS. Quote these results as associations, not effects.
- The pooled coefficients run across three countries and two levels of government with only country dummies. Conscientiousness flips by stratum (positive in Canada and Israel, null in Belgium, negative among Belgian regional MPs, n = 137). Quote any pooled trait coefficient with strata or an UNSTRATIFIED label.
- Media visibility, interview count and seed_prob must not be read as access, closeness or introduction probabilities (D-025).
- The seed_prob sign tension and the coverage-selection concern are the analyst's inferences, not paper findings, and have not been checked on roster data (DE-062).
- The paper argues for neither rejected SNA proposal (dropping regular cosponsorship edges, per-edge volume normalization) and makes no claim about LDA.

The paper supports the idea that public presence is shaped by temperament, but finds that minister status drives visibility about three times as strongly as any trait. That argues for controlling for office before reading interview or press volume as a trait. In the current code, visibility_z enters seed_prob on the default behavioral path, and the paper's trait profile for highly visible members (low A, low N) cuts against susc_trait through the cooperation and affective-N terms.
