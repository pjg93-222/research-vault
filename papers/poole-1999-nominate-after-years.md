# D-NOMINATE After 10 Years: A Comparative Update to Congress

**Keith T. Poole (Carnegie Mellon University), Howard Rosenthal (Princeton University) (1999).** *SSRN working paper, 27 September 1999 (SSRN abstract 1154162). Metadata hint verified: title, authors, year and SSRN id match the PDF. No journal version is stated in the text..* SSRN 1154162 Theme: [[themes/causal-methodological|Causal & Methodological]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Poole and Rosenthal update their 1997 roll-call study through the 105th Congress (1789-1998) using DW-NOMINATE, a dynamic probit spatial model of legislator ideal points. Four results matter here. First, legislator positions are close to fixed over a career: the linear time-trend model adds only about 0.3 points of classification over the constant model (House 85.65 to 85.96; Senate 84.04 to 84.40). Second, Congress has become one-dimensional: the second dimension now adds under 1 percent. Third, party overlap (members closer to the opposing party's mean than their own) is near zero: under 1.5% in both chambers over the last five Congresses, and none in the last three Senates. Fourth, polarization rose smoothly, with no break at 1994. A non-parametric rank method produces nearly the same orders (Spearman .960 House, .981 Senate, pooled over 1789-1998 and UNSTRATIFIED). The donor and primary claim that Paul highlighted in pink rests on a cited working paper that this text does not present.
> 
> For the system as built, the paper is a measurement baseline. It is not an engine input. Within fec, DW-NOMINATE appears as a control (fec/members/dw_control.py, also imported by hearing_antagonism_build.py) and through per-Congress Nokken-Poole scores used as a criterion. The sharpest consequence is for the say-do gate's Path V. One CU-aligned final-passage vote counts as corroboration, and in a polarized, one-dimensional chamber that vote mostly records the member's party. A read-only re-run of the same function shows strong party sorting in several categories, though three (Mortgage & Housing, Fraud Prevention, Defense CU Issues) show no party split; it is not an independent second path (details in bearings). The paper also explains why drift-based susceptibility criteria have little true variance to detect. It argues for using an ideal-point control rather than a bare party dummy wherever party-confounded tiers are interpreted, as in the CFPB isolation. The paper does nothing for lobbying, money effects, pathfinding or allocation.

## Study

**Question:** Have the trends identified in Congress (1997) toward polarization and one-dimensionality in roll call voting continued through the 105th Congress, and does the 1994 Republican takeover represent a sharp break? Secondary: do DW-NOMINATE and alternative scaling methods recover essentially the same legislator positions, and how does the U.S. Congress fit the spatial model compared with other legislatures? (Abstract p.2; Sec. 1 p.3.)

**Theory:** Basic spatial theory of ideology (Ordeshook 1976; Hinich and Pollard 1981; Enelow and Hinich 1984; Hinich and Munger 1994): legislators have ideal points in a low-dimensional policy space and choose the outcome closer to them. Dynamic extension: legislator coordinates are polynomial functions of time (Appendix eq. A1, p.19). Roll call utility is a normal-error probit over a Gaussian-type distance with dimension salience weights (eqs. A2-A4, p.20).

**Data:** U.S. House and Senate roll calls, Congresses 1-105 (1789-1998). Unit: legislator-roll call choice (Yea or Nay; abstentions discarded, endnote 9, p.30). Legislators included if they cast at least 25 votes in at least one Congress (endnote 1, p.30). House: 38,740 scalable roll calls, 10,208 scalable representatives, 10,495,639 choices (Table 1, p.23). Senate: 41,165 roll calls, 1,794 senators, 2,697,691 choices (Table 1, p.24). Joint House-Senate scaling: 79,905 roll calls, 11,389 unique legislators, 13,193,330 choices, with 613 members serving in both chambers as "glue" (Table 3, p.27; p.10). Presidents Eisenhower through Clinton included as members of both chambers using CQ presidential support roll calls (p.10). Comparison sets (Table 4, p.28-29): 105th House (946 roll calls, 443 legislators), 105th Senate (486, 101), Third and Fourth European Parliament, 1841 British Parliament, French National Assembly 1951-56, Czech Parliament 1993-97, 1995 Polish Parliament, UN General Assembly sets.

**Method:** DW-NOMINATE (dynamic, weighted, nominal three-step estimation): normal (probit) errors replacing D-NOMINATE's logit errors; per-dimension salience weights w_k; legislator coordinates polynomial in time, estimated as constant (v=0) and linear (v=1) only (p.5-6, Appendix p.19-20). Estimated by maximum likelihood over the joint choice likelihood (eq. A4, p.20). Comparators: Poole's non-parametric optimal classification (rank ordering that maximizes correct classification; one dimension only; Poole 1997, 2000), Heckman-Snyder factor analysis (p.9). Fit measured by percent correctly classified, APRE, and geometric mean probability (Table 1 notes, p.24-25). Party polarization measured by mean within- and between-party pairwise distances (Figs 7-8, p.14) and percent party overlap (Fig 9, p.15). Joint scaling of both chambers estimated with optimal classification (Table 3, p.27).

**Identification:** Not a causal design; this is measurement of ideal points. Ideal points are identified by maximum likelihood across roll calls with the salience weight for dimension 1 set to one and the probit error variance normalized to 1 (p.8). Linear trends are estimated for legislators serving at least 3 (or 5) Congresses; the authors concede the 3-Congress threshold lets random walkers appear linear, which biases toward the polynomial models (p.6). Joint House-Senate scaling assumes the 613 glue members hold fixed positions across chambers (p.10); the authors call this "reasonably supported" from the classification drop of about 1 point (p.11-12).

**Scope:** Population: all roll call votes and members of the U.S. House and Senate, 1789-1998 (Congresses 1-105). Substantive focus: Post-Reconstruction Democrat/Republican party system, 1879-1998, with extra attention to recent Congresses (p.12). Cross-national comparison (Sec. 6, p.16-17): a few legislatures at single time spans. Institution: the U.S. Congress plus selected parliaments. Era: Post-Reconstruction and modern polarization (1879-1998).

## Key findings

- On Congresses 1-99, 2D linear DW-NOMINATE nearly reproduces D-NOMINATE: correct classification 85.4 (House) and 84.6 (Senate) vs 85.2 and 84.5; dimension-1 coordinate correlations .977 House and .974 Senate; dimension-2 .923 House and .901 Senate. (Sec. 3, p.7). 85.4/84.6 vs 85.2/84.5; r = .977/.974 (dim 1), .923/.901 (dim 2)
- Adding Congresses 100-105 raises correct classification by about 0.6 points in the House and 0.3 in the Senate; recent Congresses fit the model better than earlier ones. (Sec. 3, p.7; Table 1, p.23-24). House 2D linear 3-Cong: 85.41 (1-99) to 86.08 (1-105); Senate 84.42 to 84.70
- The second dimension adds under 2 points of classification in the House and about 2.5 in the Senate over one dimension. (Sec. 3, p.7; Table 1, p.23-24). House 1D constant 83.82 vs 2D constant 85.65; Senate 81.46 vs 84.04
- The linear (time-varying) model is not a striking improvement over the constant model; lowering the linear threshold from 5 to 3 Congresses adds about 0.1 (House) and 0.3 (Senate) points. (Sec. 3, p.6-7; Table 1, p.23-24). House 2D constant 85.65 vs linear 85.96 (5-Cong threshold) and 86.08 (3-Cong); Senate 84.04 vs 84.40 and 84.70; about 0.3 points for linear over constant
- Unidimensionality trend: the second dimension now adds under 1 percent of improvement over the first in both chambers; the authors call the post-early-1970s trend dramatic. (Sec. 3, p.7-8 (Figs 1B, 2B are not in the text)). under 1 percent
- Utility-function estimates for DW-NOMINATE: second-dimension salience weight w-squared is .3160 (House, linear) and .3087 (Senate, linear); beta is 4.125 (House, 2D linear) and 4.078 (Senate, 2D linear). Standard errors are acknowledged as not econometrically correct. (Sec. 3, p.8; Table 2, p.26). w^2 .3160 House, .3087 Senate; beta 4.125 House, 4.078 Senate (2D linear)
- Optimal classification (1D, rank order maximizing correct votes) beats 1D DW-NOMINATE constant by about 2 points in the House and about 3 in the Senate, yet the two produce similar rank orders. (Sec. 4, p.10; Table 3, p.27). Spearman .960 (n=10,208) House and .981 (n=1,794) Senate; .972 (n=5,018) and .985 (n=1,183) for members with 500+ votes; joint OC 85.60% correct, APRE .567; House 85.98, Senate 84.51
- Joint House-Senate rank order (613 glue members, presidents included as members of both chambers) reproduces the chamber-separate DW-NOMINATE orders closely; the common members are classified about 1 point worse under joint scaling. (Sec. 4, p.10-11; Table 3, p.27). Spearman .952 (House, n=10,208), .965 (Senate, n=1,794); .960 and .969 for 500+ votes; 85.46% (separate House), 85.10% (separate Senate), 84.54% (joint) for the 613; cross-chamber rank correlation .790 (270 with 500+ votes: .846)
- Party means: cross-time correlation of Republican dimension-1 means is .911 and Democratic .837; cross-chamber correlation of party means .722. Median-rank correlations across chambers: Republicans .888, Democrats .850. (Sec. 5, p.13-14). r = .911, .837, .722; median rank r = .888 (R), .850 (D)
- Polarization (between minus within party distances) fell from the early 20th century to WWII, was stable until the late 1970s, and has risen steadily since; Congresses 100-105 accelerate the trend smoothly, with no jump after the 1994 freshman class. (Sec. 5, p.14-15 (Figs 7-8 not in text)). qualitative; no numbers in the text
- Party overlap has fallen to near zero: no overlap in the past 3 Senates and under 1.5 percent in both chambers for the past 5 Congresses. The 1940s-late-1970s hump is attributed to the Civil Rights split in the Democratic Party. (Sec. 5, p.15 (Fig 9 not in text)). no overlap (past 3 Senates); under 1.5% (past 5 Congresses)
- The current polarization of parties is comparable to the Federalist/Jeffersonian system of 1800 but well below late-19th/early-20th-century levels; Fig 10 covers normal two-party periods only and excludes the pre-Civil War and Civil War decade. (Sec. 5, p.15-16 (Fig 10 not in text)). qualitative
- Cross-national fit: the 105th House (one-dimensional 88.2% correct, APRE .644; two-dimensional 89.2%, .674) and Senate (88.0%, .642; 88.5%, .660) fit the spatial model about as well as most legislatures. On APRE only the Czech Parliament (.770), the 1841 British Parliament (.651) and the French National Assembly 1951-56 (.818) fit better. (Sec. 6, p.17; Table 4, p.28-29). House 88.2/.644 (1D); Czech 94.2/.770; 1841 British 89.7/.651; French 93.3/.818; EP3 89.8/.543; EP4 89.4/.536; Polish 88.9/.485
- Party per se is said to explain little roll call voting beyond the spatial model; parties are spatially adjacent ideological coalitions; members may respond to donors and primaries as well as party pressure. These claims rest on a cited 1999 working paper not presented here. (Sec. 6, p.17 (cites McCarty, Poole and Rosenthal 1999)). qualitative
- Conclusion: the trends in Congress (1997) have continued unabated; polarization is far from over, but is not as severe as in earlier eras, and no current issue appears capable of destabilizing the system as slavery did. (Sec. 7, p.18). qualitative

## Limitations

- Abstentions are forced into Yea or Nay and discarded as missing (endnote 9, p.30); abstention modeling is left to future work.
- Standard errors for DW-NOMINATE utility parameters are not econometrically correct because the full outer-product matrix (about 180,000 by 180,000 for the House) was not inverted; the authors expect the values to change little (p.8).
- The 3-Congress linear-trend threshold lets random walkers appear linear, biasing toward the polynomial models (p.6).
- Optimal classification was done only in one dimension; the two-dimensional version was deferred (p.9).
- Joint House-Senate scaling assumes that members keep fixed positions across chambers; the authors call this reasonably supported, but the 613 glue members lose about 1 point of classification under joint scaling (p.10-11).
- The 100th Congress was scaled separately and transformed to fit earlier Congresses (endnote 7, p.30). The text reports the 1-99 Senate classification as 84.6 while Table 1 (linear, 3-Cong, 1-99) reports 84.42; minor discrepancy.
- The cross-national claim that only the Czech, 1841 British and French National Assembly fit better than the 105th Congress holds on APRE and classification jointly. On classification alone, the Third and Fourth European Parliaments (89.8, 89.4) and the 1995 Polish Parliament (88.9) exceed the 105th House's 88.2 in one dimension (Table 4, p.28-29; text p.17).
- Figures 1-10 are not in the extracted text, so the trend claims resting on them (Figs 1B, 2B, 7-10) cannot be verified here.
- The party-discipline argument depends on a 1999 working paper (McCarty, Poole and Rosenthal) that is cited but not presented (p.17). The paper contains no causal analysis, so the 1994 break is assessed only descriptively (p.14-15).

## Gaps and flags

- No CU-specific roll-call scaling. The system has no member x CU roll-call matrix with dates (the brain says it is not in the repo), so it cannot test whether CU stances sit on dimension 1 or on a separate CU dimension. The paper shows this is the decisive measurement question.
- Say-do Path V counts a single final-passage vote (n >= 1) as costly-signal corroboration and does not condition on party or ideal point. In a polarized, one-dimensional chamber that largely corroborates party-line voting. A within-party or dim1-residualized vote criterion is missing.
- The engine uses a binary party dummy for partisan physics (cpf_diffusion / cpf_pressure at moneyball_core.py:543-548). It never uses ideological distance, although DW-NOMINATE is already loaded for psychometric controls (dw_control.py). Ideological distance is the paper's preferred variable.
- Party-confounded stance tiers (the CFPB axis) are not residualized on dim1 and extremity, although a loader exists.
- Abstention is ignored both in the paper (endnote 9) and in Path V. Strategic abstention on CU votes is unmeasured.
- The Path V party split (CFPB D 2% vs R 66%, Technology D 7% vs R 97%, Exam D 41% vs R 96%, Charter D 83% vs R 26%) is new agent analysis from one read-only path on 2026-10-09 (window: current vote_category_lean.json; denominators D 259 and R 271 from moneyball_core.party()). It is a re-run of the same function, not an independent second path, and is quoted only stratified by party.
- The component description for melange-sna.signed-coalitions (R sdsm vote backbone) is stale. The live fec/network/signed_coalitions.py:8 partitions the signed mention layer (member_references.json). The brain records Paul's rejection of DW-NOMINATE vote edges (topics/2026-06-feature-cosponsor-and-sector-networks.md:93). Nothing here recommends reinstating them.
- The paper's claim that members respond to donors and primaries (p.17, Paul's pink highlight) rests on an uncited-in-text 1999 working paper with no evidence presented. It must not be read as support for the competitiveness multiplier or for reciprocity, which is NOT validated (D-104..D-108).
