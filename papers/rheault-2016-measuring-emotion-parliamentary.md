# Measuring Emotion in Parliamentary Debates with Automated Textual Analysis

**Ludovic Rheault, Kaspar Beelen, Christopher Cochrane, Graeme Hirst (2016).** *PLOS ONE, vol 11 issue 12, e0168843 (published 22 December 2016).* [10.1371/journal.pone.0168843](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0168843). Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Rheault, Beelen, Cochrane and Hirst build a domain-adapted emotion lexicon for the UK House of Commons (Hansard 1909-2013, about 957 million tokens). They train 300-dimension GloVe vectors on lemma-PoS pairs, score each word by cosine similarity to 100 positive minus 100 negative emotion seeds, and keep the 4,200 most polar words. Period polarity is the lexicon-weighted sum divided by all lemmas, with a negation window to punctuation. Debate grows more positive over the century, and the rise holds under NRC, OpinionFinder and SentiWordNet. Recession years lower annual polarity change (ARMAX -0.198, SE 0.062, N=104 years, pooled over government and opposition, so UNSTRATIFIED). Election years raise it (+0.187). Labor disputes Granger-cause polarity (chi2 9.42, p=.002) but not the reverse. Split by side, the effect runs through the opposition (chi2 8.05, p=.005) and not the government (p=.761). The design is associational, and the authors say the cointegrating relation is not causal. The lexicon is validated only on film reviews, never on parliamentary speech. For Paul's systems the work matters in three places. It is the method source for the opt-in GloVe seed-expansion helper in the legacy press scorer. It qualifies the general-purpose LSD2015 tone intensity that feeds press_value: domain adaptation is argued for, the default intensity is |net|/words (a polarity magnitude, not a charge), and macro-period shifts in tone are not removed. And it shows that negative-emotion language in a legislature follows institutional role (opposition vs government) and economic shocks. That bears on the affective-N slot, which controls for party but not for majority status or period. All three are UNVERIFIED single-agent readings (DE-062).

## Study

**Question:** Can domain-adapted affective text analysis measure the emotional polarity of UK parliamentary debates over a century, and does that polarity change over time and respond to national economic conditions such as recessions and labor disputes?

**Theory:** Emotions shape decisions (behavioral economics, prospect theory, regret theory, neuroeconomics). Politicians represent constituents and so echo civilians' emotions in debate, so recessions and labor conflict should make debate more negative (pp.1-2, p.8). Elections raise positivity. Opposition MPs have accountability incentives to decry downturns, while government MPs avoid recognizing social ills, so the opposition is the channel (pp.13-14).

**Data:** UK House of Commons Hansard, all debates, oral questions and oral answers to written questions, 1909-2013 inclusive (p.2). About 956.8 million tokens, average 9.1 million per year (p.2). Lemmatized vocabulary of 108506 types with frequency 10 or more (p.3). Unit: lemma-PoS pairs for the lexicon; period-level polarity (yearly N=104 in the ARMAX; quarterly and yearly series elsewhere). Economic series, annual: log days lost to strikes, unemployment rate, misery index (unemployment plus inflation), GDP growth, plus a GDP-based recession dummy and election-year dummy. Sources: Dilipad linked parliamentary data (politicalmashup Hansard dumps); code and files on GitHub lrheault/emotion (p.2).

**Method:** Word vectors: GloVe, 300 dimensions per lemma-PoS pair, symmetric window of 15 tokens (p.3). Seeds: 200 hand-picked English emotion lemmas, 100 positive and 100 negative (p.3). Lexicon: each lemma scored in [-1,1] by summed cosine similarity to positive minus negative seeds (Eq 1, p.4); top and bottom 2000 kept, giving 4200 words. Polarity y_t: lexicon-weighted mean score divided by the count of all lemmas in the period, with a negation flag zeroing words between a negation cue and punctuation (Eqs 2-3, p.5). Stationarity: Dickey-Fuller and KPSS tests, unit root found (pp.8, S1 Appendix Tables A-D). Main model: ARMAX on first differences with recession and election dummies and 1 or 2 lags, maximum likelihood (Eq 5, Table 2, p.9). Group tests: one-sided t-tests on mean differences (Table 3, p.10). Granger causality: pairwise augmented VAR chi-square tests (Table 4, p.13). Cointegration: Johansen rank test; bivariate VECM with 2 lags in levels, orthogonalized impulse response with bootstrap bands (Fig 4, pp.12-13). Long-run effect: dynamic OLS with HAC standard errors (p.12). Long memory: spectral regression of log power on frequency, alpha in 1/f^alpha (p.7).

**Identification:** No causal design. Recession and election years enter ARMAX as exogenous binary regressors; Granger tests and VECM are time-series association tools. The authors state the cointegrating relation 'should not be interpreted as a causal effect' (p.12). The opposition mechanism is argued from theory and the group-split Granger result, not from an exogenous shock (pp.13-14).

**Scope:** Population: UK House of Commons debate text, 1909-2013 (one legislature, one country). Era: a century including two world wars, the 1926 general strike, the 1973-75 recession and the 1980s. Cross-domain portability is asserted and only validated on film reviews (p.5).

## Key findings

- Polarity rises over 1909-2013: debates become more positive in recent decades (Fig 1, p.6; text pp.5-7). The rise is robust to NRC, OpinionFinder and SentiWordNet lexicons (S1 Fig, p.7) and appears in government and opposition series (Fig 2, p.8). No slope coefficient is reported in the text.
- Recession years lower the annual change in polarity: coefficient -0.198 (SE 0.062) in ARMAX Model 1 and -0.165 (SE 0.063) in Model 2, both significant at 95 percent. Autoregressive terms rho1 -0.275 (SE 0.095) and -0.313 (SE 0.098); rho2 -0.145 (SE 0.104). N=104 years (Table 2, p.9).
- Election years raise polarity: coefficient 0.187 (SE 0.069) and 0.183 (SE 0.069) in the two ARMAX models (Table 2, p.9). Text says a similar order of magnitude, about 0.19.
- Mean-difference t-tests (Table 3, p.10): recession years differ from other years by -0.219 (t=-2.895, p=0.002; 21 recession years against 83 others). Election years differ by 0.202 (t=2.735, p=0.004). War years differ by 0.002 (p=0.511, not significant).
- Granger causality (Table 4, p.13): labor disputes Granger-cause polarity (chi2 9.424, df 1, p=0.002) but polarity does not Granger-cause labor disputes (chi2 0.056, p=0.813). Unemployment, misery index and GDP growth show no significant Granger effect in either direction (all p at least 0.12).
- Cointegration (Johansen test, S1 Appendix Table F, p.12): fitted long-run relation y_t = -0.06 - 1.39 x_t (Eq 6, p.12), with x_t the labor disputes indicator. The series stay negatively related, more than one standard deviation apart; authors say this is not a causal effect.
- Impulse response (Fig 4, p.13; text p.12): a one standard deviation shock to labor disputes produces a negative polarity response converging to about -0.18. Because the series is non-stationary, the shock is persistent until offset by an opposite shock.
- Dynamic OLS (Table G, S1 Appendix; text p.12): long-run effect of labor disputes on polarity about -0.9, roughly 0.9 standard deviations of polarity per standard deviation of disputes, significant at the 99.9 percent level with HAC standard errors.
- Party split (Table 4 bottom, p.13; S5 Fig; discussion pp.13-14): labor disputes Granger-cause opposition polarity (chi2 8.048, p=0.005) but not government polarity (chi2 0.092, p=0.761). Impulse responses are larger for opposition parties, which the authors read as the mechanism.
- Long memory (pp.7-8): quarterly spectral exponent alpha 1.03 (95 percent CI 0.86-1.20), close to pink noise. Yearly alpha 1.71 (CI 1.38-2.04), closer to Brownian noise, so unit-root methods are used.
- Seed-usage check (p.7, Google Books Ngram): relative frequency of the 100 positive seed lemmas fell 9 percent from 1909-1918 to 1999-2008, while the 100 negative seeds rose 2 percent. Authors conclude the trend is not an artifact of seed choice.
- Validation (p.5, S1 Appendix): the polarity measure alone predicts up to 80 percent of film ratings accurately, and classifiers adding it compare favorably to other sentiment models. Validation is on film reviews, not parliamentary speech.

## Gaps and flags

- LSD2015 tone has never been validated against human-coded intensity on congressional press releases. Rheault's own domain lexicon was validated only on film reviews, so neither the general nor the adapted approach has in-domain evidence here.
- The default intensity, abs(net/words) via INTENSITY=net_norm, is a polarity magnitude, not a charge. Mixed-valence heated releases score low. No test compares net_norm with lsd_charge against a behaviour criterion.
- Tone and affect measures carry no period or date adjustment. Rheault shows chamber-wide tone shifts with recessions and election years and persists (unit root). Whether member press and interview windows are date-balanced is unchecked.
- The affective-N slot controls for party but not for majority or minority status, while Rheault finds negativity concentrated in the opposition. No test splits interview negativity by majority status across the 2019-2026 chamber flips.
- The opt-in GloVe expansion helper uses stance words as seeds (support, champion, protect), which blends direction toward credit unions with affect. Rheault-style emotion-only seeds would keep tone separate from stance.
- Component description drift: components.txt describes affective_n as z(LIWC) + z(LLM), but emit_affective_n.py:39-43 says LIWC was dropped as a component in round-7b (2026-07-11) and kept only as the convergent anchor (r=+.24). The description should be refreshed.
- The paper's ARMAX recession and election coefficients are pooled over the government-opposition fault line that the same paper shows matters, so quote them as UNSTRATIFIED. Only the Granger tests are stratified.
- The paper suggests studying emotional contagion over parliamentary networks (Coviello ref 63). Any such use in Melange would put influence-style language on network edges. Treat it as an untested idea, not a recommendation, and never on revolving-door edges.
- All bearings are a single-agent reading and stay UNVERIFIED until a second path reproduces them (DE-062).
