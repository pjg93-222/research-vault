# Roman Urdu Stance Detection RoBERTa

**Bilal Khan, Khairullah Khan, Fida Muhammad Khan, Haseena Noureen, Ahmad Ali, Mohsin Shah (2025).** *IECE Transactions on Advanced Computing and Systems, Vol. 1, No. 2, pp. 78-96 (published 26 May 2025; submitted 24 Feb 2025, accepted 27 Apr 2025).* [10.62762/TACS.2025.928069](https://doi.org/10.62762/TACS.2025.928069) Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Khan et al. (2025) built a Roman Urdu dataset of Pakistani political tweet-and-reply pairs, about 8,374 labelled agree, disagree or unrelated. They benchmarked five TF-IDF classifiers against a fine-tuned RoBERTa. Reported accuracy: random forest 0.95, SVM 0.94, logistic regression 0.93, decision tree 0.92, naive bayes 0.89, all on 1,675 test rows. RoBERTa reached 0.97, but on an "augmented" set with support 6,699, which the paper does not document as held out. That support equals 80 percent of the corpus, so the RoBERTa row may not be on the same test split as the others (an inference). There is one 80/20 split, with no kappa, no seeds, no hyperparameters and no confidence intervals. The augmentation is described with generic transformations (rotation, flipping, cropping, scaling, noise, colour adjustments) and no text-specific procedure, and the conclusion contradicts the results section. As evidence the paper is weak. The ranking gaps of 1 to 4 points have no error bars, and the transformer-wins headline is not a like-for-like comparison.
> 
> It still matters here because the system cites it as a method source. fec/stance/train_stance_domain.py is literally titled "Khan et al. pipeline". It copies the paired target-conditioned input, the five-classifier ladder, the class-balancing analog and the expectation that "transformer should beat all". fec/stance/stance_eval.py takes macro-F1 as its headline and adds "unrelated" as a class "per ICCK/TACS" practice. Per the analyst, on the system's own press data the paper's two transferable claims did not hold (a transformer on the augmented press set at 0.757 macro-F1, below a tuned word+char LinearSVC at 0.816; the Khan multi-view augmentation rejected on 2026-07-03). These figures were not re-verified. The lasting value is in the evaluation conventions: "unrelated" as its own class, macro-F1 as the headline, and fine-tuning only once labels are clean. The model-ranking claims do not carry over. The code docstring repeats the paper's 0.93 to 0.97 figures as if they were faithful, comparable numbers. It should flag that the RoBERTa support (6,699) differs from the classical test support (1,675) and is not documented as held out.

## Study

**Question.** Can a fine-tuned RoBERTa model, and five traditional machine learning classifiers on TF-IDF features, classify stance (agree / disagree / unrelated) in Roman Urdu political tweets and replies, and which performs best? A secondary aim is to release a labeled Roman Urdu political stance dataset, since none was publicly available (Abstract p.78; Intro objectives p.80).

**Theory.** No explicit theory is tested. Stance is framed as a dialogic social act with evaluation, positioning and alignment, citing definitions attributed to "Kockelman" and "John" (pp.79-80). The task is modeled as three-class text classification (Fig. 1, p.80). No hypotheses are stated.

**Data.** Sample: 8,374 political tweets and comments (Abstract p.78). Table 1 (p.86) reports 8,373 rows and 11 columns, so the abstract and table differ by one row. Unit: a tweet-comment pair combined into one input sequence (p.92). Table 2 (p.87) shows example rows with tweet ID, comment, POS tags, named entities, dependency features, source, stance label and sentiment score. Labels: agree, disagree, unrelated. Source: Twitter API, keyword/hashtag search on Pakistani party and leader names (#ImranKhan, #NawazSharif, #PPP, #PMLN, #PTI; p.84). Collection period: not reported. Annotation: three annotators, with discussion to resolve disagreements; inter-annotator agreement described only as "substantial", with no statistic (p.84). Split: 80% train, 20% test (p.88). Test support is 1,675 (Agree 583, Disagree 602, Unrelated 490; Tables 3-7). The RoBERTa table (Table 8, p.93) reports support 6,699 on "the augmented dataset" (p.92), so RoBERTa and the classical models appear to be evaluated on different-sized sets; 6,699 equals 80 percent of the corpus and the paper does not document it as held out. Data availability: "on request" (p.94).

**Method.** Preprocessing: stop-word removal, tokenization, POS tagging, lowercasing, lemmatization and phonetic spelling normalization (Section 3.3, pp.86-87). Features: TF-IDF for the classical models (pp.88-91). Classifiers: logistic regression (Eq. 5), decision tree with entropy/information gain (Eqs. 6-7), SVM with margin objective and kernel (Eqs. 8-10; kernel type not stated), random forest with majority vote (Eqs. 11-13; number of trees not stated), naive bayes (Eqs. 14-15). Deep model: RoBERTa fine-tuned on the concatenated tweet and comment, with a [CLS] classification head and softmax (pp.91-92). Dataset augmentation and class balancing are said to be applied (p.91), but the augmentation is described with generic transformations (rotation, flipping, color changes) and no text-augmentation procedure is given. Platform: Google Colab, Python (Section 4.1, p.88). Metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1 (Section 3.6, p.87). No hyperparameters, epochs, seeds, cross-validation, confidence intervals or significance tests are reported.

**Identification.** None. This is a descriptive benchmark on a single random 80/20 split of one dataset. No causal or quasi-experimental design is used.

**Scope.** Population: political tweets and replies in Roman Urdu (code-mixed, informal, multiple spellings) about Pakistani parties and leaders, drawn from Twitter (now X). Institution: none (not a government, party or legislative dataset). Era: collection dates not stated; published May 2025. Language scope: explicitly Roman Urdu, with claims about low-resource and code-mixed languages in general (Abstract p.78; Section 5 p.94).

## Key findings

- Fine-tuned RoBERTa has the highest overall accuracy of the six models, on the augmented evaluation set. (Accuracy 0.97; Agree P/R/F1 0.98/0.93/0.95; Disagree 0.95/0.99/0.97; Unrelated 0.96/0.99/0.98; support 6,699; Table 8 p.93; Section 4.4.2 p.92; Section 4.5 p.93; Abstract p.78)
- Random forest is the best classical model, followed by SVM, logistic regression, decision tree and naive bayes. (RF 0.95 (macro F1 0.95, test n=1,675); SVM 0.94; LR 0.93; DT 0.92; NB 0.89; Table 6 p.90 (RF); Table 5 p.90 (SVM); Table 3 p.89 (LR); Table 4 p.89 (DT); Table 7 p.91 (NB); summary Section 4.5 p.93)
- Naive bayes is called the weakest model, 'close to random guessing'. (NB accuracy 0.89. The majority-class share on the test set is about 0.36 (602/1,675), so the 'random guessing' wording is not supported by the reported numbers.; Section 4.5 p.93)
- Computational cost is said to trade off against accuracy (RoBERTa costlier, RF cheaper), but no training time, memory or FLOP figures are reported. (None reported; Section 4.5 p.93)
- Errors concentrate on sarcastic, ambiguous and implicit statements; some disagree items are labeled unrelated when negation is absent. No error counts or confusion matrix are shown. (None reported; Section 4.5 p.93)
- Dataset augmentation and class balancing are credited with improving performance, but no with/without comparison is shown. (None reported; Section 4.4.1 p.91; Section 4.5 p.93)
- The paper claims a 97% result on an independent Twitter dataset, but that dataset is not described. (97% on an unnamed independent dataset; size and source not given; Section 3.5 p.85)
- The F1 discussion leaves template placeholders unfilled in the text, next to the table. (Placeholders [X], [Y], [Z] not filled in; Section 4.5 p.94)

## Limitations

- Stated by authors: RoBERTa misreads sarcastic, ambiguous and indirect statements, and implicit disagreement (p.93). The authors call for sarcasm detection and better contextual embeddings.
- Stated by authors: future work on other transformer architectures (XLM-R, XLNet, GPT-style and multilingual models) (pp.91, 94); hybrid stance plus fake-news classification; domain adaptation; cross-domain training with related low-resource languages; code-switching; a wider set of political topics; tools for other low-resource languages (p.94).
- Stated by authors: BERT and LSTMs were excluded for lower accuracy on Roman Urdu and higher compute, but no numbers are shown (p.91).
- Reader check, not an author claim: the dataset size is 8,374 in the abstract and 8,373 in Table 1. The test support of 1,675 for the classical models differs from the 6,699 support used for RoBERTa, so the models may not be compared on one common test split (the 6,699 support equals 80 percent of the corpus; an inference).
- Reader check: no inter-annotator agreement statistic (kappa or alpha) is reported; 'substantial agreement' is asserted only (p.84).
- Reader check: no error bars, repeated runs, cross-validation or significance tests; a single 80/20 split with differences of 1-4 points between models.
- Reader check: RoBERTa training details (learning rate, epochs, batch size, seed, max length) and classical hyperparameters (SVM kernel, RF tree count, TF-IDF settings) are missing, so the study cannot be reproduced from the text.
- Reader check: the augmentation paragraph describes generic transformations (rotation, flipping, color adjustment) and gives no text-augmentation method (p.91).
- Reader check: the Conclusion says all six models 'turned out as the best model' (p.94), which contradicts Section 4.5, and it mentions 'software repositories' (p.94), which does not match the Twitter political-stance task.
- Reader check: collection period, retrieval date, keyword list completeness and sampling bias are not reported; the dataset is not public ('on request', p.94).
- Reader check: reference-list errors. Yan et al. (ref [9], p.94) gives 2020 and 2018 in one entry. Ref [12] (Kavatagi & Rachh, a hate-speech paper) and ref [17] (Kucuk 2017, joint NER and stance) do not match the topics the text attributes to them, so the related-work citations need checking.
- Reader check: no network, lobbying, persuasion, turnout or legislator analysis, and no election-outcome linkage; the political content is only the topic domain.

## Gaps and flags

- The paper's one usable pointer is that implicit and indirect stance is the residual failure. The system knows its dominant error (implicit support read as neutral, n=78), but it has no dedicated handling for implicit stance or sarcasm in either the SVM or DEBATE readers.
- The production press classifier (stance_clf_composed.pkl) still has no 'unrelated' class (07-03 note), although the evaluation harness and tweet trainer treat 'unrelated' as first-class. Training and evaluation label spaces are mismatched on the press side.
- train_stance_domain.py lists the paper's accuracies as a faithful baseline and expects 'transformer should beat all'. The docstring does not record that the paper scored RoBERTa on a different test support (6,699 vs 1,675), or that the system's own result, per the analyst and not re-verified, reversed the ordering. A reader of the code would inherit an unsupported expectation.
- The code comment at fec/stance/train_stance_domain.py:103 ('transformer should beat all') and the docstring at :5-8 present the paper's model ordering as established. The paper's RoBERTa and classical results use different supports (6,699 vs 1,675), and the system reportedly measured the opposite (0.757 vs 0.816 macro-F1, not re-verified). This is a stale or unsupported expectation embedded in code, not a validated prior.
- Brain notes conflict on the augmentation history. The 2026-07-02 note credits '--augment (Khan multi-view)' plus tuning with the 83.9% / 0.816 result. The 2026-07-03 note says Khan augmentation hurts classical models and was rejected, with tune-only reaching 0.835. The component description follows the 07-03 version. Any citation of the 0.816 figure should state which configuration produced it.
- Any quote of the paper's 0.97 must name its denominator: support 6,699 on the 'augmented dataset', not the 1,675-row test used for the classical models. The paper's own 'close to random guessing' claim for naive bayes at 0.89 is false against a majority-class share of about 0.36.
