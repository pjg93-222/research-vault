# Lobbying coalitions and their determinants: a systematic literature review

**Andrea Pritoni, Alberto Bitonti, Giuseppe Montalbano (2026 (vol. 18; first published online 2025-11-05)).** *European Political Science Review, 18 (2026), pp. 1-17.* [10.1017/S1755773925100143](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1755773925100143). Theme: [[themes/lobbying|Lobbying]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Pritoni, Bitonti and Montalbano run a PRISMA-ScR scoping review of 80 English-language political science and public administration articles on lobbying coalitions, published 1985-2023 and indexed in Web of Science. Sixteen of those articles, contributing 77 hypotheses, enter a vote-count meta-analysis. Large-N quantitative designs dominate the field: 42 of 80 articles (52.5%). Social network analysis appears in only 4 of 80 (5%), and QCA in none. Most studies define coalitions by behavior: 59 of 80 use behavioral coordination, 12 use policy camp and 6 use formal organization. Across all 77 hypotheses, 57 are supported (74%). That figure is UNSTRATIFIED by polity. By level, support is meso 11/14, micro 35/45, macro 10/16 and composite 1/2. The 'weapon of the weak' hypothesis is supported in 11 of 12 tests, though a minority of studies find the reverse, and the authors suggest a minimum-resource threshold. Issue context is supported 6/7, ideology 6/7 and third-party brokers 3/3. The SNA-based tests have the weakest record of any design, 5 of 9 supported (55.6%; the 2010s SNA-design row of Table 5). The descriptive mapping covers all 80 articles, but the 16-article hypothesis meta-analysis studies only why groups form coalitions, and it also drops policy-camp studies and keeps only group- or lobbyist-level units. The review calls the evidence on whether coalitions win contradictory and leaves that question to future work.
> 
> For Paul's systems, the paper matters because the coalitions it studies are coalitions among interest groups. Melange's 'coalitions' are mostly blocs of legislators: Ward clusters on press lean, signed partitions, and cosponsor spread. Read strictly, the review supports two narrower points about how Melange models the industries themselves. First, coalition alignment depends on the issue, which favors the per-bill contested registry over fixed global sides. Second, the CU side is modeled as a lone actor, even though the literature says weaker groups pool resources. The review gives no evidence that coalitions raise influence, so neither point justifies adding allied money as a weight.

## Study

**Question:** RQ1: What are the key characteristics and trends of research in the academic debate on lobbying coalitions? RQ2: What are the main explanations used to account for interest groups' choice to form or join a coalition? (p. 3)

**Theory:** No single theory is adopted. The authors say the field is implicitly shaped by a rationalist framework with methodological individualism (fn. 13, p. 9). Hypotheses are sorted into macro, meso and micro levels, plus composite hypotheses. The 'weapon of the weak' argument (weaker groups pool resources; Hanegraaff and Pritoni 2019) is the central tested proposition. Interest-group definitions follow Truman (1951) and Berry (1977); coalition definitions follow Junk (2020b): policy camp, behavioral coordination, or formal organization.

**Data:** Web of Science Core Collection, English-language 'article' documents, 1 Jan 1985 to 31 Dec 2023, title/keyword search for lobby, coalition, interest, group, alliance, restricted to Political Science and Public Administration categories. Initial hits N=45; after abstract screening N=34 eligible; after snowballing N=80 final sample (unit: article). Coded by two researchers with a codebook. Meta-analysis subsample: 16 articles, 77 theoretical hypotheses (unit: hypothesis), restricted to articles that treat coalition formation as the dependent variable, use behavioral or organizational definitions, use the individual group or lobbyist as unit, and test hypotheses empirically.

**Method:** PRISMA-ScR scoping review protocol (Tricco et al. 2018) with descriptive counts (year, venue, polity, design, definitions) and a vote-count style meta-analysis: for each hypothesis, supported or not supported; confirmation rate = supported / total by level, factor and decade. Intercoder reliability by Krippendorff's alpha on 8 randomly selected articles (10% of sample): K-alpha = 0.889 (p. 4). Decade assignment uses article publication year.

**Identification:** Not applicable: descriptive literature review with no causal identification strategy. The authors frame coalition formation as the dependent variable and note that the influence-of-coalitions question is left for future work (pp. 9, 15).

**Scope:** Population: English-language articles in WoS political science and public administration journals on interest groups and lobbying coalitions, 1985-2023 (excludes sociology, economics and business journals, books, chapters and grey literature; fn. 3, 4, 17). Geography dominated by the US (41 of 80) and EU (25 of 80). Era: 1985-2023, decades 1990s, 2000s, 2010s, 2020s.

## Key findings

- Attention to lobbying coalitions has risen slowly; at least three articles per year since 2013; peaks in 2000, 2014, 2016, 2019. (2000: 4 of 19; 2014: 7 of 38; 2016: 4 of 26; 2019: 9 of 60; 80 articles total; p. 5 (Fig. 2 text))
- Most-receptive venues are Interest Groups & Advocacy and the Journal of European Public Policy. (IG&A 10; JEPP 9; Journal of Politics 6; EU Politics 5; AJPS 4; APSR 4; p. 5; Table 1 p. 6)
- Political systems studied are dominated by the US and EU; Canada and Australia each appear once. (US 41; EU 25; Germany 9; Netherlands 7; UK 6; Denmark 5; Sweden 5; p. 5-6; Table 2 p. 6)
- Large-N quantitative designs dominate; SNA is rare and QCA is absent. (Large-N 42 of 80 (52.5%); combination of methods 12 (15.0%); qualitative case study 11 (13.8%); small-N comparison 9 (11.2%); SNA 4 (5.0%); theory only 2 (2.5%); QCA 0; p. 6-7; Table 3 p. 7; Table A1 (online appendix))
- Behavioral definitions of interest groups and coalitions dominate; 14 studies give no interest-group definition. (Interest group: behavioral 56 of 80 (70%), organizational 10 (12.5%), unstated 14 (17.5%). Coalition: behavioral 59, policy camp 12, formal organization 6; p. 7, p. 9, p. 13)
- Overall, 74% of tested hypotheses are supported. (57 supported, 20 not supported, 77 total; 74.0%; Table 4 p. 10; text p. 10-11)
- Hypothesis support by level: meso best, macro worst, micro near meso. (Macro 10/16 (62.5%); meso 11/14 (78.6%); micro 35/45 (77.8%); composite 1/2 (50%); Table 4 p. 10; p. 11)
- Factor-level support: relative weakness and third-party brokers are most consistently supported; similarity and type of interest least. (Relative weakness 11/12 (91.7%); third parties 3/3 (100%); issue context 6/7 (85.7%); ideology 6/7 (85.7%); relative strength 5/7 (71.4%); coalition features 5/7 (71.4%); external challenges 10/16 (62.5%); similarity 7/11 (63.6%); type of interest 3/5 (60%); Table 4 p. 10)
- Weapon of the weak: most studies find weaker groups more likely to coalesce; a significant minority find the opposite; the authors suggest a minimum-resource threshold. (Relative weakness 11 of 12 hypotheses supported; cited counter-evidence: Beyers and Donas 2014; Gray and Lowery 1998; p. 11)
- Diachronic pattern: knowledge accumulation in the 1990s-2000s, a setback in the 2010s; macro factors abandoned after the 1990s. (1990s 19-8 (70.4%); 2000s 9-0 (100%); 2010s 20-7 (74.1%); SNA studies 5-4 (55.6%); mixed-method 4-1 (80%); Table 5 p. 12; text p. 11-13)
- Conditions for coalition formation: high-salience, conflictual issues; ideological propensity to pool resources; third-party brokers. (No new counts; supported by the hypothesis tallies above; p. 11 (conditions i-iii); p. 13 (conclusion))
- The effect of coalitions on influence is contradictory in the literature; not reviewed here. (Positive: Junk 2019; Nelson and Yackee 2012. Negative: Bunea 2013; Haider-Markel 2006; Heinz et al. 1993; Junk 2020a; p. 8)
- Future research: composite micro-meso-macro models, SNA and QCA, a possibly curvilinear resource-coalition relation, and coalitions as explanatory variables for influence. (Not measured; proposals only; p. 14-15)

## Limitations

- WoS Core Collection starts in 1985; pre-1985 literature is excluded (p. 14).
- Restriction to political science and public administration journals may exclude relevant work in other fields; sociology journals on nonprofits are excluded on purpose (fn. 4, fn. 17).
- Books, book chapters and grey literature are excluded, which the authors say may bias the sample toward quantitative studies (fn. 3).
- Snowball sampling introduces path dependence toward older, more-cited articles (fn. 5, fn. 7); more than half of the final sample came from snowballing (p. 5).
- Keyword expansion (advocacy, organisation, pressure) added only 6 articles, which the authors read as validating the search terms (fn. 2).
- Publication bias toward US and EU studies is acknowledged (fn. 10).
- Hypothesis meta-analysis uses a dichotomy (supported or not), losing granularity about strength and limits (p. 14).
- Only 16 of 80 articles enter the hypothesis analysis, and hypotheses are counted rather than weighted by study quality.
- Intercoder reliability was checked on only 8 articles (10%) (p. 4).
- Field fragmentation: the authors note many hypotheses and a lack of one unified framework, which may explain the mixed findings (fn. 13).

## Gaps and flags

- Melange has no model of coalition formation among interest groups. Credit unions are a single actor, and the other industries are fixed 'opposition' in every context (build_opposition_prior.py:68-75). The literature's main determinants (relative weakness, issue salience and conflict, ideological propensity, third-party brokers) are not represented.
- The contested-bills schema cannot record an allied industry on a bill, only counterparties (contested_bills.py:175-176). Strange-bedfellow alignments therefore cannot be stored.
- The system has no group-to-group broker layer. Third-party brokers are the factor with the most uniform support (3/3), but no Melange component represents brokerage between interest groups. The staffer-network 'bridging' measure concerns staff layers and must not be read as brokerage or introduction access (D-025).
- Several components call legislator clusters 'coalitions' (Ward blocs, signed partitions, cosponsor coalition spread). The review's definitional typology shows that none of them records coordination, so the term should be scoped wherever it is displayed.
- D-021: the natural way to operationalize 'behavioral coordination' coalitions from Melange data would be an industry-to-industry graph from co-registration on the same bills or issue codes in LDA. That would use LDA as an edge or weight source, which D-021 bars. LDA may only identify allies, never connect or weight them.
- The paper's call for more SNA (Paul highlighted 'SNA and QCA') is not evidence that SNA works. SNA-based hypotheses have the lowest confirmation rate in Table 5: 5 of 9 (55.6%; 2010s SNA-design row), versus 74% overall. Quote that only with its denominator and window. The paper does not argue for either rejected SNA proposal (dropping regular cosponsorship edges; per-edge volume normalization), so nothing needs flagging on that front.
- The 74% overall confirmation rate and the 91.7% weapon-of-the-weak rate are vote counts on the 16-article subsample, whose polity mix is not reported (the US 41/80 and EU 25/80 split describes the full 80-article sample). They are vote counts unweighted by study quality and should be quoted as UNSTRATIFIED.
- Staleness: the component card and brain note say all Ward blocs are labelled pro-CU (a known mislabel). The current R file at cu_press_release_scraper.R:3193-3195 labels blocs as pro, hostile or mixed by mean bloc lean. Which version produced the live output is UNVERIFIED and needs a second check (DE-062).
- Any pairing of this review with Melange (for example, CUs as the 'weaker' group by the joined LDA totals of ABA $228M vs CUNA $93M, vintage 2024-10-25 with coverage reliable through 2021) is new agent analysis and stays UNVERIFIED (DE-062).

Paul recorded no takeaways for this paper; both annotation entries are citation-only. His highlights stress three points: SNA is underused, coalitions form around salient, conflictual issues, and third-party brokers matter. The component map attaches the paper to melange-sna.press-coalition-ward. That link merges two different coalition concepts. Pritoni reviews coalitions among interest groups, while the Ward component clusters legislators by press lean, which is at most a 'policy camp' with no coordination evidence. The paper's real implications lie elsewhere. It supports the per-bill contested registry (issue-specific alignment) and exposes a gap: the CU side is modeled as a lone actor with all other industries fixed as opposition. Paul's highlight on SNA underuse should not be taken as an endorsement of SNA. In this review SNA-design tests have the lowest design-row support rate in Table 5 (5/9, 2010s). - [[aref-2021-identifying-hidden-coalitions]] — complements — Aref and Neal find k-cluster coalitions among House members from signed cosponsorship networks. That is the legislator sense of 'coalition'. Pritoni covers the interest-group sense.
- [[aref-2020-detecting-coalitions-optimally]] — complements — This is the earlier two-group signed-partition method on legislator cosponsorship backbones. It works at the legislator level, so Pritoni's group-level determinants do not transfer to it.
- [[kim-2025-measuring-interest-group]] — complements — Kim et al.'s per-bill Support/Oppose/Engage positions from LDA reports could show the issue-specific group alignments Pritoni describes. D-021 still limits LDA to identity, not edges or weights.
- [[moreno-2021-shadowing-shielding-effective]] — complements — The component-literature map cites Romero Moreno 2021 for the contested-bills component (it models a controller against a fixed opponent). Pritoni supplies the empirical case for setting counterparties per issue rather than globally.
- [[azaiez-2025-hypergraph-analysis-european]] — complements — Azaiez and Mandel apply hypergraph network methods to European Commission lobby meetings. It is an instance of the network analysis of lobbying that Pritoni finds rare (SNA as the main design in 4 of 80 articles).
- [[kim-2020-mapping-political-communities]] — complements — Kim and Kunisky fit a bipartite link-community model to interest-group-to-politician lobbying networks. It is a statistical network approach to lobbying communities, though it does not model coalitions between groups.
