> [!abstract] Summary
> Broockman and Kalla run a pre-registered forced-choice conjoint with 6,000 registered voters matched to the L2 voter file who voted in the 2024 general election (Verasight, weighted; the voter-file match is the verification). Respondents choose between hypothetical 2028 presidential pairs of real politicians. On each of three issues, drawn from a bank of 29, each candidate is randomly given either the party's standard position or an "elite middle" position. Pooled, the move to the middle gains Democrats +1.1 pp and Republicans +1.8 pp. The authors argue that this pooled figure hides offsetting effects, so it should only be quoted with its strata. The effect is largest where the party's own position is unpopular (Republicans +3.2 pp in tercile 3 of Table A6, read as the most liberal-opinion tercile; that ordering is inferred from the pattern of results, not stated beside the table). Where a majority already backs the party's position, the effect is null or negative (Republicans -2.4 pp). At the individual level, voters who hold the party's position punish the move (Republican-position holders -3.6 pp), while voters who disagree reward it. Even "moderate" respondents (the paper's own definition: liberal on fewer than 75% of issues for the Democratic analysis, more than 25% for the Republican one) reward the move only on issues where they disagree with the party, and fewer than 10% answered consistently across five issues. The authors' reading is that voters do vote on issues, but along several dimensions at once, so gains and losses cancel out in the average.
> 
> For Paul's systems, the paper bears mainly on Terrier's canvass layer. Terrier's support score and its "undecided" persuasion term both treat a person's partisan midpoint as the place where persuadability lives. That is a one-dimensional reading. The paper suggests that a mixed partisan score usually marks a cross-pressured voter: someone who can be moved on particular issues and pushed away on others, not a voter persuadable in general. Terrier has no data on voters' issue positions and no input for the candidate's positions, so it cannot represent this. For Melange, the evidence carries over only by analogy, because the paper studies voters, not legislators. Its main structural lesson for Melange is that an average across issues hides offsetting issue-level responses.

## Study

**Question:** Why do candidates appear to gain only modestly from moving toward the other party's positions, and does that small measured effect mean voters do not vote on issues? The paper asks whether relaxing two common assumptions (that movement toward the other party or the elite middle is movement toward the median voter, and that voter preferences are one-dimensional) can make the measured effect small even when every voter votes on issues.

**Theory:** Spatial model of elections (Black 1948; Downs 1957). Two assumptions are relaxed: (1) movement toward the other party or the "elite middle" equals movement toward the median voter, and (2) voters' preferences are one-dimensional. With multidimensional, cross-pressured voters and party positions that can be popular, moving toward the elite middle can win some voters and lose others. The net effect can be null or negative even when all voters vote on issues (Figure 1c, pp. 7-9). The argument is partial equilibrium and does not model candidate entry or general-equilibrium position choice.

**Data:** Survey conjoint experiment fielded by Verasight on 6,000 registered voters matched to the L2 voter file who voted in the 2024 general election and gave informed consent. Weighted with Verasight weights to the 2024 general electorate (Table A1, which also lists demographics). Unit of analysis: respondent x task x candidate x policy (N(Dem) = 180,000 and N(Rep) = 180,000 observations in regression tables; 6,000 respondents x 10 tasks x 3 policies). Each respondent completed a pre-treatment battery on 5 randomly selected of 29 policy issues. Pilot: 4,028 Prolific respondents, August 2025 (Appendix F). Fielding dates of the main study are not stated in the text. Candidates: 2028 hypothetical pairs of real politicians (Table A2 lists 26 names with perceived ideology and name recognition; Table A3 candidate-level vote shares).

**Method:** Forced-choice conjoint with two candidates (one Democrat, one Republican) per task, each with three randomly assigned issue positions from a bank of 29. Each position is independently assigned to the standard party position or the elite middle position with equal probability. Primary estimand: AMCE of adopting the elite middle position. Estimating equation (Eq. 1, p. 12): Y_ijk = alpha + beta * EliteMiddle_ijk + X_i gamma + e_ijk, estimated separately for Democratic and Republican candidates, with pre-registered pre-treatment covariates (ideology, presidential vote, share liberal in district/state, race, college, party ID 1-7 in Tables A4-A9). Heterogeneity: interactions with policy-domain indicators, terciles of public support for the liberal position (Figure 4), and an indicator for majority support for the party's standard position (Figure 5). Individual-level analyses split by the respondent's own pre-measured issue position (Figures 6-7). Standard errors clustered by respondent; survey weights applied throughout. Pre-registered at OSF (footnote 13). The estimator is described only as a linear model of the form in Eq. 1; the paper does not name it explicitly.

**Identification:** Randomized conjoint design. Each candidate's position on each issue is independently randomized between the standard party position and the elite middle position, so the elite-middle coefficient is identified by randomization within the experiment (AMCE). Candidate names are real and the perceived-party positions were validated (Figure A1: large majorities identified party positions). The estimand is partial-equilibrium and does not capture candidate entry, general-equilibrium repositioning or campaign-level persuasion.

**Scope:** Population: US registered voters who voted in the 2024 general election (weighted to the 2024 electorate). Institution: hypothetical 2028 presidential general-election matchups in a survey experiment, not actual elections. Era: current public opinion as of fielding (after 2024); the paper states its findings describe incentives under current opinion. Issues: 29 domains (abortion, immigration, healthcare, taxes, etc.) that map onto party divides; emerging non-partisan issues were dropped after the pilot.

## Key findings

- Average effect of adopting the elite middle position (vs standard party position) on one of three issues: Democratic candidates gain 1.1 pp, Republican candidates gain 1.8 pp, both p < 0.001 (pooled, UNSTRATIFIED). Described as modest. (Dem +0.0110 (SE 0.0018); Rep +0.0181 (SE 0.0018); Results, p. 14; Table A5)
- Issue heterogeneity is substantial. Democratic effects range from about -1.7 pp (Social Security) to +4.5 pp (affirmative action). Republican effects range from -4.2 pp (transgender sports) to +7.3 pp (gays and lesbians). (Dem: -1.7 to +4.5 pp; Rep: -4.2 to +7.3 pp; Results, pp. 14-16; Figure 3; Table A4)
- Returns are largest where the party's standard position is least popular. Democrats benefit most on policies where their standard position is furthest from the median voter (more conservative public). Republicans benefit most where the public is liberal. Little benefit where the party already reflects majority sentiment. (Dem tercile-1 interaction +0.0215 (SE 0.0028), tercile-2 +0.0105 (SE 0.0031), tercile-3 -0.0001 (SE 0.0032); Rep tercile-1 +0.0008 (SE 0.0029), tercile-2 +0.0229 (SE 0.0030), tercile-3 +0.0319 (SE 0.0032). Tercile labels as printed in Table A6.; p. 17; Figure 4; Table A6 (tercile interactions))
- On issues where a majority prefers the party's standard position, moving to the elite middle has a null or negative effect. The Republican effect is significant (about -2.4 pp, p < 0.001). The Democratic effect is negative and not significant. (Rep majority-issue -0.0237 (SE 0.0048); Dem majority-issue -0.0042 (SE 0.0041); Rep non-majority +0.0247 (SE 0.0020); Dem non-majority +0.0142 (SE 0.0019); pp. 18-19; Figure 5; Table A7)
- At the individual level, the same move helps and hurts the same candidate. Respondents who hold the party's standard position on an issue do not reward the elite-middle move and may penalize it. Republican candidates lose among respondents who hold the Republican position (about -3.6 pp). Democratic respondents with the Democratic position show a non-significant effect. (Dem candidate: own=D -0.0068 (SE 0.0064), own=M +0.0331 (SE 0.0078), own=R +0.0264 (SE 0.0069). Rep candidate: own=D +0.0404 (SE 0.0070), own=M +0.0562 (SE 0.0076), own=R -0.0364 (SE 0.0071).; pp. 19-20; Figure 6; Table A8)
- Respondents classified as overall moderate (the paper's definition: liberal on fewer than 75% of issues for the Democratic analysis, more than 25% for the Republican one) still reward moving to the elite middle only on issues where they disagree with the party's standard position. Moderates who agree with the Republican position on an issue penalize Republican candidates who move to the elite middle. (Rep candidate, own=R -0.0524 (SE 0.0112); Rep candidate, own=D +0.0381 (SE 0.0072); Dem candidate, own=D -0.0011 (SE 0.0113), own=R +0.0239 (SE 0.0069). Per Table A9's note, the moderate subsample is N(Dem) = 5,056-7,983 and N(Rep) = 4,806-10,404 across columns.; pp. 20-21; Figure 7; Table A9 (Table A9 covers the moderate subsample))
- Less than 10% of respondents gave consistently Democratic, Republican, or elite-middle answers across all five issues. The authors read this as cross-pressure, not measurement error. (<10% consistent across 5 issues; Results, p. 14 (see footnote 14, p. 14))
- The paper's main claim is interpretive. A small average return to moderation does not show voters ignore issues. Voters vote on issues, but in a multidimensional way that one-dimensional models miss. (; Introduction p. 2-4; Figure 1c p. 8; Conclusion pp. 21-24)
- Results are reported as roughly linear in the number of elite-middle positions a candidate takes, with limited interaction between the two candidates' choices. (; Appendix Figure A10 (text reference on p. 14, footnote 15))
- Pilot (4,028 Prolific respondents, Aug 2025): adding candidate messages had minimal effects. Emerging non-partisan issues (school phones, monopoly policy, supply constraints) gave no consistent evidence of vote-share effects; some evidence on supply-constraint items. (n = 4,028; Appendix F, p. A16)
- Figure 1c is a stylized two-dimension example, not an empirical estimate. Figure A7 describes a stylized case in which 50% more voters respond to positioning than the net-effect calculation shows. (Figure A7 illustration: 50% more responsive voters than the net estimate; pp. 7-9 (Figure 1c); Figure A7, p. A17)

## Limitations

- Conjoint presents candidates in a stylized format without established records, less-informed voters, or turnout dynamics (p. 23; Hall and Thompson 2018).
- The 29 issues do not exhaust the policy space, and different elite-middle positions could yield different results (p. 23).
- Partial equilibrium only. Nationalized elections could also contribute to modest returns to moderation (p. 24; Colao et al. forthcoming).
- Findings describe electoral incentives under current public opinion. Persuasion can shift opinion on transgender and undocumented-immigrant policies, which could change calculations (p. 24).
- Partisan cue-taking and thermostatic backlash suggest that simply adopting a position is unlikely to move opinion toward it, and could move it the other way (p. 24).
- Standard party positions were assumed to be perceived as party positions. Validation (Figure A1) shows large majorities identified them, but not for all issues in every subgroup.
- Candidate-level results (Appendix E) are exploratory. The same positions were signaled for all same-party candidates, which may understate moderate candidates' advantages (p. A13-A14).
- Hypothetical survey effects are not interpreted as precise magnitudes ('we do not ascribe much importance to the point estimates').
- Alternative interpretation (footnote 14): the mixed responses could reflect one-dimensional preferences with different issue cutlines for voters and elites.
- Future research directions: why parties take unpopular positions (Figures A11-A12 suggest primary voters are not entirely responsible), whether returns change as opinion evolves, and field experiments with real campaign communications (p. 24).

## Gaps and flags

- Terrier has no voter issue-position data. CUA measures consumer exposure to America's Credit Unions (ACU) issues, not policy positions, so it cannot tell a cross-pressured voter from a true centrist. The paper shows that this distinction decides the sign of the response.
- Terrier has no input for candidate positions. Support s is identical for every same-party candidate, so the app cannot show how a moderate or out-of-step candidate changes who is persuadable.
- The untapped-potential persuasion term has no message or issue dimension. It cannot say which issue to raise with which voter, which is the targeting lever the paper's heterogeneity implies.
- The midpoint-peaked persuasion term (1 - |2s - 1|) has never been validated against observed persuasion heterogeneity, whether from a canvass RCT or a survey.
- Melange's overall stance tier does not surface how spread out a member's per-topic tiers are, so a mixed Champion/Opponent record looks the same as a uniformly neutral one.
- Out-of-population citation: Paul's component positions list Broockman & Kalla 2026 under melange-influence-engine.persuadability-table. The paper measures voters' response to candidate positions, not whether legislators can be persuaded by lobbying. It cannot calibrate PERS or support the inverted-U, and the bearing is analogy only.
- Annotation conflation: Paul's position for terrier-canvass.untapped-potential describes heavily highlighted door-canvass protocol material (nonjudgmental exchange, perspective-getting, a 7-minute version). That material comes from Kalla & Broockman 2020, not this 2026 paper. This paper has no canvass protocol.
- The pooled AMCEs (Democrats +1.1 pp, Republicans +1.8 pp; N = 180,000 respondent x task x candidate x policy rows per party, from 6,000 respondents) span a known fault line: majority versus non-majority issues, and the respondent's own position.
