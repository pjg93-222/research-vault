# Influencing Connected Legislators

**Marco Battaglini, Eleonora Patacchini (2016).** *NBER Working Paper No. 22739 (October 2016; JEL D72, D78). Not peer-reviewed per the NBER disclaimer. Metadata hint confirmed: authors, year, and NBER w22739 are correct..* Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Battaglini and Patacchini model two opposed interest groups that bid for votes from legislators who care about money and about voting the same way as their peers. They prove the game has a unique equilibrium. When legislators are office-motivated and have log utility, each group pays each legislator in proportion to that legislator's Bonacich (Katz-Bonacich) centrality. When legislators also care about policy, the weights become a modified Bonacich centrality that depends on preferences and pivot probabilities, and it converges back to plain Bonacich in large chambers. The empirical part fits a spatial-autoregressive model of total PAC money to U.S. House members across the 2004-2012 cycles. Ties come from shared committees (N=2,128 member-Congress rows, phi=0.209, 0.217 after a control-function correction) and shared alma maters (N=1,166, phi=0.084). Degree, closeness and betweenness add little: betweenness is weakly significant (10 percent level) in the committee column, and R^2 is 0.148 in most Table 4 columns (0.149 in one), about the same as the model with no network terms. The authors chose not to build the network from cosponsorship, because cosponsorship is itself driven by money (fn. 30). This is an NBER working paper and has not been peer-reviewed.
> 
> The most direct bearing is on `fec/engine/kb_benchmark.py`, which cites this paper and implements its prediction as a diagnostic. Its design departs from the paper in three ways:
> - It runs Katz on the cosponsorship-based kernel `build_P`, the network the paper avoids because cosponsorship depends on money.
> - It pools the Senate with the House, while the paper covers the House only.
> - It sets attenuation at 0.85 of the convergence bound, while the paper's phi (about 0.08 to 0.25) is an SAR coefficient on a different scale, so the two are not directly comparable.
> 
> The stored results (Spearman rho 0.135 against CU money and 0.230 against bank plus financial-services money, n=533 across both chambers, file written 2026-08-18) are therefore unstratified, unverified, and about seven weeks stale. The paper's test of unweighted degree and betweenness on PAC money is only an analogy for the degree/betweenness InfluenceScore, which it did not test. It also points to a gap: in the allocator, money to one member reaches that member's network neighbours only after a discrete conversion.

## Study

**Question:** Do legislators' social ties affect how interest groups allocate campaign contributions? Specifically, how do interest groups allocate money across legislators connected by a social network, and do legislators' network positions (Bonacich centrality) predict PAC contributions in the U.S. House?

**Theory:** Two interest groups (one for policy A, one against) each have a budget and promise contingent payments to legislators, who vote after observing offers. Legislators value contributions (concave utility, log in the estimation), value conformity with their social network (weights G, so utility rises with peers voting the same way), and may care about the policy (policy motivation; office-motivated legislators have zero policy preference). Interest groups do not know legislators' preferences exactly (uniform noise with density Psi). Under Assumptions 1 (sufficient noise, Psi small) and 2 (I - beta*G invertible and positive), there is a unique pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (Props 1-2). With office-motivated legislators the equilibrium transfers maximize a weighted sum of legislators' monetary utilities with weights equal to Bonacich centralities (Prop 1, Sec 3.2.1). With policy-motivated legislators the weights are modified Bonacich centralities (eq. 15, Prop 2). As the number of legislators grows, or as policy preference weakens, the modified weights converge to simple Bonacich centralities (Corollary 1; Prop 3, Sec 3.2.2). The central empirical implication is that the network externality parameter (phi*) must be positive for social ties to matter.

**Data:** Unit: legislator-Congress observations for U.S. House members (stacked across Congresses). Sample periods: 109th-113th Congresses (election cycles 2004-2012). Each committee network has roughly 440 Representatives (including midterm replacements) and about 20 standing committees; the alumni network has over 400 nodes and thousands of links (fn. 11). Regression N: committee-network specification 2,128 observations; alumni-network specification 1,166 observations (more than 50% of congressmen linked by alma mater; Sec 4.2.1). Dependent variable: total PAC contributions to each candidate in $ millions, from FEC files aggregated by the Center for Responsive Politics, trimmed at 1st and 99th percentiles; range $9,519 to $7,178,406; total PAC spending $310 million (110th) to $453 million (112th) (Sec 4.2.2). Cosponsorship data for Table 1: all bills proposed in the House 109th-113th Congresses from THOMAS (244,519 dyads for alumni-cosponsorship; 242,735 dyads for committee-membership). Controls: margin of victory (FEC publications, supplemented by state agencies), per capita income by district (ACS one-year estimates), DW-NOMINATE first dimension (Nokken-Poole modified coordinates), committee chair, seniority, relevant powerful committee (Ways and Means, Energy and Commerce, Appropriations, Rules, Financial Services), joint committee (Economic, Taxation, Library, Printing), party (Republican=1), gender, top-10 university attendance (about 6% of congressmen), four election-cycle dummies. Committee and roster data: Clerk of the House, Stewart and Woon site, CQ roster for the 113th; alumni: Biographical Directory of Congress; ideology/party/gender: GovTrack.

**Method:** Theory: backward induction over a two-stage game (voting stage then influence stage); existence by Brouwer; uniqueness via Lemma 1 (unique voting equilibrium when office-motivated, or policy-motivated with sufficient uncertainty); equilibrium characterized as maximization of weighted monetary utilities (Props 1-2). Empirical: a spatial autoregressive (SAR) model, s = alpha*1 + phi* W s + beta'X + eps (eq. 23), where s is log-transformed contribution-related outcome (eq. 17-18 derive log utility FOC; eq. 20-22 derive the SAR form), estimated by maximum likelihood (Anselin 1988). Benchmarks: OLS without network effects, AIC comparisons, likelihood-ratio test of phi*=0 (chi-square, 1 df), and OLS with degree, betweenness and closeness centrality (Table 4). Robustness: party-weighted committee network, chairmanship-weighted committee network, alumni networks with graduation-period windows (4 and 2 years). Multiple interest groups (Sec 5.2), threshold objectives (Sec 5.3) and heterogeneous policies (Sec 5.4) are extensions that leave the SAR form unchanged up to scale.

**Identification:** Committee network: links are proportional to shared committees, which may be endogenous to unobserved legislator traits that also drive contributions. The authors use a control-function (Heckman-style) correction following Qu and Lee (2015): a first-stage linear probability dyadic model of committee sharing on characteristic distances (eq. 24), then the first-stage residual term is added to the outcome equation (eq. 25). Exclusion: dyad-specific variables in link formation are excluded from the outcome equation; Table 2 column (2) reports the correction. The assumption that the selection effect is the same for all politicians is stated in fn. 23. Alumni network: asserted exogenous by construction to the political process, since shared institutions predate election; no selection correction is applied. Validity of the alumni network is argued via Table 1 panel (a) (alumni links predict cosponsorship) and panel (b) (alumni links barely predict committee sharing, R^2 0.002-0.003). Cosponsorship is not used to build the network because it is endogenous to contributions (fn. 30).

**Scope:** Population: U.S. House of Representatives members serving in the 109th through 113th Congresses (election cycles 2004-2012), with PAC contributions from FEC/CRP data. Institution: Congress with House committee structure and PAC campaign finance. Era: 2004-2012 cycles. Theory applies to any network with a finite number of legislator types. The authors note that the theory is about monetary transfers and social networks in legislatures generally. Not studied: Senate, state legislatures, non-monetary resources, lobbyist behavior, and endogenous changes in network topology.

## Key findings

- Equilibrium exists and is unique in pure strategies under Assumption 1 (noise sufficiently large) and Assumption 2; with office-motivated legislators the optimal transfer allocation maximizes a weighted sum of monetary utilities with weights equal to Bonacich centralities. Under log utility the transfer is proportional to Bonacich centrality. Numbers: Assumption 1: Psi*(beta_max + ...) <= 1/2 (bound on the noise density Psi); Assumption 2: I - 2*Psi*G invertible and positive. Proportionality factor depends on the shadow cost of money.. (Sec 3.2.1, Prop 1 (p. 14); Sec 2, Assumptions 1-2 (p. 8, 10))
- With policy-motivated legislators the allocation weights become modified Bonacich centralities that depend on preferences and pivot probabilities; they converge to simple Bonacich centralities as the policy-preference weight goes to zero and as the number of legislators grows large. Numbers: Prop 3: convergence as n goes to infinity; the proof uses Lemma 3.2 (pivot probabilities and their derivatives go to zero).. (Sec 3.2.2 (eq. 15-16, Prop 2, p. 15); Corollary 1 (p. 15-16); Prop 3 (p. 16); proof in Sec 7.3 (p. 39-40))
- Committee-network estimate of the network externality parameter is positive and significant; with the control-function correction it is slightly larger. Numbers: MLE phi = 0.2088*** (SE 0.0697), N = 2,128; corrected phi = 0.2165*** (SE 0.0703); selection term psi = -0.1132*** (SE 0.0016).. (Sec 4.3, Table 2 columns (1)-(2), p. 24-25)
- Alumni-network estimate of the externality parameter is positive and significant. Numbers: MLE phi = 0.0837*** (SE 0.0262), N = 1,166.. (Sec 4.3, Table 2 column (3), p. 25)
- Network effects significantly improve model fit over OLS without network terms; the likelihood ratio test rejects phi = 0 in both networks. Numbers: Committee: AIC OLS 5686.323 vs MLE 5679.196; LR statistic 11.127. Alumni: AIC OLS 3201.537 vs MLE 3193.922; LR statistic 9.615. Table brackets show p = 0.003 (committee) and p = 0.001 (alumni), while the text says p-value equal to 0.000.. (Sec 4.3, Table 3, p. 26)
- Standard centrality measures (degree, closeness, betweenness) do not explain PAC contributions; the model with them fits about as well as the no-network baseline, though betweenness is weakly significant in the committee column. Numbers: Degree 0.0004 (SE 0.0003) in committee network, -0.0018 (SE 0.0053) in alumni network; Closeness 0.5902 (SE 0.4711) and 0.5903 (SE 0.4771); Betweenness -4.9e-05* (SE 2.9e-05) in committee network, -3.2e-05 (SE 5e-05) in alumni; R^2 = 0.148 in most Table 4 columns (0.149 in one).. (Sec 4.3, Table 4, p. 26-27)
- Results are robust to party-weighted and chairmanship-weighted committee networks and to alumni networks restricted to overlapping graduation periods. The party-weighted estimate is larger than baseline. Numbers: Party-weighted committee network: phi = 0.2486*** (SE 0.0656) MLE; 0.2588*** corrected. Chairmanship-weighted: 0.3575*** (SE 0.0866) and 0.2152*** (corrected). Alumni, 4-year window: 0.07700*** (SE 0.0290), N = 767. Alumni, 2-year window: 0.06694** (SE 0.0306), N = 597.. (Sec 5.1, Table 5, p. 27-28)
- Alumni links strongly predict cosponsorship but barely predict committee sharing, supporting the claim that the alumni network is a distinct channel from committee-based ties. Numbers: Alumni link on cosponsorship: 0.180*** (SE 0.008) bare, 0.069*** (SE 0.007) with controls; R^2 rises from 0.008 to 0.11. Alumni link on committee membership: 0.006 (SE 0.006) bare, 0.012* (SE 0.007) with controls; R^2 0.002-0.003. Authors state the alumni network explains less than 1% of committee formation versus about 11% of endorsement.. (Sec 4.2.1, Table 1, p. 21-22)
- Control coefficients have the signs the literature predicts: chairs and relevant-committee members get more PAC money; close races, ideological extremity and seniority are associated with lower or higher contributions as stated; Republicans receive more. Numbers: Chair 0.4006*** (SE 0.0967) committee; Margin of Victory -0.8972*** (SE 0.0885); DW_ideology -1.0774*** (SE 0.1241); Party (Republican) 0.1443** (SE 0.0573); Per capita income 0.0061** (SE 0.0025).. (Sec 4.3, Table 2 and Table 3, p. 25-26)

## Limitations

- Authors describe this as a first attempt to bring predictions to data; they note significant room for further analysis (Sec 6, p. 30-31).
- Theory covers only monetary transfers; non-monetary resources (expertise, contacts) are left for future work (Sec 6).
- Interest groups do not alter network topology in the model; linking that to Ballester, Calvo-Armengol and Zenou (2006) key-player analysis is left open (Sec 6).
- Committee network may be endogenous; the control-function correction is parametric and assumes the selection effect is identical across legislators (fn. 23); the selection term's sign suggests unobserved expertise drives both contributions and network links (Sec 4.3).
- Bootstrapped standard errors (1,000 replications) are used because no closed-form adjustment exists for the generated regressor (fn. 25).
- Alumni sample (N = 1,166) oversamples top-10 university graduates; a top-10 dummy is included, and top-10 attendance is about 6% of congressmen (Sec 4.2.2).
- Cosponsorship is not used as the network because it is itself an endogenous outcome (fn. 30).
- Policy-motivated case requires sufficient uncertainty (Assumption 3 / Lemma 1); the proofs for policy-motivated legislators are in the online appendix, not the text file.
- Modified Bonacich weights are laborious to compute for large networks and depend on preferences and the voting rule, not only topology (Sec 3.2.2, p. 15).
- Closed-form Bonacich relation depends on utility specification (fn. 3); logarithmic utility is assumed for the empirical mapping (Sec 4.1).
- Reviewer-level caveats from this extraction: the text layer garbles Greek symbols (phi*, beta, psi, Psi), so the exact formula defining the SAR parameter is reconstructed from surrounding text. The text states p = 0.000 for the LR tests but the table brackets show 0.003 and 0.001. The introduction says the outcome is business PAC contributions, while Sec 4.2.2 describes total PAC contributions. Table A.1 (variable definitions and summary statistics) is not in the text file.

## Gaps and flags

- No exogenous social-tie layer. Every diffusion and centrality kernel is built from cosponsorship, which the paper treats as endogenous to money (fn. 30). There is no shared-committee co-membership network and no alumni network, although the paper shows both carry a significant externality (phi 0.209 committee, 0.084 alumni).
- The KB attenuation (KB_DELTA 0.85 in kb_benchmark.py; attenuation_fraction 0.82 in the dashboard template) is set near the convergence bound, not estimated. Nothing estimates a network-externality parameter with a likelihood-ratio test against a no-network baseline, as the paper's SAR maximum-likelihood fit does.
- The benchmark is raw Spearman with no controls. The paper shows margin of victory, DW-NOMINATE, chair, party and seniority are large predictors of PAC money, so an uncontrolled rho mixes centrality with these covariates.
- kb_benchmark pools both chambers and is not stratified by chamber or party. The paper's evidence is House-only.
- In the joint optimizer, money spills over to peers only through discrete conversions, with no continuous network multiplier on the value of a dollar.
- data/network/kb_benchmark.json was last written 2026-08-18, despite a weekly-refresh step at fec/weekly_refresh.py:523. The member desk still shows kb_pct from this stale file.
- UNSTRATIFIED: kb_benchmark.json correlations (rho 0.135 vs CU money, 0.230 vs bank plus financial-services money, 0.247 vs optimizer allocation; n=533 House+Senate; written 2026-08-18; money window = whatever congestion.json held then, cycle not recorded) pool across chamber and party, both known fault lines. Quote them only with that label.
- UNVERIFIED (DE-062): no second path has reproduced the KB-versus-money rho values. They also come from the cosponsorship network, which this paper deliberately avoids as endogenous to contributions, so they cannot be presented as a test of the Battaglini-Patacchini equilibrium.
- The kb_benchmark.py docstring (lines 2-5) says groups allocate 'IN PROPORTION TO KATZ-BONACICH CENTRALITY'. The paper gives exact proportionality only for office-motivated legislators with log utility; otherwise the weights are modified Bonacich or monotone functions.

This is borderline and is not a confirmed violation.
