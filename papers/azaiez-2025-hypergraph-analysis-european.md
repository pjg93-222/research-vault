# A hypergraph analysis of the European Commission lobby network

**Amina Azaiez, Antoine Mandel (2025).** *Applied Network Science, 10:45 (received 8 April 2025, accepted 22 August 2025). Metadata hint confirmed..* [10.1007/s41109-025-00735-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41109-025-00735-6). Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Azaiez and Mandel model five years of disclosed European Commission meetings (Dec 2019 to Nov 2024) as a hypergraph. Each meeting is one hyperedge joining officials and stakeholders, giving 5,717 nodes and 18,561 meetings. The hypergraph is one connected component and splits into Commissioner-centred silos (modularity 0.700). Most meetings are one-on-one. The rare large meetings hold the network together: edge betweenness correlates 0.74 with meeting size.
> 
> Among stakeholders only, a size- and degree-preserving null model shows a rich club at every degree k. Plain null models are biased downward for an EC-member rich club in the full network, but hybrid null models test the full hypergraph and find stakeholder rich-club behaviour less salient yet still qualitatively distinguishable (p.4-5, p.15). A hypercore decomposition puts companies and trade associations in the core of the full network and NGOs in the core of the stakeholder-only network. In a standardized OLS over 1,178 companies (R2 0.398), hypercoreness rises with lobbying FTE (0.464) and assets (0.253). The authors say this is association only, with reverse causation possible.
> 
> The most portable result for Paul is methodological. Projecting meetings onto pairwise cliques inflates nodes that attend a few large meetings. Within each framework the two coreness measures correlate at 0.93 and 0.79, and cross-framework correlations are weaker (exact values in Fig. 6). Melange builds two clique-style projections of group events: cosponsor rosters, and staffer payroll containers. The cosponsor network already discounts big bills by 1/sqrt(C). The staffer co-tenure operator has an open owner ruling on container-size normalization, and the paper's inflation finding is direct evidence for that ruling. The vault records a Pearson r of 0.8694 between staffer degree and office size, but that was measured on the v5 generation before the 54 admin containers were excluded on 2026-09-07, so it is not a property of the current operator.
> 
> The paper also says centrality measures embeddedness, not influence, and that resources drive it. This fits the system's retirement of cosponsor centrality from the Influence pillar and D-025's wording rule. The paper's own Discussion slips into "access and influence" language, which must not carry over. The paper's edges come from a register that names both parties to each contact. US LD-2 filings do not, so this design cannot be reproduced with LDA data (D-021).

## Study

**Question:** Does a highly interconnected core of actors dominate the European Commission lobbying network (p.2)? What determines company centrality in that network (p.2, p.8), and do stakeholder categories (companies, trade associations, NGOs) differ in their core position (p.6-7)?

**Theory:** Network theory of power concentration: core-periphery and rich-club structure as evidence of concentrated access and influence (p.2, p.4, p.10). Higher-order (hypergraph) theory: meetings are modeled as simultaneous multi-actor interactions because coordination among more than two actors matters (p.3). Centrality is treated as a proxy for embeddedness, not directly for influence (p.10). Interpretive concepts used in the discussion: Laurens' "bureaucratic capital" for business (p.7); Beyers and De Bruycker on NGO coalition-building and Hanegraaff and Pritoni on coalitions as a sign of weakness (p.7-8); "aggressive lobbying" (Kone and Farnhill 2019) (p.9).

**Data:** Unit: a meeting (hyperedge) between EC officials (Commissioners, cabinet members, directors-general) and interest representatives. Node: an EC official or a stakeholder. Period: 1 Dec 2019 to 30 Nov 2024 (first von der Leyen Commission), from the EC meetings register (p.4, p.12). Full hypergraph H: |V| = 5,717 nodes and |E| = 18,561 hyperedges, including repeated multi-edges; one connected component (p.4). Stakeholder-only hypergraph Hstak: 43.32% of nodes in the largest component (p.4). Duplicate meetings merged with fuzzy partial-ratio >= 70, merging 460 pairs (p.12-13). Stakeholder attributes from the EU Transparency Register (data Jan 2017 to Jan 2024; 43 meeting identification codes not found; 34 duplicate names consolidated) (p.12-13). Firm financials and sector (NACE) from Bureau van Dijk Orbis, with a 93.9% match rate (p.13). Regression sample: companies, N = 1,178 in H and N = 702 in Hstak (p.8, Table 1).

**Method:** Hypergraph construction from meeting co-attendance. Global structure: degree shares by stakeholder category, modularity (Louvain-type clustering, SI 8.4), edge-size and betweenness analysis, simpliciality (Landry et al. 2024) and edge entropy. Core-periphery: generalized normalized rich-club coefficient for hypergraphs, rho(k) = phi(k)/phi_rand(k), with phi_rand from six hypergraph configuration null models (Chodrow 2020), sampled by Metropolis-Hastings edge-swap rewiring (p.14-15). Hypercore decomposition (k,m) and hypercoreness centrality R(i) (Mancastroppa 2023, p.16). Core(r) composition by category for r where Core(r) is 10% to 1% of nodes (p.6-7). Comparison of hypercoreness with coreness on clique-expansion graphs (Pearson correlations, p.16-17). Regression: OLS of company hypercoreness on log FTE lobbying staff, log assets, binary levels of interest (European, global, national), EU head-office dummy, and NACE sector dummies (baseline C Manufacturing); coefficients standardized; skewed variables log-transformed; categories grouped; outliers removed (p.8-9, p.17-18).

**Identification:** None. The OLS regression is cross-sectional and associational. The authors state the resource-centrality links "should not be read as one-way causal effects" and describe reverse causation (p.9). Robustness is checked through null models, multiedge versions and a regression on all stakeholder types (SI 8.6, p.10).

**Scope:** Population: meetings disclosed by high-level EC officials, and the registered interest representatives who attended them. Institution: the European Commission only. The Council, European Parliament and other EU bodies are excluded (p.11). Era: 1 Dec 2019 to 30 Nov 2024 (first von der Leyen Commission). Core-periphery claims apply to the disclosed meetings, not to lobbying overall (p.10-11).

## Key findings

- Global structure: the hypergraph is one connected component with heavy-tailed degrees; companies and groups account for 34.58% of stakeholder degree, trade and business associations 25.35%, NGOs 23.08%, all others 17.00%. (p.3 (Global structure of the hypergraph)) Numbers: |V| = 5,717; |E| = 18,561; 34.58% / 25.35% / 23.08% / 17.00%
- Meeting size and connectivity: most meetings are one-on-one, but large meetings are rare and key to connectivity. Edge betweenness correlates strongly with edge size. (p.4) Numbers: correlation 0.74 between edge betweenness and edge size
- Siloed organization: the full network has strong community structure centred on individual Commissioners, so departments rarely share meetings. (p.4 (SI Fig. 8, section 8.4)) Numbers: modularity 0.700
- Stakeholder-only network is less connected. Its largest component holds 43.32% of nodes, with many small components. Meetings are homogeneous by stakeholder type. Meetings are more nested than random chance. (p.4 (Fig. 1 on p.5)) Numbers: largest component 43.32%; three simpliciality scores higher in the data than in size- and degree-preserving random hypergraphs (values in SI Fig. 14)
- Rich-club among stakeholders: on the stakeholder-only hypergraph, rho(k) > 1 at all k under the stakeholder-only null models. The authors read this as persistent rich-club behaviour among stakeholders. (p.4 (Fig. 2a); methods p.15) Numbers: rho(k) > 1 for all k
- Full-network rich-club is not testable as stated: the full H lacks EC-only edges, which biases rho downward against null models that contain them. Under the hybrid null, the stakeholder rich-club is qualitatively present but less salient. (p.4-5 (Fig. 2b); methods p.14-15 (Fig. 5)) Numbers: rho < 1 for k > 10 under null models (1)-(2); hybrid null (3)-(4) quantitatively weaker
- Core composition, full network: companies' share in the high-hypercoreness core rises above their graph share, and trade associations stay above their graph share. NGOs' share falls. (p.6 (Fig. 3a)) Numbers: core size shown for Core(r) at 10% to 1% of nodes
- Core composition, stakeholder network: companies stay significant, NGOs' core share rises notably, and trade associations' share falls. NGOs are over-represented in the largest meetings and trade associations in the smallest. (p.7 (Fig. 3b); p.7-8 (Fig. 12 in SI)) Numbers: qualitative; no NGO or association share values given in the text
- Absence of research institutions: think tanks, research institutes and academic institutions are absent from the core of both networks. (p.8) Numbers: qualitative
- Regression (companies, standardized OLS): FTE lobbying staff and assets are the strongest predictors in both networks. EU head office is strongly significant. Global and European interest levels are positive. National level is not significant. (p.8-9 (Table 1)) Numbers: H (N = 1,178, R2 = 0.398): FTE 0.464***, assets 0.253***, EU HQ 0.097***, global 0.113***, European 0.067*, national -0.041 (ns). Hstak (N = 702, R2 = 0.298): FTE 0.395***, assets 0.157***, EU HQ 0.197***, global 0.080*, European 0.009 (ns), national -0.018 (ns)
- Sector deviations from manufacturing: mining, energy and ICT companies are more central in the full network. Transport and finance are less central in the stakeholder-only network. (p.9-10 (Table 1)) Numbers: H: B 0.064**, D 0.057*, J 0.055*. Hstak: H -0.087*, K -0.101**
- Hypercoreness versus clique coreness: the two clique-based measures correlate at r = 0.93 and the two hypergraph measures at r = 0.79. Cross-framework correlations are weaker. Clique projection inflates nodes that attend a few large meetings. (p.16-17 (Fig. 6, Fig. 7)) Numbers: r = 0.93 (clique pair); r = 0.79 (hypergraph pair)
- Authors' policy reading: concentration of access among business actors, limited civil-society access to economic issues, and resource-driven centrality suggest a consultation process that is potentially biased and unequal. They also flag compartmentalization across departments. (p.10-11 (Discussion)) Numbers: none

## Limitations

- Centrality is not influence. The authors say network centrality does not directly measure influence, and cite prior work that links central positions to influence (p.10).
- Cross-sectional OLS shows associations only; resources and centrality may cause each other (p.9).
- Full-network rich-club cannot be tested against EC-only edges, because H has none (p.14).
- Results depend on multi-edge treatment: with repeated meetings the core fragments at higher orders, so the analysis uses versions without multi-edges (p.5-6).
- Duplicate detection uses a fuzzy-match threshold of 70, chosen by manual inspection of 60 to 100 pairs (p.12-13).
- Register linkage is imperfect: 43 identification codes were not found, and the register is used only for the most recent entry (p.12-13). Orbis match rate is 93.9%, with manual checks for lower-confidence matches (p.13).
- Scope is the EC only, excluding the Council and Parliament (p.11).
- NGO coalition interpretation is uncertain. Meeting-initiation dynamics would be needed to establish the mechanism (p.8).
- The regression covers companies only; NGOs and associations are analysed descriptively (p.8, p.10).
- The authors say findings on the representativeness of large firms (0.02% of EU businesses, 38% of workforce) raise questions but are not tested (p.9).

## Gaps and flags

- No component represents group events as hyperedges. Cosponsor rosters, payroll containers and interview episodes are all clique- or star-projected, so no hypergraph-native core or centrality is available for comparison.
- The staffer co-tenure operator still has no container-size treatment (OPEN ruling at build_staffer_social_network.py:222-234), even though the sidecar exposure index already divides by headcount. The paper's clique-inflation result bears directly on that ruling.
- Concentration and core claims in the sector diagnostics use an ad hoc 3x-hub rule, with no degree- and size-preserving null model. The system cannot say whether a sector's rich-club or core structure exceeds what its degree sequence implies.
- No core-periphery or k-core decomposition exists for any Melange graph. Core composition by actor category (party, chamber, committee, staffer role), as the paper does for stakeholder types, is not computed.
- Multi-edge treatment (repeated co-sponsorship or repeated co-tenure versus binarized ties) is not reported as a sensitivity axis. The paper shows its core fragments at higher orders when repeated meetings are kept.
- There is no US contact register comparable to the EC meetings register, so a lobbyist-to-office contact network cannot be built from disclosure data (D-021 holds).
- D-025 wording conflict: Paul highlighted the Discussion sentence (p.10) that core-periphery structures show a concentration of 'access and influence'. If imported, that reading would make graph ranks or core membership into access or influence claims, which D-025 bars. The paper itself says centrality does not directly measure influence (p.10) and that the resource links are not causal (p.9).
