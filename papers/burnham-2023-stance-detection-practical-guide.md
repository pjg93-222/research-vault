# Stance Detection: A Practical Guide to Classifying Political Beliefs in Text

**Michael Burnham (2023).** *arXiv preprint arXiv:2305.01723v2 (Nov 2023), Penn State. The PSRM 2025 venue hint is not supported by the text.* Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Burnham's methods guide treats stance as textual entailment: a document's stance is how its author would answer a stated proposition. Stance is kept separate from sentiment. The guide compares three ways to classify it on about 2,135 human-coded Trump-approval tweets and newsletter sentences, scored by MCC. Supervised models: bag-of-words gets 0.49 to 0.51 and transformers 0.60 to 0.68. Zero-shot NLI: DeBERTaV3 Base gets 0.40 and Large 0.65. In-context LLMs: GPT-4 gets 0.78, or 0.80 with logit bias, but costs $12.52 per run and cannot be archived. The main practical finding is about context. Human coders disagree more when the target is not named (34.0% versus 19.5%), and NLI degrades most in that case. When outside knowledge is needed, supervised or in-context classifiers do better. The guide recommends NLI as the reproducible default. LLMs are fine for expanding training data or for validation, but not as the main source of labels. The arXiv text is v2. An online check confirms the published version in PSRM 13 (2025), 611-628, online September 2024. This bears directly on Paul's CU press-stance stack, which runs Burnham's own Political DEBATE base model (a later, politics-trained NLI model) as its main reader. The system's own history repeats the paper's context finding. Zero-shot DEBATE missed implicit anti-CU stances (oppose F1 0.08), and fine-tuning on 552 labels fixed this. The paper also highlights three things the system has not settled: model size (base versus large), labels that fall back to Claude pre-labels, and the lack of an MCC or hypothesis-wording check. All system-side figures are the project's own reports, measured on small held-out sets (n = 87 or 156). They are UNVERIFIED by a second path.

## Study

**Question:** How should political scientists define and operationalize stance detection (the position a document expresses toward a proposition), and which classification approach (supervised, NLI zero-shot, or in-context generative LLM) should they choose given document context, compute, reproducibility and human labor constraints? A secondary aim is to show that NLI and in-context classifiers can replicate a supervised-classifier finding on COVID-19 mitigation stance (Block Jr et al. 2022).

**Theory:** Stance is an individual's attitudes, feelings, judgments or commitment toward a proposition (Biber and Finegan 1988), defined here as how an individual would answer a proposition (Definition 1). Sentiment is positive or negative emotional valence and is treated as a separate dimension (Definition 2). Stance detection is recast as textual entailment (Dagan et al. 2005; AlDayel and Magdy 2021): text T entails stance S when a human reading T with context C would infer that T supports S (Definition 4, pp.3-4). Context (information in the document plus the classifier's knowledge) governs ambiguity and therefore accuracy (Sections 2.2-2.3, pp.3-5).

**Data:** Trump-approval test set: public tweets (n = 1,135 per text on p.5; Table 6 Twitter sources sum to 1,134, a small internal mismatch) plus 1,000 random sentences containing 'Trump' from 117th Congress congressional e-newsletters (DC Inbox; Cormack 2017). Human coded twice with third-annotator adjudication. Total test set about 2,135 short documents (p.17). Table 6 (p.32): MTSD 398, ConStance 98, KE-MLM 380, SemEval2016 97, Titling 161, Newsletters 1,000. Replication data (Section 5, pp.21-23): 862,923 COVID-19 tweets from 23,476 users, 1 Sep 2020 to 28 Feb 2021; user ideology from tweetscores (Barbera 2015); 2,000 hand-labeled training tweets with coding rules for non-compliant tweets; 300 randomly sampled labeled tweets used as the NLI validation sample.

**Method:** Three classifier families compared. (1) Supervised: bag-of-words logistic regression, random forest and SVM (grid search) versus RoBERTa, BERTweet, PoliBERTweet and DeBERTaV3 base (Bayesian hyperparameter sweep, 10 runs each; MCC primary metric; pp.11-12). (2) NLI zero-shot: DeBERTaV3 Large NLI model; each tweet paired with hypothesis templates (single versus multi-hypothesis; keyword matching of hypotheses to documents; 9 synonymous hypotheses for sensitivity; pp.14-15). (3) In-context: GPT-4, GPT-3.5 Turbo and Zephyr 7B with system and user prompts, temperature 0, zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought and logit bias variants (Table 5, pp.19-20). Replication: negative binomial regression of count of threat-minimizing tweets on ideology, county COVID death rate, their interaction, county demographics, political lean and state fixed effects, following Block Jr et al. (2022) (pp.21-22).

**Identification:** No causal identification strategy. The replication is an observational negative binomial regression with county and state controls and state fixed effects; the paper compares coefficient direction and size across three label sources (original supervised labels, two NLI hypothesis sets) rather than claiming causal effects.

**Scope:** English-language US political text: Trump approval in tweets and congressional newsletters (117th Congress newsletters; tweets from several stance projects) and COVID-19 mitigation stance on Twitter (Sep 2020 to Feb 2021). Guidance is presented for political text in general, but the evidence rests on one target (Trump), one replication case, and models available as of 2023-2024.

## Key findings

- Stance is distinct from sentiment and should be measured independently; sentiment can inform intensity but does not replace stance (Section 2.1, pp.2-3).
- Ambiguity is higher when the document does not mention Trump: 34.0% of non-Trump documents versus 19.5% of Trump-mentioning documents had conflicting human codes (Section 3.3, p.10).
- NLI classifiers degrade most when Trump is not mentioned; supervised and in-context classifiers adapt better to low-context documents (Figure 2, p.10).
- Supervised results (Table 3, p.12, Twitter only): logistic regression MCC 0.51, random forest 0.49, SVM 0.49, RoBERTa 0.60, BERTweet 0.63, PoliBERTweet 0.68 (best); DeBERTaV3 base without domain adaptation 0.63.
- Laurer et al. (2024), cited on p.12: 500 training examples let language models consistently beat other algorithms trained on several thousand.
- NLI results (Table 4, p.14): DeBERTaV3 Small MCC 0.15, Base 0.40, Large 0.65 (83% accuracy); only the Large model is described as comparable to supervised classifiers and human-level.
- Test-set cost and performance (Table 5, pp.19-20; cost text p.17): GPT-4 standard prompt MCC 0.78 at $12.52 for 2,135 documents; GPT-3.5 Turbo MCC 0.58 at $0.68; Zephyr 7B MCC 0.59 with no API cost but about 2.5 min on a consumer GPU.
- Logit bias raised GPT-4 MCC to 0.80 and GPT-3.5 to 0.63 at no extra cost (Table 5). Chain-of-thought gave no perceptible accuracy benefit for GPT-4 and tripled its cost ($38.62 versus $12.52), and Zephyr 7B failed template compliance (p.19).
- Scaling cost: classifying 1,000,000 tweets with the cheapest in-context approach would cost $6,260 (p.17), so in-context models are recommended mainly for small sets, training data expansion or validation.
- Hypothesis sensitivity: nine synonymous NLI hypotheses gave average MCC 0.64, range 0.62 to 0.66 (p.15).
- Replication (Section 5.2, pp.22-23): NLI hypothesis set 1 MCC 0.70 (89% accuracy), set 2 MCC 0.75 (91%), original supervised classifier MCC 0.66 (86%) on 300 labeled tweets; NLI and supervised labels agree at MCC 0.84, and regression direction and size are consistent across the three models (Figure 3, p.22).
- Threat-minimizing tweets concentrate at the conservative pole in all three label sets (Figure 4, p.23); average author ideology -0.66 overall, and disagreement samples average -0.11 (set 1) and -0.07 (set 2).
- Author's conclusion: when document information is sufficient, NLI classifiers reach performance comparable to supervised classifiers without training; when outside context is needed, supervised or in-context classifiers are better (Section 6, pp.23-24).

## Gaps and flags

- No MCC anywhere in the stance stack. Reports use accuracy, macro-F1 and Cohen's kappa (train_stance_domain.py:61-64). With class imbalance (support 2,323 / neutral 1,151 / oppose 224 in production), MCC with bootstrapped CIs is the metric the paper argues for.
- Hypothesis wording is fixed and was never sensitivity-tested (stage5_debate.py:26-31). The paper's 9-synonym test gave an MCC range of 0.62-0.66, which is a cheap check to run on the 87-row test.
- Fine-tuned DEBATE large has never been scored against base on the same member-grouped human-gold test. The paper's base-to-large zero-shot gap is 0.40 to 0.65 MCC.
