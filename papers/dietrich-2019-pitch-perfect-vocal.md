# Pitch Perfect: Vocal Pitch and the Emotional Intensity of Congressional Speech

**Bryce J. Dietrich (University of Iowa), Matthew Hayes (Rice University), Diana Z. O'Brien (Rice University) (2019).** *American Political Science Review 113(4): 941-962 (received Oct 21, 2017; accepted July 1, 2019; published online Aug 23, 2019).* [10.1017/S0003055419000467](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055419000467) Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Dietrich, Hayes and O'Brien use 74,158 US House floor speeches from 2009 to 2014, each at least 50 words, taken from Clerk HouseLive audio (the Table 1 models use 74,151 speeches and 619 members). They measure each speech's mean vocal pitch (F0, extracted with Praat) and z-score it against the speaker's own baseline. They argue this is a measure of arousal that is difficult for speakers to control. It tracks intensity, not valence (footnote 1). Female members speak about 0.09 to 0.11 SD above their baseline when they mention women (interaction terms), while male members change by 0.02 SD, which is not significant (Table 1). Party-by-owned-topic interaction terms range from 0.288 to 0.380 SD across four models (Table 4). On days when many women speak with high pitch about women, male members mention women more (Model 5.2 only), raise their own pitch on the topic (three-way interaction, Table 6), and co-vote more with the women speakers (Tables 5-7). These response results are observational, and the authors say they are not causal (around pp. 956-957).

## Study
**Question.** Do small within-speaker deviations in vocal pitch (mean F0 relative to a speaker's own baseline) measure legislators' emotional intensity on an issue? Test case: do female House members speak with higher-than-baseline pitch when referencing women, compared with (a) male MCs on the same topic and (b) their own speech on other topics? Secondary questions: does pitch track issue commitments (women's interest-group scores; party-owned issues), and do emotionally intense female speeches about women relate to male MCs' subsequent speech and voting (p. 941-942, 944, 951)?

**Theory.** Emotional arousal tightens the vocal folds and raises pitch (Puts, Gaulin and Verdolini 2006; Owren and Bachorowski 2007; Mauss and Robinson 2009) (p. 942). Pitch is a measure of arousal/intensity, not valence (fn 1). Because pitch is hard to control, it sits low on the "leakage hierarchy" (Zuckerman and Driver 1985; Ekman et al. 1991; Kraut 1978), so it is a less strategic signal than topic choice, word choice or speech length (p. 942-944). Descriptive representation theory: female legislators have experience-based commitment to women's concerns (Reingold 1992; Mansbridge 1999), so their intensity should rise when speaking about women (p. 944-945). Signalling extension: when many women speak intensely about women, male colleagues attend and respond (Boyd, Epstein and Martin 2010; Mendelberg, Karpowitz and Oliphant 2014), with a possible backlash alternative (Kathlene 1994; Krook 2015) (p. 951, 957). Partisan extension: parties own issues and partisans speak with more intensity on owned issues (Petrocik 1996; Petrocik, Benoit and Hansen 2003) (p. 949-951).

**Data.** Source: HouseLive (Office of the Clerk) video, audio and closed-caption text for all US House floor debates, 6,432 hours from 863 debates, 2009-01-06 to 2014-08-04 (p. 944). Unit of analysis: individual floor speech of at least 50 words; N = 74,158 speeches (p. 944). Speaker sex from GovTrack; race, seniority, DW-NOMINATE and party from GovTrack/Voteview; committee chair from Stewart and Woon (2016) (p. 946). Table 1 model N: 74,151 speeches and 619 MCs (Models 1.1-1.2); 71,198 speeches and 613 MCs (Models 1.3-1.4). Female-speech counts: 13,484 speeches by women (2,403 mention women, 17.82%); 60,667 by men (5,507 mention women, 9.08%) (p. 946). Women's own speech, male-on-female-day analysis: 50,235 speeches, 509 men (Table 5). Male pitch analysis: 49,914 speeches, 506 men (Table 6). Voting analysis: 21,920 observations, 485 men (Table 7). Interest groups: 24 women's groups via Project Vote Smart, averaged per MC (p. 948). Women's bills: Volden, Wiseman and Wittmer (2018); CQ "Bills to Watch" count (p. 952). Closed captions reported ~95% accurate by the vendor; authors' transcription of 100 speeches matched (fn 11).

**Method.** Pitch: mean fundamental frequency per speech extracted with Praat (Boersma 1993 algorithm; male floor 75 Hz, ceiling 300 Hz; female range 100-500 Hz), then z-scored within speaker so units are SD above/below each MC's baseline (p. 945-946; alternative Praat windows in SI S22-S30). Topic: binary indicator for any term in Pearson and Dancey (2011b) women dictionary; alternatives are word-share and a 30-topic structural topic model (STM) (p. 945-946). Models: multilevel logit for "Women mentioned" (Models 1.1-1.2, 5.1-5.2) and multilevel linear regression for standardized pitch with random intercepts per MC (Models 1.3-1.4, 4.x, 6.x); outcome models for Table 5, 6, 7 are multilevel logit/linear with day-level Female Speeches, Female Pitch and interactions (p. 946-956). Descriptive comparisons: Welch-type t-tests across speakers and groups (Table 2; Table 3 top-25 vs bottom-25 female MCs by pitch difference; t = 2.59, df = 48). Partisan issue test: Table 4 interaction of party with party-owned STM topics. Male voting outcome: proportion of votes cast with female speakers, standardized against each male MC's prior co-voting rate (p. 955-956). Predicted values from Models 5.2, 6.1 and 7.2 at female pitch +/-2 SD (p. 953-956).

**Identification.** None causal. The paper states the design is observational and that "legislators' speeches on women simply cannot be randomly assigned" (p. 957). Within-speaker z-scoring removes stable speaker-level pitch differences (including sex differences in baseline pitch), random intercepts absorb unobserved MC heterogeneity, and a rich control set is used (race, seniority, chair, DW-NOMINATE, party, one-minute speech, duration, election year). Placebo tests and dyadic models are in SI S55-S62. Male-response models restrict to men who spoke on the same legislative day as women's speeches and add agenda controls (Women's bills, CQ bills). The volume-times-pitch interaction is the key test. Robustness: results unchanged under alternative DV operationalizations (word share, STM) (p. 946), re-estimated without observations beyond +/-2 SD (fn 28, p. 953), and different Praat pitch windows (p. 945-946).

**Scope.** Population: all US House floor speeches of at least 50 words during the 111th-113th Congresses (Jan 2009 to Aug 2014), roughly 77-81 women in the House in that span (p. 956). Institution: US House floor only (not the Senate, committees, or state legislatures, which the authors name as future audio sources, p. 958). Era: 2009-2014. Speeches are natural floor speech, not acted or induced emotion (p. 944). The authors explicitly do not claim general causal effects of women's speech on male behavior (p. 957).

## Limitations
- Observational design; the authors say they do not claim causal effects of female MCs' intense speech on male behavior, and that alternative explanations cannot be ruled out (p. 957).
- Male response is not zero at low female participation: at few female speeches the effect is absent or negative, so the signalling story is conditional on volume (p. 957).
- Backlash is examined only with one voting measure and cannot be ruled out in other outcomes; votes may come before or after speeches because the data have no time stamps (fn 31, p. 955-956).
- Pitch-based intensity is an arousal measure, not valence; a single pitch number cannot say whether a speaker is enthusiastic or angry (fn 1, p. 942).
- Mean F0 per speech is a coarse measure; other factors (question intonation, duration) also move mean pitch, though controls are included (fn 8, p. 945; p. 958-959).
- Women-topic is a coarse dictionary indicator; alternatives used as robustness checks (p. 945-946).
- Effect size is small: 0.09 SD for female MCs on women's topics, which the authors concede is 'slight' and likely below conscious control (p. 947).
- Confidence and expertise could explain some intensity; the paper does not separate them, and male MCs with the most women's bills did not show the same pattern (fn 19, p. 948).
- Republican-owned and Republican-women effects are weaker or not significant in some specifications, partly due to small N of Republican women (p. 948-949; fn 17; t = 0.14 with df = 7 for Republican women in Table 3 comparison).
- Male MCs' floor attendance is unobserved; the sample uses all male MCs who spoke the same day as a proxy for exposure (fn 24, p. 953).
- Closed-caption text is ~95% accurate per the vendor (fn 11, p. 945).
- Table 3 group averages do not extract cleanly from the PDF text; only the reported t-tests are used here.
- Single chamber (US House) and single era (2009-2014); generalization to other chambers or periods is untested (p. 958-959).

## Gaps and flags
- No comparison group. The paper's evidence is a cross-speaker contrast (female vs male, times topic), but topic_emotional_intensity reports one member's topic mean with nothing to compare it against (b5_audio_descriptors_candidate.py:237-249).
- Power. The floors are 5 units per member, 2 topics and 2 units per topic (audio_rules.py:20-22). At those floors the SE of a topic mean is roughly 0.45-0.7 SD, while the paper's effects are 0.09-0.38 SD at an average of about 120 speeches per member (not a stated requirement). The 0.45-0.7 SD figure is arithmetic assuming independent units, and no minimum detectable effect was computed.
- The sign is fixed by construction when a member has 2 topics. The brain records a within-member permutation-null fix (sign_informative, commit 4193f0140 on the unmerged speech-recognition branch), but the live b5_audio_descriptors_candidate.py has no null or sign_informative field. The brain's account was not checked here.
- No controls for question intonation, speech duration or speech type, and no venue term, in either the per-member z or the WJ subject_effects (build_descriptive_speech.py:2491-2531). The paper treats these as confounds of mean F0.
- The floor-speech audio does not use Clerk HouseLive. The system instead verifies identity from captions with an LLM. The paper does not say the chamber attributes each speech to a member, so whether Clerk floor video can serve as an identity source is an open check.
- No issue-ownership check. The paper's party-owned-topic pitch effect (0.29-0.38 SD) gives a ready external sanity check for any topic-pitch descriptor, and the system has not run it.
- The desk shows per-member topic pitch shifts from as few as 2 WJ reactions (build_descriptive_speech.py:2521; make_member_desk_00.tpl:1280). The window and count are shown, but at that n the values are noise relative to the paper's effect sizes. Treat them as UNVERIFIED descriptive output, never as an intensity finding.
- The brain says topic_emotional_intensity was fixed with a permutation null, but the live candidate file lacks it. Whether this is a committed-vs-live discrepancy was not checked against the branch.
- The paper's male-response results (Tables 5-7) are observational and gender-specific (authors, around pp. 956-957). Any import into cascade or threshold constants would be unvalidated. Do not describe them as influence probabilities.
- No conflict with the rejected SNA proposals, D-021 or D-025. The paper does not touch cosponsorship edges, edge normalization or LDA.
