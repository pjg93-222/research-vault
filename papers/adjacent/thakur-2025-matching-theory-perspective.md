# Matching theory and Senate committee assignment

**Ashutosh Thakur (2025).** *Political Science Research and Methods, 1-25 (Original Article; open access)*. [https://doi.org/10.1017/psrm.2025.10043](https://doi.org/10.1017/psrm.2025.10043). Theme: [[themes/adjacent|Adjacent]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Thakur (PSRM 2025) applies matching theory to US Senate committee assignment. He argues that the two parties fill seats through different mechanisms. Senate Republicans use a seniority-ordered Serial Dictatorship, which the paper says seems to be run in practice as TTC/YRMH-IGYT, a strategyproof, individually rational and Pareto-efficient mechanism (a pure seniority Serial Dictatorship is strategyproof but not individually rational). Senate Democrats use something close to the Boston mechanism, with tie-breaking votes by the committee-on-committees (COC) that the paper, citing a CRS source, describes as secret. Under the Boston mechanism, members have reason to misreport their preferences.
> 
> The evidence is explicitly suggestive.
> - Freshman Democrats list Prestige committees as first choice 8% less often than non-freshmen (p = 0.15, not significant at conventional levels; 81st-103rd Congress request data, 1947-1995), which is consistent with hedging. Republicans show no such gap (p = 0.80, first choices only, from 91st and 103rd Congress archive data, because 76 percent of non-freshman Republicans rank one choice).
> - Mean seniority at appointment differs across parties on some committees (e.g., Environment & Public Works: R 3.80, N=30, vs D 1.00, N=14; t=3.05). The author reads this as possible COC discipline on the Democratic side, assuming equal true-preference distributions across parties.
> - A reduced-form structural model for Republicans only (426 new assignments, 104th-114th) finds high common value and small idiosyncratic variance for Finance and Appropriations. Constituency committees such as Small Business have low common value and depend on constituency particulars.
> - Seniority on a committee mixes revealed value with queuing benefits.
> 
> For Paul's system, the paper speaks mainly to the committee/Influence pillar. That pillar prices seats by CU-relevance tier, with fixed chair and ranking multipliers. It does not distinguish chamber or party. The paper suggests that who holds a seat is partly an artifact of party-specific mechanisms, seniority queues and COC discipline. This bears on the chicken-or-egg question of partisanship and committee selection. It also suggests committee office is not simply a costly, member-chosen signal, though the paper does not test this.
> 
> Scope limits: Senate only (about 100 senators), data from 1947-2017, the House not studied, and no causal identification. None of this validates the tier weights. Any test of the committee pillar against outcomes should be stratified by party and chamber, or labelled UNSTRATIFIED.

## Study

**Question.** How do the US Senate parties actually assign members to committees, i.e., what matching mechanism does each party use to turn rank-ordered committee requests into assignments, and what do those mechanisms imply for strategic reporting, party discipline by the committee-on-committees (COC), incumbents' property rights, and the validity of canonical theories of legislative organization (Distributive, Informational, Cartel-Agenda)? (Sec. 1, pp. 2-3.)

**Theory.** Matching theory (school-choice and house-allocation mechanisms: Serial Dictatorship, Boston mechanism, Top Trading Cycles / YRMH-IGYT, with existing-tenant and property-rights norms). Argues Senate Republicans use a strategyproof seniority-based Serial Dictatorship (which the paper says seems to be run as TTC/YRMH-IGYT in practice) and Senate Democrats use an approximation of the non-strategyproof Boston mechanism with COC tie-breaking. Revisits Distributive (Shepsle-Weingast), Informational (Gilligan-Krehbiel, Krehbiel) and Cartel-Agenda (Cox-McCubbins) theories (Sec. 2 and 4, pp. 3-5 and 16-18). Motivational theories (Mayhew reelection, Fenno power and policy, Ferejohn pork and monetary gain) are background (Sec. 2, p. 4).

**Data.** Three sources. (1) Frisch and Kelly (2006) Senate Democrat committee request data, 81st-103rd Congresses (1947-1995); unit is a Democratic senator's ranked request. (2) Stewart and Woon (2005) Senate committee assignments, 103rd-114th Congresses (1993-2017); unit is a senator-committee assignment. (3) Novel archival dataset of Senate Republican committee requests from the Robert and Elizabeth Dole Archive (University of Kansas) for the 91st and 103rd Congresses (approx. 1969-1971 and 1993-1995); the paper does not report the number of Republican request records. Table A5 uses 104th-114th assignments with per-committee Ns (e.g., Small Business R=31, D=18; Environment R=30, D=14; Rules R=18, D=15). The structural section (Sec. 5.3) uses 426 new Senate committee assignments across 11 sessions (104th-114th), 25 committees and 97 distinct senators; each committee has 7-36 new assignments (fn. 22, p. 20). Ns for Figure 1 and Figure A1 are not reported. Figure A1 notes 76% of Republican non-freshmen rank only one choice, so only first choices are compared.

**Method.** Primarily theoretical and descriptive. (a) Institutional reconstruction of the two procedures from CRS reports (Schneider 2003, 2006, 2014) and Shepsle (1978), with archival correspondence exhibits (Tables 1-2). (b) Mechanism-design analysis: static properties of SD, SD with squatting rights, SD with waiting list, TTC/YRMH-IGYT, and the Boston mechanism (Table 3, citing Abdulkadiroglu-Sonmez 1999, 2003; Kojima-Unver 2014). (c) A two-committee partial-equilibrium model of a single Democrat's rank-order choice against a stochastic environment with an existing-tenant guarantee u(x)>0, yielding Proposition 1 (higher guaranteed payoff implies weakly higher rank positions, p. 15; proof in Online Appendix A.2). (d) Descriptive comparisons: share of rank-1 requests by committee type, freshman vs non-freshman Democrats (Figure 1, p-values); Republican freshman vs non-freshman (Figure A1); average chamber seniority at request (Table A4); cross-party difference in means of seniority at appointment with t-stats and difference in SDs (Table A5); revealed-preference ranking of committee value using average seniority with the 1-3 most senior members excluded (Table A6). (e) Reduced-form structural model u_ic = delta_c + eps_i with eps_i mean 0 and variance sigma_eps^2, identifying committee common value and idiosyncratic variance from the mean and variance of chamber seniority at appointment (and minimum-seniority moments mu_min, sigma_min) for Republicans only (Tables A7-A8), justified by the seniority-based TTC/YRMH-IGYT mechanism. Cross-party comparisons assume the true preference distributions of Republicans and Democrats are the same (p. 19).

**Identification.** No causal identification strategy. The paper calls its evidence "suggestive" (Sec. 5.1, p. 18). Identification of strategic behavior rests on mechanism-design logic: Republicans' SD/TTC mechanism is strategyproof, so reported requests are treated as true preferences (revealed preference), while Democrats' Boston mechanism is not, so requests are treated as strategic and predicted to vary by incumbency. Freshman vs non-freshman contrasts are the within-party comparison (Prediction 3a). Cross-party contrasts of seniority at appointment (Predictions 2b, 2c) are used to infer non-seniority tie-breaking or party discipline by the Democratic COC, under the stated assumption of equal true-preference distributions. Structural estimates of delta_c and sigma_eps are identified only if seniority at appointment reflects the TTC/YRMH-IGYT allocation and the additive utility form holds. Prediction 4 (committee value from seniority, excluding high-seniority members to net out queuing benefits) is argued by revealed preference.

**Scope.** Population: US Senate party committee assignments (Republican and Democratic caucuses). Institution: Senate standing committees, categorized as Constituency, Policy, Prestige and Admin committees (Table A3) and as A/B/C committees (Table A1). Era: Democrat requests 1947-1995; Republican archival requests 91st and 103rd Congresses; assignments 1993-2017 (103rd-114th), with the structural analysis on 104th-114th. Excludes the House as a test case (discussed as background), and post-election seat-ratio negotiations (fn. 9).

## Key findings

- Senate Republicans assign committees via a seniority-based Serial Dictatorship that seems to be run in practice as TTC/YRMH-IGYT (Sec. 3.5.1); TTC/YRMH-IGYT is strategyproof, individually rational and Pareto efficient, while a pure seniority SD is strategyproof but not individually rational. Senate Democrats use an approximation of the Boston mechanism with COC tie-breaking votes, which is not strategyproof. The two parties therefore use structurally different mechanisms. Numbers: Table 3: Democrat Boston mechanism is not strategyproof; Republican SD with seniority is strategyproof but not IR; SD with squatting rights is strategyproof but neither IR nor PE; SD with waiting list is strategyproof and IR but not PE; TTC/YRMH-IGYT is strategyproof, IR and PE.. (Abstract p. 1; Introduction pp. 2-3; Sec. 3.1-3.3 pp. 7-9; Table 3 p. 14; Sec. 3.5.1 pp. 12-14)
- Prediction 1 (Republicans request truthfully, Democrats strategically) is supported only by archival anecdote and a CRS quote; the paper says it lacks enough data to test Prediction 1 directly. Numbers: No test statistic. Paper states it cannot test Predictions 1, 2(a) and 3(b) (p. 18).. (Sec. 3.3 pp. 8-9 (Exhibit #4, Gregg letter 1992); Sec. 5.1 p. 18)
- Freshman Senate Democrats request fewer popular, highly sought-after Prestige committees than non-freshmen, consistent with hedging against the Boston mechanism when they lack an existing-assignment guarantee, though the gap is not significant at conventional levels. Numbers: 8% fewer Prestige first-choice requests for freshmen vs non-freshmen, p = 0.15 (Frisch-Kelly 1947-1995 data). Average chamber seniority at request: Prestige 3.88 yrs, Policy 2.89 yrs, Constituency 2.58 yrs.. (Sec. 5.2.1 p. 19; Figure 1 p. 18; Table A4 p. 23)
- No comparable substitution away from Prestige committees appears among Republican freshmen vs non-freshmen, consistent with strategyproofness of the Republican mechanism. Numbers: p = 0.80 for the difference (Dole Archives, 91st and 103rd Congresses). First-choice comparison only, because 76% of Republican non-freshmen rank only one choice.. (Sec. 5.2.1 p. 19; Figure A1 p. 25)
- Cross-party differences in seniority at appointment (Predictions 2b, 2c) point to possible party discipline or non-seniority tie-breaking by the Democratic COC on certain committees, not on Prestige and Constituency committees as a group. Environment and Commerce have reported t-stats; Governmental Affairs has one star. Numbers: Environment & Public Works: R mean 3.80 (N=30) vs D 1.00 (N=14), diff 2.80, t=3.05, SD diff 5.03. Commerce, Science & Transport: diff -1.74, t=-2.09, SD diff -2.87. Governmental Affairs: diff 4.93, t=1.76, SD diff 1.59. Budget and Rules show mean diffs of -0.33 (t -0.14) and -1.62 (t -0.56) and SD gaps of -6.43 and -4.05, with no test reported for the SD gaps.. (Sec. 5.2.2 p. 19; Table A5 pp. 23-25)
- Revealed-preference ranking of committee value (Prediction 4): Rules, Finance, Governmental Affairs and Appropriations are the most sought-after committees, even after excluding the most senior members. Numbers: Table A6 reports Republican average chamber seniority by committee for all members and excluding the 1, 2 and 3 most senior. Per-committee cell mapping is not recoverable from the extraction.. (Sec. 5.2.3 p. 19; Table A6 p. 24 (extraction partly garbled))
- Structural reduced-form model (Republicans only): committee common value and idiosyncratic variance can be recovered from the mean and variance of seniority at appointment. Powerful Policy and Prestige committees (Finance, Appropriations) have large common values and small idiosyncratic differences; Constituency committees (Small Business, Armed Services) depend on constituency particulars and have lower common value; Policy committees (Environment, Labor) have large idiosyncratic components. Numbers: Sample: 426 new Senate assignments, 25 committees, 97 senators, 104th-114th (fn. 22). Table A7/A8 numeric cells are garbled in extraction, so no mu or sigma values are reported here.. (Sec. 5.3 pp. 19-20; Tables A7-A8 pp. 24-25)
- Incumbents with a guaranteed existing assignment are more truthful in reporting rank-orders: a Boston-mechanism player with a better guaranteed payoff weakly raises rank positions for each committee (Proposition 1). This gives Prediction 3(b), which the paper says it cannot test with its data. Numbers: Proposition 1: if u(y) > u(x) with y > x, optimal rank-orders satisfy y_i >= x_i for all i. No empirical test reported.. (Sec. 3.5.2 pp. 14-16; Proposition 1 p. 15; Prediction 3 p. 16; untested per p. 18)
- Assignment stickiness may reflect mechanism design rather than only specialization: under the Republican mechanism, existing tenants at risk of swaps, and queuing benefits from within-committee seniority, confound the interpretation of high seniority on a committee as revealed value. Numbers: None; qualitative argument with the queuing confound acknowledged.. (Sec. 3.5.3 pp. 16-17; Prediction 4 p. 16; Figure C1 (online appendix))
- Canonical theories: the Distributive Theory anticipates the Democratic mechanism is non-strategyproof but treats equilibrium too naively and does not fit the Republican mechanism. The Informational Theory can explain possible Democratic COC tie-breaking criteria but has difficulty with the Republican mechanism. The Cartel-Agenda channel is largely unavailable to Republican leaders but available to Democratic COC tie-breakers, and only under excess demand. Numbers: Three-politician, three-committee example: commitment to a tie-breaking rule can produce any of six matchings in the perfectly correlated case; discipline requires excess demand.. (Sec. 4 pp. 16-18; Sec. 6 p. 20; three-politician example p. 11)
- Worked Republican example: SD gives a non-individually-rational matching (Inc3 loses seat C); SD with squatting rights and SD with waiting list each fail one property; TTC/YRMH-IGYT gives an IR and Pareto-efficient matching. Numbers: Example 1 (five seats, four incumbents, one freshman): SD gives (Inc1-D, Inc2-C, Inc3-B, Inc4-E, Fr1-A); TTC/YRMH-IGYT gives (Inc1-D, Inc2-B, Inc3-C, Inc4-E, Fr1-A).. (Sec. 3.5.1 pp. 12-14, Example 1 p. 12)

## Limitations

- The paper says its empirical evidence is suggestive only (Sec. 5.1, p. 18), not causal.
- No systematic Senate Republican request dataset exists for testing Prediction 1, 2(a) or 3(b) (p. 18). Republican request data are archival, limited to the 91st and 103rd Congresses, and the number of records is not reported.
- Cross-party comparisons assume the distribution of true preferences is identical for Republicans and Democrats (p. 19); relaxing this would need matching or structural estimation, which was not done.
- Only first-choice requests are compared for Republicans, because 76% of non-freshman Republicans rank one choice (Figure A1 note, p. 25).
- The freshman vs non-freshman Democratic difference (8%, p = 0.15) is not significant at conventional levels.
- The Democratic Boston mechanism is approximated; COC tie-breaking votes are secret and unobserved, so a Democratic structural model cannot be estimated (fn. 21, p. 20).
- The structural model is reduced form; richer models with politician- and session-specific terms could not be estimated (fn. 22, p. 20), with 426 assignments across 97 senators.
- Republican strategyproofness is supported partly by archival anecdote (Exhibit #4) and the Dole letter (Exhibit #5); Exhibit #6 (sequential implementation) is also anecdotal.
- Interdependent preferences (over who else sits on a committee, or House counterparts) would break strategyproofness of both mechanisms (fn. 13, p. 9).
- The author treats seniority-only tie-breaking as unlikely for Democrats (p. 18), but rival tie-breaking criteria are not formally tested.
- Queuing benefits and specialization stickiness confound interpreting high seniority on a committee as revealed value (p. 16).
- Party discipline is modeled only through Democratic COC tie-breaking; Republican leader influence is shown only through the Dole exhibits (Sec. 3.4, pp. 10-11).
- The Republican mechanism is a static SD/TTC approximation; dynamic rules and sequential implementation are simplified (fn. 7 p. 6; fn. 17 p. 14).
- Excludes the House, post-election seat-ratio negotiations (fn. 9, p. 7), and does not model policy effects of committee membership.

## Gaps and flags

- The committee pillar is the same for both chambers and both parties. Nothing records how the seat was obtained (seniority queue vs COC selection) or the member's seniority. The paper shows that selection into seats differs by party, so committee-score validity is untested within strata.
- There is no committee-value (prestige or common-value) term separate from CU relevance. A Highly seat on a low-common-value constituency committee such as Small Business and a Highly seat on a high-value policy committee are priced identically.
- Chair and ranking multipliers ignore that Senate leadership largely follows within-committee seniority queues. The paper flags queuing benefits as confounding seniority read as value.
- The pillar effectively pools across a fault line the paper identifies: the Republican seniority mechanism vs Democratic COC tie-breaking. Any committee-score coefficient or correlation against outcomes must be reported by party (and chamber), or labelled UNSTRATIFIED.
- The paper's evidence is self-described as suggestive. Key contrasts are not significant at conventional levels (Democrat freshman gap p=0.15), and Table A6-A8 numbers are garbled in extraction. Do not quote committee value estimates from it.
- Senate only (about 100 senators). Nothing transfers to House seat-holders without separate evidence.
- Hall & Wayman (1990) and Fouirnaies & Hall (2018), cited here on lobbying and committee access, are background only. Neither licenses access or influence language for committee seats in the system.

The paper agrees with Paul's committee-pillar note flagging a chicken-or-egg problem between partisanship and committee selection. It gives a mechanism for that worry: Democratic COC tie-breaking can discipline placement, while Republican placement is mostly seniority. The paper does not support Paul's broader premise that committees are the structural power layer (Porter) in any way that would underwrite the tier weights or the 40% Influence weight. Those weights remain owner policy that this paper does not test.  The note does not yet record that seat selection differs by party, which matters for any future validation of the committee pillar.
