# Using step and path selection functions for estimating resistance to movement: pumas as a case study

**Katherine A. Zeller, Kevin McGarigal, Samuel A. Cushman, Paul Beier, T. Winston Vickers, Walter M. Boyce (2016).** *Landscape Ecology (vol. 31, pp. 1319-1335; published online 2 Nov 2015). Special issue: Multi-scale habitat modeling..* [10.1007/s10980-015-0301-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10980-015-0301-6) Theme: [[themes/adjacent|Adjacent]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Zeller et al. (2016) used GPS tracks from ten pumas in the Santa Ana Mountains (75,716 fixes at 5-minute intervals, 2011-2014) to fit two kinds of conditional-logistic movement model. Step selection functions (SSFs) compare each observed step with available alternatives. Path selection functions (PathSFs) do the same for whole 24-hour paths. Both were fitted at 19 Pareto-kernel scales from 532 m to 7,390 m. The predicted relative probability was inverted to give a resistance surface, which CircuitScape then used to locate road-crossing candidates on I-15.
> 
> Main results:
> - **Scale.** Coefficients are very sensitive to scale. Some cover types change sign, and PathSF urban coefficients run from about -10 to -30.
> - **Method.** PathSFs fit better under 10-fold cross-validation (CCC 0.992 vs 0.976 for SSFs).
> - **Sampling interval.** Coarsening fixes to 60 minutes shifts coefficients 3-4 fold and flips some signs.
> - **Crossing locations.** These change with method, scale and prediction framework, and scale is the biggest driver.
> - **Unvalidated core step.** The authors say no empirical evidence supports treating resistance as the inverse of probability.
> 
> The paper has nothing on lobbying, legislators or political networks. It matters here only by analogy, and only in one place: the path-finding layer turns modelled tie strength or probability into an additive cost (-log p or -ln(0.8·strength)) and then takes the cheapest route. The paper is a close case study showing that this kind of probability-to-cost inversion is an assumption, and that the routes it produces depend on scale and sampling choices. It also gives a template for testing such costs: compare the routes actually used against available alternatives in a case-control design. The staffer-route search already labels its cost as "not a probability of access", which fits both the paper's caution and D-025. What it lacks is any used-vs-available calibration, which PathSF would supply if an outreach log ever exists. All transfer is analogical (animals on land cover, not people in organisations). Strength is weak throughout. This analysis is UNVERIFIED until a second path reproduces it (DE-062).

## Study
**Question.** Can step selection functions (SSFs) and path selection functions (PathSFs) be extended to model habitat selection during movement at multiple spatial scales, and how sensitive are the regression coefficients and predicted resistance surfaces to (a) SSF vs PathSF, (b) scale, (c) paired vs unpaired prediction framework, and (d) GPS acquisition interval (5 min vs 60 min)?

**Theory.** Resource selection functions (used vs available design; Johnson 1980 third-order selection; Manly et al. 2002). SSFs (Fortin et al. 2005) and PathSFs (Cushman et al. 2010) are conditional logistic models of movement selection; predicted relative probability exp(sum b_i x_i) is inverted to give resistance. The paper argues that movement-based selection can differ from resource-use (point) selection, citing Squires et al. 2013, Elliot et al. 2014 and Zeller et al. 2014. Scale of analysis is defined as the Pareto-kernel radius (ecological neighborhood), not data grain.

**Data.** Ten pumas (Puma concolor), six female and four male, Santa Ana Mountains, southern California; Lotek 4400S GPS collars set to 5-min fixes, Oct 2011 to Mar 2014. Collar duration 9-71 days (median 29). Final dataset 75,716 fixes after removing 2D fixes with PDOP above 5 (individual range 1,650-18,464; median 7,147). Unit of analysis: used/available step (steps of 200 m or more, buffered 30 m) and used/available 24-h path (n = 315 paths, 12 start-hour subsets averaged). Land cover: CalVeg vector data at 1:24,000, rasterized to 30 m, aggregated from 25 types to nine classes (chaparral 45%, urban 19%, coastal scrub 14%, annual grassland 6%, coastal oak woodland 5%, agriculture 5%, riparian 3%, perennial grassland 2%, barren/open 1%). Pseudo data: the 5-min data subsampled to 60-min fixes. Study period 2011-2014; no sex-specific analysis (sexes pooled).

**Method.** Multi-scale SSF and PathSF with Pareto-weighted kernels. 19 time intervals (5 min, 20 min, then every 20 min to 360 min); generalized Pareto fitted per interval with gpd.fit (gPdtest); kernel radius = min(97.5th percentile of displacement, maximum observed displacement), giving scales of 532 to 7,390 m. Available area for each step/path = mean land cover proportion across kernels placed along the step/path. Predictors = differences in land cover proportion between used and available (no intercept, response always 1). Simple single-variable conditional logistic models at each scale; multiple models using the weakest-effect class as reference; characteristic scale = scale with largest absolute coefficient in simple models; multi-scale model combines characteristic scales. Mixed-effects (random puma) conditional logistic models failed to converge, so glm was used. Model performance: 10-fold cross-validation (Johnson et al. 2006) with Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC). AIC compared only within SSF and within PathSF (equal sample sizes). Resistance predicted in unpaired (pixel-only) and paired (pixel plus kernel context) frameworks; resistance = 1/probability, not rescaled. Acquisition-interval bias: 60-min pseudo steps and paths treated against 5-min truth; bias = mean absolute difference in coefficients per cover type, averaged per scale. Connectivity: CircuitScape current density between protected areas on either side of I-15 (Temecula corridor); top 20 current-flow pixels along I-15 compared across resistance models.

**Identification.** No causal identification strategy. Inference rests on the within-animal case-control design (each used step/path is compared with its own available area, which is the standard conditional logistic set-up), plus two internal comparisons: (1) 5-min data as truth versus 60-min subsampled pseudo data for the same animals, to isolate the effect of acquisition interval; (2) paired versus unpaired prediction from the same fitted coefficients, to isolate the effect of prediction framework. Out-of-sample performance is assessed by 10-fold cross-validation with CCC. Cross-method comparisons of AIC are explicitly not made.

**Scope.** Population: ten pumas with established home ranges in one fragmented, urbanizing region (Santa Ana Mountains, southern California). Dispersing animals were not sampled and the authors say results may differ for them. Era: GPS data 2011-2014. Land cover from CalVeg (USDA Forest Service 2007). Methods are presented as general for SSFs/PathSFs at any GPS interval; empirical claims apply only to pumas in this study area.

## Key findings
- Regression coefficients are highly sensitive to the scale of analysis. Annual grassland and agriculture change sign across scales. For PathSFs, urban coefficients range about -10 to -30 and chaparral about -4 to -15. (PathSF urban -10 to -30; chaparral -4 to -15; 19 scales from 532 to 7,390 m; Results, Characteristic scales, Fig. 3 (p. ~1326); Discussion (p. 1331))
- Pumas show multiple characteristic scales of response during movement, described as mostly bi-modal. Coarse scales characterize urban and agriculture; fine scales characterize the remaining cover types (annual grassland, barren, chaparral, coastal scrub, perennial grassland, and others). (Scales 532 to 7,390 m; 19 time intervals from 5 min to 360 min; Results p. 1326; Discussion p. 1331)
- PathSFs generally produced larger-magnitude coefficients than SSFs. Characteristic scales also differed between the two methods for every cover type except chaparral, most for coastal oak woodland and riparian. Annual grassland was the exception to the larger-PathSF pattern. (Differences in characteristic scale for all types except chaparral; Results p. 1326; Discussion p. 1331)
- Predictive performance (10-fold CCC) rose with scale and PathSFs outperformed SSFs at all scales except the finest (532 m PathSF was the weak case). Best SSF CCC 0.976 at 6,555 m; best PathSF CCC 0.992 at 7,390 m. The multi-scale model did not have the highest CCC but was close: 0.943 (SSF) and 0.982 (PathSF). (SSF best 0.976 (6,555 m); PathSF best 0.992 (7,390 m); multi-scale 0.943 SSF, 0.982 PathSF; Results, Model performance, Fig. 4 (p. 1326); Discussion p. 1331)
- Within each method, AIC decreased with increasing scale. The multi-scale model had the lowest AIC for SSFs; the 6,555 m model had the lowest AIC for PathSFs. AIC could not be compared between SSFs and PathSFs because sample sizes differed. (SSF lowest AIC = multi-scale; PathSF lowest AIC = 6,555 m; Results, Model performance (pp. 1326-1328, Appendix 3 not in text))
- Acquisition interval bias: 60-min pseudo data produced a consistent 3-4-fold difference in regression coefficients relative to 5-min data. Bias was larger for PathSFs than SSFs. Some cover types changed sign. Annual grassland and barren were preferred in 5-min steps but avoided in 60-min pseudo steps. Coastal scrub, avoided in the true steps, appeared preferred in the pseudo steps. (3-4-fold consistent difference; 60-min vs 5-min; Results, Acquisition interval bias, Fig. 5 (p. 1328); Discussion (p. 1331))
- Paired-framework resistance surfaces are more heterogeneous, with a greater range and larger maximum values, than unpaired surfaces. Unpaired surfaces assign one resistance value per cover type regardless of context. In the paired surfaces, urban produces rings of high resistance on entry but low resistance inside. (Urban (19% of area) gives highest unpaired resistance; 91st-100th percentile identical across unpaired surfaces; Results, Sensitivity of resistance surfaces, Fig. 6, Appendices 5-6 (pp. 1328-1330); Discussion p. 1331-1333)
- Resistance values were higher for PathSFs than SSFs and increased with scale. The finest scale had the lowest resistance range, especially for SSFs. The authors attribute the scale trend to coefficients becoming more negative as scale increases. (Finest scale lowest range; resistance increases with scale; Results p. 1328; Discussion p. 1330)
- Road-crossing candidate locations (top 20 current-flow pixels along I-15) differed between SSF and PathSF, among scales, and between prediction frameworks. Locations were more similar at the same scale across methods and frameworks than within the same method across scales, so scale is a major driver of connectivity differences. Pseudo-step (60-min) models biased crossing locations and missed middle-I-15 sites, and SSF models also flagged sites absent from true-path models. (Top 20 pixels along I-15 per model; Results p. 1330 (Fig. 7, Appendix 7 not in text); Discussion p. 1331)
- Authors recommend PathSF models over SSFs when a choice is available, and caution that resistance surfaces and corridors are sensitive to method, scale and GPS schedule. (; Conclusions (p. 1334))

## Limitations
- Only ten pumas, with sexes pooled; mixed-effects models with individual as random effect failed to converge, so glm was used and individual heterogeneity was not modeled (p. 1325).
- Only land cover predictors were used; slope, topographic ruggedness and roads, known to affect puma selection, were excluded to simplify the methods (p. 1332).
- Study animals had established home ranges; the authors state results may not hold for dispersers, who may select habitat at finer scales (p. 1331-1332).
- The inverse of predicted relative probability is assumed to equal resistance; the authors state no empirical evidence supports this (p. 1333).
- Whether the paired framework is superior to the unpaired framework, or a hybrid, is unresolved; the paired surfaces may not capture absolute fitness costs, and the authors note concerns about rings of high resistance around urban areas (pp. 1328-1333).
- Resistance surfaces were compared visually and by distributional summaries only; no method for whole-surface validation was provided beyond road-crossing locations (p. 1333).
- Connectivity analysis is a single illustrative CircuitScape run in one corridor (Temecula / I-15) and was described as cursory (p. 1326).
- Bias was assessed only at one alternative interval (60 min); the optimal acquisition interval for pumas was not determined, and a shorter-than-5-min interval may be better (p. 1332).
- AIC cannot be compared between SSFs and PathSFs because sample sizes differ, limiting method-level model selection.
- The scale of analysis is the Pareto-kernel radius, and the grain was held at 30 m; the effect of grain was not examined.
- The 24-h path analysis averages over 12 start-time subsets rather than estimating a single model.
- Brownian bridge alternatives were suggested but not tested (p. 1333).

## Short quotes
- "Regression coefficients were extremely sensitive to scale and pumas exhibited multiple scales of selection during movement." (Abstract, Results (p. 1319))
- "Given our results, when there is a choice, we recommend PathSF models be used over SSF models." (Conclusions (p. 1334))
- "a mismatch between GPS collar acquisition interval and species vagility may ultimately bias corridor conservation planning" (Discussion (p. 1331))

## Gaps and flags
- No used-vs-available (case-control) calibration of any route cost: the staffer and recruitment path costs are hand-set transforms of strength or probability and have never been fitted to observed route choices or successful introductions.
- No sensitivity report showing how the returned top route changes when the cost transform (-ln vs 1/p vs linear) or the tie-strength time window changes. The paper shows that the cost-surface and route choices can be the dominant source of route differences.
- No test of whether multi-hop recruitment behaves like independent hops (step-wise) or like a whole path. Chain reliability assumes independence.
- Wording guard (D-025): the paper's 'resistance' and 'relative probability of movement' language must not be imported to describe staffer or recruitment routes as closeness, access or introduction probabilities. The current code wording ('not a probability of access') already complies.
- The paper does not argue for either rejected SNA proposal (dropping regular cosponsorship edges; per-edge volume normalization). Nothing to flag there.
- All bearings are analogical, cross-domain (puma movement on land cover) and UNVERIFIED (DE-062).

Paul's pathfinding notes call for learned heuristics, A*-style handling of unstable information, and dynamic replanning. The built staffer router uses heuristic 0 and hand-set costs. This paper does not speak to heuristics. It adds a caution Paul's notes lack: the step that turns a probability into a cost is itself an unvalidated assumption, and route outputs are sensitive to it.
