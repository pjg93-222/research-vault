# Optimal Targeting in Fundraising

**Tobias Cagala, Ulrich Glogowsky, Johannes Rincke, Anthony Strittmatter (2021).** *arXiv preprint arXiv:2103.10251v3 [econ.EM], 29 Sep 2021 (dated September 30, 2021 on the title page). No journal appears in the text. AEA RCT Registry ID AEARCTR-0007581. The metadata hint matches the text..* Theme: [[themes/causal-methodological|Causal & Methodological]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Cagala, Glogowsky, Rincke and Strittmatter ran a natural field experiment in 2014 with a Catholic Church fundraiser in one German city (N=19,779 church members). A small gift (postcards, cost 1.16 euro) was randomly assigned with the solicitation letter, stratified by warm list (past donors, N=2,354) and cold list (never donors, N=17,425). Sending the gift to everyone barely paid for itself on the warm list (net +0.06 euro, not significant) and lost money on the cold list (-0.97 euro per person). Gift effects varied widely across warm-list donors, from -11.59 to +17.99 euro between the 5th and 95th percentiles. The authors used AIPW scores and a depth-2 exact policy tree (Athey-Wager policy learning) to learn who should get the gift. Under 20-fold cross-validation, the rule sent the gift to 33% of the warm list and raised expected net donations by about 2.1-2.2 euro (about 14%) over all-gift, no-gift and random assignment. On the cold list the rule chose almost nobody (1.4%). Geospatial covariates alone did about as well as the full set of covariates. Applied to a caliper-matched 2015 sample (radius 0.1), the rule gained significantly; against no-gift significance held up to radius 0.25, and the paper reports no significance above 0.1. On the full 2015 sample it showed no significant gain.
> 
> What this means for Paul's systems: a targeting rule for a costly treatment is only justified when it beats uniform and random benchmarks out of sample, and that requires randomized variation in the treatment. Melange's money allocators and Terrier's canvass potential score both target on heterogeneity that is assumed, through response curves, multipliers and per-district weights. Neither is learned from a randomized assignment. The paper describes the experiment and benchmark test the system lacks. It does not validate any of the system's constants. Its setting (individual charitable donors) also differs from the system's targets: legislators for Melange, and voters for Terrier.

## Study

**Question:** Can a charity raise more net donations (donations minus fundraising cost) by targeting an unconditional gift (small Dürer postcards mailed with a solicitation letter) to a subset of potential donors, using causal machine learning to learn an optimal targeting rule from a randomized field experiment, and does that rule generalize? Theory: charities face costly fundraising and donor overhead aversion (Rose-Ackerman 1982; Name-Correa and Yildirim 2013; Gneezy et al. 2014); the optimal rule targets "net donors" whose expected additional donation exceeds the marginal cost; heterogeneous motives (altruism, warm glow, reciprocity, sequential reciprocity) make gift effects heterogeneous across individuals.

**Theory:** Charities are public-goods providers facing costly fundraising and donor overhead aversion. Targeting a costly instrument at net donors (expected gain above marginal cost) maximizes net donations (Name-Correa and Yildirim 2013). Donor heterogeneity from altruism, warm glow and reciprocity (Falk 2007; Falk et al. 2018) makes gift effects heterogeneous.

**Data:** Natural field experiment in 2014 with a Catholic Church fundraiser in a German urban area (26% of residents are church members). Unit: individual church member (donor). Sample of 19,779 individuals: warm list (previous donors) N=2,354 (1,180 gift, 1,174 control); cold list (never donors) N=17,425 (2,283 gift, 15,142 control). Randomization stratified on list, gender, household type, quintiles of predicted baseline giving and age quintiles. Outcomes: donation amount in euro in the first year after the experiment (2015) and the first two years; donation dummy; net donation (donation minus gift variable cost, 1.16 euro). Covariates: (a) administrative records (gender, marital status, age, residency, donation history 2006-2015, per the text) and (b) Google Maps geospatial counts within 300 m (restaurants, supermarkets, medical, cultural facilities, churches) plus distances to station, city hall, main church and airport, travel time and elevation. External validity: 2015 follow-up experiment on a new warm-list sample (text says N=3,616; Table G.2 says 3,612), 50% gift probability; the 2015 sample is matched to 2014 by caliper propensity-score matching (radius 0.1). The text states donation records run 2006-2015 while also saying two post-experiment years were observed; the second year's dates are not reconciled in the text.

**Method:** Potential-outcomes framework. CATE delta(x) and ATE identified under stratified randomization and SUTVA (Appendix B). Outcome-model/propensity nuisance estimation: logit for propensity, OLS for outcome regressions (Table D.1), combined into an augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) score (Eq. 3-4). Optimal targeting rule estimated directly via Athey and Wager (2021) empirical welfare maximization, using exact policy-learning trees of Zhou, Athey and Wager (2018) with search depth two (policytree R package). Gains evaluated by 20-fold cross-validation of out-of-sample net donations versus all-gift, no-gift and random-gift benchmarks. Descriptive heterogeneity: sorted-effects approach (Chernozhukov, Fernandez-Val and Luo 2018) with multiplier-bootstrap bands, plus best linear predictor test with causal forest (Chernozhukov et al. 2020). Data-type ablations (socioeconomic, donation history, geospatial, combinations). Sensitivity to depth one and three exact trees, CART (fixed and cross-validated depth), weighted logit (baseline and flexible), logit lasso, classification forest; nuisance robustness via population propensity and cross-fitted lasso (Tables I.1-I.4). Identification of the targeting gain does not require identifying the causal effect of each characteristic; trees indicate correlations only.
Identification strategy: random assignment of the gift within strata (Zi), exogenous of potential outcomes conditional on Zi, 0 &lt; p(z) &lt; 1; heterogeneity variables Xi are not needed for identification.

**Identification:** Random assignment of the gift stratified on list, gender, household type, quintiles of predicted baseline willingness to give and age quintiles; SUTVA; overlap 0 &lt; p(z) &lt; 1 (Appendix B). Targeting gains are identified from the AIPW score; no need to observe heterogeneity variables for identification.

**Scope:** Population: church members in one German urban area, targeted by one Catholic Church fundraiser with an annual solicitation mailing, split into warm (past donors) and cold (never donors) lists. Institution: a single religious charity; religious giving is the largest private giving category in Germany (about 35% of private giving per the text, citing Deutscher Spendenrat 2016). Era: 2014 treatment with 2015 (and second-year) outcomes; 2015 follow-up experiment on warm list. The paper states the findings may be specific to the setting.

## Key findings

- Gift ATE on donations (first year): warm list +1.21 to +1.24 euro, not significant. Net of the 1.16 euro gift cost: +0.05 to +0.08 euro, not significant. Cold list ATE +0.19 euro (significant); net of cost -0.97 euro per donor, so the all-gift rule loses money in the cold list. (warm ATE 1.21-1.24 (SE 1.15-1.25); warm net 0.05-0.08; cold ATE 0.19 (SE 0.07-0.10); cold net -0.97 (SE 0.07-0.10); preferred AIPW warm ATE 1.22, net 0.06; Section 5.1, Table 1 (p.17-18))
- Heterogeneity: sorted effects show substantial individual variation in warm-list gift effects; 45% of warm-list individuals have estimated effects above the 1.16 euro cost. Effects range from +17.99 euro (95th percentile responders) to -11.59 euro (5th percentile). In the cold list, effects exceed cost only above the 92nd percentile and are small. (warm: 45% above cost; 5th pct -11.59 euro; responsive individuals +17.99 euro; cold: 5th pct -0.73, 95th pct +1.38 euro; Section 5.2, Figure 2 (p.19-20))
- Best linear predictor test with causal forest detects heterogeneity in the warm list, significant only at the 10 percent level (p 0.088 one-sided), and none in the cold list. (warm heterogeneity loading 0.959* (SE 0.710); cold 0.511 (SE 1.069); Section 5.2 and Table E.3 (p.21; Appendix p.12))
- Warm list optimal rule (cross-validated out-of-sample): gift is assigned to 33% of warm-list individuals; expected net donations 17.61 euro, which is 2.14 euro (13.8%) above all-gift, 2.20 euro (14.3%) above no-gift, and 2.17 euro (14.1%) above random-gift, all significant. (share treated 0.33; net donation 17.61 euro (SE 0.97); vs all-gift 2.14*** (0.82); vs no-gift 2.20*** (0.81); vs random 2.17*** (0.58); Section 5.3, Table 2 Panels A-B (p.21-22))
- Secondary outcomes (warm list): donation probability in first year is +0.025 (significant at 5%) vs no-gift, with no significant difference from the all-gift rule; net donations over two years are 32.94 euro, +2.33 vs all-gift and +3.75 vs no-gift, so the gains persist and there is no evidence of pull-forward. (donation probability 1st year 0.503; vs no-gift 0.025**; two-year net donations 32.94 euro; +2.33* vs all-gift; +3.75*** vs no-gift; two-year probability 0.582; Section 5.3, Table 2 Panel C (p.22-23))
- Cold list optimal rule assigns the gift to only 1.4% of cold-list individuals. Expected net donation 0.15 euro, essentially equal to no-gift (-0.005, not significant), and significantly above all-gift (0.97 euro) and random-gift (0.48 euro). The paper concludes the gift should not be sent to cold-list individuals at all. (share treated 0.014; net donation 0.15***; vs all-gift 0.97*** (0.10); vs no-gift -0.005 (0.012); vs random 0.48*** (0.05); Section 5.3, Table 3 (p.23-24) and Conclusion (p.33))
- Warm-list targeted (net donor) vs non-targeted (net receiver) characteristics: targeted donors gave more before the experiment (average 4.10 vs 3.90 prior donations; 130.98 vs 123.39 euro total prior donations), live at lower elevation (308.66 vs 321.38 m), and have more restaurants, churches and cultural facilities nearby. Socioeconomic differences are small. Sample: 787 targeted vs 1,567 non-targeted. (elevation std diff 157.80; cultural facilities 0.240 vs 0.050 (std diff 32.27); female share 0.507 vs 0.539 (std diff 6.46); Section 5.4, Table 4 (p.24-25))
- Cold-list targeted vs non-targeted: targeted individuals live closer to city hall, main station and airport, have fewer restaurants nearby (5.48 vs 10.40), and are more often female (0.558 vs 0.503) and single (0.713 vs 0.642). Sample: 251 targeted vs 17,174 non-targeted. (distance to city hall 1.364 vs 2.816 km (std diff 103.02); restaurants 5.482 vs 10.40 (std diff 57.08); Section 5.4, Table 5 (p.26))
- Data needed: the warm-list rule using only geospatial data (17.40 euro expected net donation, +1.92** vs all-gift, +1.98** vs no-gift) or only donation history (17.20 euro, +1.73** and +1.79**) performs about as well as the baseline (17.61 euro). Socioeconomic data alone do not beat the three benchmarks (15.71 euro; differences insignificant). Adding socioeconomic data does not improve the rules. Cold list: geospatial-only rule gives 0.15 euro, the same as the baseline. (warm geospatial-only 17.40; donation-history-only 17.20; socioeconomic-only 15.71 with 0.29 vs no-gift and 0.27 vs random-gift (insignificant); cold geospatial-only 0.15 with vs no-gift -0.005; Section 5.5, Tables 6-7 (p.27-29))
- External validity over time: the 2014 rule applied to a caliper-matched 2015 warm-list sample (radius 0.1) yields 19.85 euro expected net donation, +2.64* vs all-gift and +2.30** vs no-gift, and 35.6% treated. Applied to the full 2015 sample it yields 17.16 euro with no significant gain over all-gift (-0.88) or no-gift (+0.45). The text says the 2015 average gift effect is 1.33 euro vs 0.08 in 2014. (matched: 19.85, +2.64*, +2.30**, random +2.47**; full 2015: 17.16, -0.88, +0.45, -0.22; share treated 0.356 matched, 0.353 full; 2015 gift ATE 1.33 vs 2014 0.08; Section 5.6, Table 8 and Figure H.1 (p.30-32))
- Caliper sensitivity: the 2014 rule significantly beats no-gift up to a caliper radius of 0.25; beating all-gift is harder because the gift raises average net donations in the full 2015 sample. Above 0.1 the effects are no longer statistically significant but remain positive in expectation. (significance against no-gift holds to caliper 0.25; radius 0.1 is the standard; Section 5.6 and Appendix H, Figure H.1 (p.31-32))
- Sensitivity: in the warm list, the baseline exact policy tree (depth 2) dominates the alternative estimators, which yield lower net donations. Depth-1 trees and flexible logit have the lowest performance. The cross-validated-depth CART is the only alternative that significantly beats both all-gift and no-gift (17.40 euro, +1.93** and +1.98**). In the cold list, logit lasso and cross-validated CART are 0.01 euro higher, but the no-gift conclusion holds. (CV-CART warm 17.40 (+1.93 vs all-gift); flexible logit 16.28; depth-1 tree 15.05; cold logit lasso/CV-CART +0.01 euro; Section 5.7, Tables I.1-I.2 (p.32-33; Appendix p.18-19))
- Nuisance-parameter robustness: using the population propensity score or cross-fitted logit-lasso nuisances gives essentially identical results (warm 17.63 and 17.35 euro expected net donation; cold 0.15 euro). (population propensity: warm 17.63, +2.15***, +2.21***; cross-fitted lasso: warm 17.35, +1.85***, +1.79**; Appendix I, Tables I.3-I.4 (p.20-21))
- Example decision trees: the warm-list tree splits first on maximum prior donation (101.76 euro) and home elevation (313.55 m). The cold-list tree splits on number of restaurants within 300 m (5), distance to city hall (1.28 km) and distance to airport (3.06 km). The text warns that trees show correlations, not causal effects. (warm leaves: gift 4.94 euro (N=758), 81.13 euro (N=48); cold leaf 21.44 euro (N=58); Section 5.4 and Figure F.1 (p.26-27; Appendix p.13))

## Limitations

- Authors state that findings 'may well be specific to the setting' (one Catholic Church fundraiser, one German urban area, one annual letter with a folded-card gift).
- The optimal targeting rule identifies the policy gain, not the causal effect of each characteristic; trees show correlations, and unobserved variables (e.g., income, willingness to give) may drive the heterogeneity (Section 5.4).
- The gain estimates are cross-validated (K=20) rather than from a fresh holdout sample; external validity is tested only on one 2015 follow-up with warm-list individuals.
- External validity holds only when the new sample is similar to the training sample: the 2014 rule beats no-gift up to caliper 0.25 and the paper reports no significance above 0.1; the full 2015 sample shows no significant gains (Table 8).
- Cold-list results have far fewer treated individuals in the rule (1.4%, 251 individuals), and depth-three exact trees are infeasible in the cold list (Table I.2 note), so the cold-list tree comparison is limited.
- Data scope: the authors use only covariates the charity can collect (administrative records and Google Maps data); richer data could change results (Section 5.5 and Conclusion).
- Robustness checks use parametric nuisance estimators (logit and OLS); the authors note the AIPW estimator requires consistent and fast-converging nuisance estimators (Section 4.3).
- Internal inconsistencies in the text: the 2015 follow-up sample is 3,616 in the text but 3,612 in Table G.2 and 1,806+1,806 in Table G.1; the Table 8 note cites an expected 17.01 euro while the table shows 19.85; the text says donation records cover 2006-2015 but also refers to two post-experiment years.

## Gaps and flags

- No randomized or held-out variation in any costly treatment (PAC dollars, outreach, canvass contact). Every response heterogeneity in the allocators and in Terrier is an owner-set prior, not an estimated CATE.
- No benchmark-relative evaluation: neither the optimizer nor the workbook allocator reports realized, net-of-cost gain against all-fund, no-fund or random-fund allocations.
- Terrier's untapped-potential score targets on support level and turnout propensity. It has no estimate of who responds to a knock, and saved walk results are selected (non-random), so they cannot identify response.
- There is no check that a targeting rule carries over to a changed population (new Congress, new district, new cycle), which is the paper's 2015 caliper test. The paper's rule lost significance on the full new sample.
- There is no test of which covariate families (cheap geospatial vs administrative vs socioeconomic) are sufficient for targeting, which matters for Terrier's vendor-field costs.
- Flag: Do not cite this paper as evidence that dollars buy more in competitive seats or that reciprocity holds. It studies charitable donors, not legislators, and reciprocity is NOT validated (D-104..D-108).
- Flag: Any figure quoted from it must name its window and denominator. Examples: 2014 treatment with 2015 first-year outcomes; warm list N=2,354, of whom 787 were targeted; cold list N=17,425; the 2015 follow-up sample is 3,616 in the text and 3,612 in Table G.2 (an internal inconsistency).
- Flag: The headline +14% gain is cross-validated in-sample (K=20). On the full 2015 sample, the gain over all-gift was not significant (-0.88 euro). Quote both, never the 14% alone.
- Flag: The paper makes no SNA, LDA-edge or per-edge normalization argument, so the rejected-proposal flags do not apply.

It partly cuts against his ROI framing of money (flip-model and break-even positions): uniformly applied, the costly instrument barely covered its cost, and it paid only when targeted on response that had been learned from randomized data. It partly supports his joint-optimizer position that a learned policy should eventually replace hand-built greedy targeting. This paper adds the complementary point: who to contact should be estimated from randomized contact, not assumed from support level and turnout propensity.
