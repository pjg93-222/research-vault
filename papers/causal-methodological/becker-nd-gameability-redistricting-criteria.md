# The Gameability of Redistricting Criteria

**Amariah Becker, Dara Gold (Not stated in the text. It cites a May 2022 order and the 2021 Wisconsin litigation, so it is a 2022-era paper.).** *Not named in the text. The text gives only a 'Journal Article Version' link (rdcu.be/cYmc0) and the SSRN abstract 4132745. Hint correction: the authors are Amariah Becker (1A Becker Consulting LLC) and Dara Gold (The Washington Post, News Engineering), not 'Becker, Gold' only. Both worked at MGGG, Tufts, during the research. Competing interests: funded by Jenner & Block LLP (p.1)..* Theme: [[themes/causal-methodological|Causal & Methodological]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Becker and Gold take two common redistricting criteria, preserving counties and changing the prior plan as little as possible, and show that each can be measured in many defensible ways. The measures disagree about which map is best. On a hand-built 24x24 grid, Plan 2 splits the fewest counties (1), Plan 3 has the lowest Shannon split entropy (1.265) and Plan 4 has the best Even Splits score (2.485). In a 10x9 grid example, the plan that ties for the lowest People Change (9.5%) has the second-worst Area Change (13.3% against a best of 7.8%). On Minnesota (11 maps) and Wisconsin (6 litigation maps), no map is best on both a county-split measure and a least-change measure. Two Minnesota plans move almost the same share of people (1.90% and 1.93%), yet their incumbent-people change is 13.90% against 1.93%. The paper says stakeholders can cherry-pick the metrics that score best for their plans. Its recommendation is to define metrics explicitly but avoid hard numeric caps. The analysis is descriptive only, with no inference. It used relaxed population balance (up to 1%) and precinct or ward units, and the study was funded by Jenner & Block.
> 
> The paper's bearing is narrow: district change metrics and rank weights. That code compares old and new congressional boundaries (CD116 vs CD119 in `redistricting_density.py`, and the CBP/ACS vintage mismatch in the census scraper) and orients Cook PVI across the mid-decade redistricting. In both places the system uses implicit change measures: whether the seat label changed (plus a per-member cu_share delta), a weighting based on block counts, and a state-list flag plus a PVI-shift flag. The paper shows that this kind of choice decides the answer. Its weaker, general lesson, that choosing a metric reorders the winners, also applies to the score blend's three weight sets, where the code (0.45 Leg, 0.10 Press) and the spec (0.25 Leg, 0.30 Press) disagree.

## Study

**Question:** How should 'preserve subdivisions' (county splitting) and 'least change from a prior district plan' be measured, and do different defensible measures rank the same maps differently, so that the criteria can be gamed by choosing a metric?

**Theory:** No formal game-theoretic model. The conceptual frame is measurement dependence: a vague criterion admits many metrics, and each metric favors different plans. The paper uses information-theoretic measures (Shannon entropy, Variation of Information) and a pair-counting index (Fowlkes-Mallows) borrowed from clustering and information theory. It defines 'gameable' as 'exploitable or manipulable', not in the classic game-theoretic sense (p.2 fn.2).

**Data:** Unit: district plans built from base units (precincts/VTDs, census blocks, wards). No voters are modeled. (1) Hand-built 24x24 grid example: 9 counties, four districts of 144 cells each, population 1 per cell; 7 plans scored on county-split metrics (Table 1, p.13). (2) 10x9 grid example: 84 precincts, 84 people, 90 land units, 4 districts of 21 people; baseline plus 4 balanced variants (Fig. 7 p.17; Table 2 p.27). (3) Minnesota: a 2012 congressional baseline, the 2022 court-ordered map, and 10 search-generated plans. Built from VTDs with up to 1% population deviation, using 2020 Census data (Table 3 p.29; Sec 6.1 p.27-28). (4) Wisconsin: 2012 baseline plus 6 congressional plans submitted in 2021 litigation (Governor, Hunter, Congressmen, Congressmen-alt, Mathematicians, PMC), scored at ward level after transferring block assignments (Table 4 p.35; fn.11 p.34). Period: 2012 baseline, 2020 Census, 2021 litigation, 2022 court map. Sample sizes: 7 grid plans, 4 change-grid variants, 11 Minnesota maps, 6 Wisconsin submissions.

**Method:** Descriptive metric comparison (no inference). Section 4 defines county-split measures in four families: count-based (counties split, unnecessarily split, total county parts, split-county parts, unnecessary split-county parts, total county fragments), boundary-based (intra-county cut edges, and their length), entropy-based (Shannon entropy, Even Splits score, Square Root Entropy) and weighted variants. Section 5 defines least-change measures in matching-based form (People, Area, Precinct change), matching-free boundary forms (common-refinement perimeter, symmetric-length and symmetric-cut-edge perimeter), pairing forms (incumbent-people, incumbent-precinct, precinct-pair change), and Fowlkes-Mallows and Variation of Information. Each measure is computed on hand-built grid plans with worked arithmetic. Minnesota and Wisconsin plans are scored on all measures. Computer-generated Minnesota plans come from local search using ReCom and flip moves (DeFord-Duchin-Solomon 2021; Fifield et al. 2020), each run to maximize or minimize one metric. This search is not representative sampling. Plans 1-5 in Table 1 were hand-designed to illustrate properties, not produced by the optimizer (fn.4 p.12).

**Identification:** None. The paper makes no causal or statistical identification claim. Its validity rests on the metrics being well defined and computed correctly. Verification is by hand-worked examples on the grids (e.g., Shannon ~1.821, Even Splits ~4.804 for Fig. 2; FM ~21%, VI ~22.8% for Fig. 7) and by the public replication repository.

**Scope:** Population: US congressional and state-legislative district plans, with state examples from many states' constitutions and statutes (Iowa, Wyoming, Kentucky, South Carolina, Pennsylvania, Nebraska, Ohio, North Carolina, Washington, Idaho, Michigan, Louisiana, Kansas, New Mexico, New York, Utah). Empirical cases: Minnesota (2012 and 2022 congressional maps) and Wisconsin (2021 litigation). Institution: state courts and special masters in redistricting impasses (e.g., Wisconsin Supreme Court, New Hampshire Supreme Court, Minnesota Special Redistricting Panel). Era: the 2010-2022 cycle. Only three states have district-core preservation in constitution or statute (NM, NY, UT, per Yablon, cited at p.15). The paper itself describes the Wisconsin ruling as one of the 'most notorious' least-change decisions (p.15).

## Key findings

- Different county-split metrics pick different best plans on the same grid set. Plan 2 has the fewest counties split (1), Plan 3 has the lowest Shannon entropy (1.265) and fewest intra-county cut edges (18), and Plan 4 has the lowest Even Splits score (2.485) and fewest total county fragments (11). (Table 1, p.13; Sec. 4.3 text p.12) — Counties split: P1=3, P2=1, P3=2, P4=2, P5=2, P6=5, P7=9. Shannon: P3=1.265 best. Even Splits: P4=2.485 best. Intra-county cut edges: P3=18 best.
- The most compact grid plan (Plan 6, designed to maximize compactness) splits the most counties apart from Plan 7. The paper says plans designed to preserve counties do better across the board. (Table 1, p.13; text p.12) — Plan 6: 48 cut edges (lowest), 5 counties split, 16 total county parts. Plan 7: 154 cut edges, 9 counties split.
- Worked sample plan (Fig. 2, 24x24 grid, 4 districts of 144): splits 3 counties (C2, C5, C7); 12 total county parts; 14 total county fragments; 34 intra-county cut edges; Shannon entropy about 1.821; Even Splits about 4.804. (Sec. 4.2, pp.7-11) — 3 counties split; 12 parts; 14 fragments; 34 intra-county cut edges; Shannon 1.821; Even Splits 4.804.
- Change metrics pull apart. On the grid balanced variants, Plan 2 ties Plan 3 for best People Change (9.5%), while Plan 1 has the best Area Change (7.8%). The text says Plan 2 has the second-worst Area Change. (Table 2, p.27; Sec. 5.4 p.27) — People change: P1 11.9%, P2 9.5%, P3 9.5%, P4 26.2%. Area change: P1 7.8%, P2 13.3%, P3 12.5%, P4 26.7%.
- Minnesota: the court-ordered 2022 map is not the best on any measure but is 'far from the worst'. Plans 7-10 were built to do well on change measures, and the best change scores are spread across four plans. (Table 3, p.29; Sec. 6.1 pp.33-34) — Court-ordered: 9 counties split, 99 total county parts, Shannon 7.84, People change 7.50%, Area change 13.07%. Best change scores: Plan 9 People 1.90% (Plan 8 1.93% second), Plan 7 Precinct 1.09%, Plan 10 Area 0.28%.
- Population-based and incumbency-based change can diverge sharply. Plan 8 and Plan 9 move almost the same share of people, but Plan 9's incumbent-people change is far worse because an incumbent's precinct moved. The text says Plan 9 moves 0.03% more people than Plan 8; Table 3 shows the reverse (1.90% vs 1.93%), so this is an internal inconsistency to verify. (Table 3 p.29; Sec. 6.1 p.33) — Incumbent-people change: Plan 8 = 1.93%, Plan 9 = 13.90%. People change: Plan 8 = 1.93%, Plan 9 = 1.90% (table) vs text '+0.03%' for Plan 9.
- Trade-off between county splits and least change in the Minnesota set: no plan is best on both families. Plans 1 and 2 have some of the best county-split scores and some of the worst least-change scores. (Sec. 6.1 p.33; Table 3 p.29) — Plan 1: 1 county split, 0 unnecessarily split; People change 86.30%. Plan 2: 2 counties split, People change 98.07%.
- Wisconsin real-world plans: the Mathematicians plan is best on nearly all county-split metrics. Congressmen-alt is best on several change metrics but worst on every county-split metric. The court adopted the Governor's plan. No submitted map is best on both families. (Table 4, p.35; Sec. 6.2 p.34) — Mathematicians: 7 counties split, 80 total county parts, Shannon 5.60, Even Splits 11.49. Congressmen-alt: 14 counties split, 88 total county parts, Shannon 10.84; People change 3.88% (best). Governor: People change 5.50%, Area change 1.40% (best).
- Wisconsin least-change was undefined in the November 2021 ruling (the court 'called for a least-change approach'). The later final order defined it as the number of voters who remained in their old districts. This gave multiple parties grounds to claim their maps changed least. (Sec. 6.2 p.34; Sec. 5.1 p.15 (dissent quote)) — Not applicable (qualitative).
- Conclusion: vague criteria are gameable. The paper recommends that states define metrics explicitly but avoid hard numeric thresholds (e.g., a 2-county maximum), because feasibility depends on political geography. (Sec. 7 Conclusion p.37) — Not applicable (qualitative).

## Limitations

- Relaxed constraints for tractability: plans built from precincts/VTDs/wards rather than census blocks, and population deviation up to 1%, whereas congressional plans need one-person balance (Conclusion p.37; Sec. 6.1 p.28).
- The Minnesota court-ordered map was coarsened to precincts, which introduced a 1.3% population deviation not reflective of the actual districts. The sample maps are not valid proposals under one-person, one-vote (fn.9 p.28).
- Wisconsin metrics were computed at ward level, so figures would differ slightly at the block level (fn.11 p.34).
- Grid plans 1-5 and 6-7 were hand-designed to illustrate properties, not optimizer output (fn.4 p.12).
- Incumbent-based metrics ignore whether incumbents are seeking reelection (p.22).
- The measures surveyed are not exhaustive (p.25). Population-based and area-based scores are sensitive to projection (p.18), and the entropy measures use population only.
- Stated future work: communities of interest, compactness and partisan fairness are not analyzed (p.37).
- Internal inconsistency to verify: text (p.33) says Plan 9 moves 0.03% more people than Plan 8, but Table 3 (p.29) gives 1.90% vs 1.93%.

## Gaps and flags

- No least-change measure between CD116 and CD119. redistricting_density.py records a district-label inequality flag (seat_changed), a count of split counties and a per-member cu_share delta, none of which can tell a renumbered district from a redrawn one.
- Old-to-new allocation weights count blocks, not people. The paper shows that switching the basis between population and area reorders results, and the size of this basis effect on the CU-share deltas has not been measured.
- The census-cd-metrics boundary-mismatch question (how large the error from the CBP 116th vs ACS 119th mismatch is) has no metric chosen to answer it. Per-state People Change or Variation of Information would supply one.
- COMPET_MULT does not measure per-member constituency turnover across the 2025 and 2026 maps. Redistricting is flagged only by state list and by a PVI delta of 5 points or more.
- The paper does not argue for either rejected SNA proposal (dropping regular cosponsorship edges or per-edge volume normalization), and it makes no claims about LDA, revolving-door ties or graph ranks. No brain-rule conflict.
- Competing interest: the research was funded by Jenner & Block LLP (p.1), a litigation-adjacent sponsor. The examples include Wisconsin litigation maps.
- Internal inconsistency in the paper: the text on p.33 says Plan 9 moves 0.03% more people than Plan 8, but Table 3 on p.29 gives 1.90% vs 1.93%. Do not quote that difference without the caveat.
- Pre-existing system doc conflict, surfaced here and not caused by the paper: pillar_policy.py:11-16 has Leg .45 / Press .10, while MATHEMATICAL_SPECIFICATION.md:12 has Leg .25 / Press .30.
- Any change to PVI basis or overrides is Paul's ruling (D-107).

Paul has no annotations on this paper, and none of his component positions cites redistricting-metric literature, so there is no takeaway to agree with or go stale. Two points connect to positions he does hold. First, Paul endorses Cook PVI as a vulnerability measure (Gaynor). This paper adds the caveat that once maps are redrawn, which PVI belongs to which member depends on how district change is measured, and the system currently handles that with flags and his D-107 overrides rather than a change metric. Second, the paper's general warning that metric choice is gameable fits his position that the blend weights are owner policy with no literature behind them. It argues for documenting one weight set rather than for any particular weights.
