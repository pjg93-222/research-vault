# Shadowing and shielding: Effective heuristics for continuous influence maximisation in the voting dynamics

**Guillermo Romero Moreno, Sukankana Chakraborty, Markus Brede (2021).** *PLOS ONE 16(6): e0252515 (published June 18, 2021; received Jan 26, 2021; accepted May 17, 2021). Metadata hint confirmed..* [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0252515](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0252515). Theme: [[themes/adaptive-influence-maximization|Adaptive Influence Maximization]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Romero Moreno, Chakraborty and Brede (PLOS ONE 2021) study influence maximisation in a voter model with zealots. An active controller spreads a budget over the nodes of one 1,133-node university email network against a passive opponent whose allocation is fixed and known. They compare a discrete regime (K nodes at fixed gain) with a continuous regime (any intensity per node). The continuous optimum is spread out: about 111 of 1,133 nodes get above-average allocation, and the 1,013 near-flat nodes carry near-uniform allocation (std/mean 0.11 for those nodes). It beats the best discrete plan by 5-10% when the opponent targets fewer than 100 nodes.
> 
> Hub (degree) targeting explains discrete optima but explains little in the continuous regime. Two heuristics do: shadowing (spend where the opponent spends) and shielding (spend on the neighbours of the opponent's targets). Continuous shadowing plus shielding comes within 1.89e-3 of optimal vote share under budget advantage, against 2.09e-2 for degree targeting. In the low-allocation limit the best response is linear in the opponent's spend, with coefficient (Ba/Bb − 1): a richer controller shadows the opponent and a poorer one avoids the opponent's nodes. When both controllers optimise, the unique pure Nash equilibrium is uniform allocation. The paper uses no political data, no lobbying data and no noise.
> 
> The paper matters here because Melange's money layer is exactly this setup. optimize.py allocates a CU pot in $500 steps against fixed industry money, a passive opponent, and pflip is a single-node a/(a+b) vote-share form. In the system, though, dollars act only on the funded member's own pflip and do not spread to neighbours; only conversion events (optimize.py shift_pro) change a converted member's stance and so neighbours' camp totals, which is not the paper's shielding mechanism. An opponent-share field w_b already exists, labelled for a "shadowing layer", but no engine reads it. The contested-bills counterparty registry is loaded and displayed by build_dashboard_bundle.py (display-only); no engine consumes it. The paper's support is theoretical and comes from one network, so it supports the design concept and gives no calibration.

## Study

**Question.** How do optimal influence allocations differ between the continuous regime (flexible intensity per node) and the discrete regime (K nodes at fixed gain) in influence maximisation for the voter model with zealots? Can three heuristics (hub/degree preference, shadowing, shielding) explain optimal allocations? How exploitable is a passive opponent, and what is the pure-strategy Nash equilibrium when two active controllers optimise simultaneously? (Abstract p.1; Introduction pp.2-3.)

**Theory.** Voter model with external controllers (zealots) on a weighted network. Each node's probability of holding opinion A follows a rate equation (Eq 1, p.3). Steady state solves (L + Wa + Wb)x = wa (p.3); vote share X is Eq 2 (p.4). Controller A is active, controller B passive, both with budgets (Eq 3, p.4). Theoretical lenses: "shadowing" (first-order, target the opponent's nodes), "shielding" (second-order, target neighbours of the opponent's nodes), and degree preference (hub targeting). Analytical solution via a heterogeneous mean-field (HMF) approximation (Eq 4, p.5; Eqs 10-12, p.14) with series expansions in the low-allocation (w << d) and high-allocation (w >> d) limits (pp.6-8). Proof of concavity and uniqueness of the Nash equilibrium is deferred to S1 Appendix Section 12.

**Data.** Single empirical network: email interchange network of members of the Universitat Rovira i Virgili (Tarragona), N = 1133 nodes, taken from Guimerà et al. 2003 (ref 49; S1 Dataset). Unit of analysis: individual node (agent). Time period: not stated in the article. Edge count: not stated in the text. Synthetic/other topologies are referenced only to S1 Appendix Section 11 and Figs 10, 12, 13, which are not in the text file. Budget convention: B_a + B_b = N<d>/30 in most experiments; Fig 1 uses B = N<d>/60 per controller. Passive opponent typically targets K = 16 random nodes (discrete); K values up to 256 are used in Fig 3 and K varies in Fig 1b-c.

**Method.** Numerical optimisation for the active controller. Continuous: gradient ascent with step size mu = 50 and tolerance 1e-10, projection onto the N-simplex, backtracking, O(N^3/epsilon) cost (Methods, pp.15-16). Discrete: stochastic hill-climbing for 20N iterations or until no change in the last 10N (p.16). Analytical: HMF approximation and series expansions in the low and high allocation limits (Eqs 5-8, pp.7-8). Game-theoretic: iterated elimination of dominated strategies with gradient ascent to find the pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (p.13). Heuristics compared by vote-share gap ΔX against the numerical optimum, with binary-search tuning of heuristic parameters (pp.16-17, Table 1). Reported results are averages over 15 instances (K=16, Fig 6) or 15 and 60 instances elsewhere (Fig 4, Fig 5).

**Identification.** Not applicable in the econometric sense. No causal or statistical identification strategy. The "explanatory" claim rests on (a) visual and group-overlap analysis of numerical optima (Figs 1, 3, 4), (b) rank correlations between allocation and degree (Fig 5), and (c) comparing vote-share gaps of heuristics to the numerical optimum (Fig 6).

**Scope.** Population: one 1133-node email network (URV Tarragona), treated as a typical social-network topology; the paper claims results generalise to complex networks (S1 Appendix, not checked here). Institution: none; the network is an academic email network, not a political population. Era: not stated; the reference data is from 2003. Political relevance is framed only in general terms ("political competition", "winner-takes-all scenarios such as political elections or referendums", p.6). The model is abstract opinion dynamics, not calibrated to any election or lobbying outcome.

## Key findings

- Optimal continuous allocations are spread widely across the network rather than concentrated on a few hubs. Around 111 of 1133 nodes (|H|+|A|, about 10%) get above-average allocation; 1013 nodes (V) are mostly flat (std/mean = 0.11); 9 nodes get zero (text also says 7 at p.5). Allocations range from 0.1 to 5. (Results, 'Structure of optimal allocations in the continuous regime', p.5, lines 285-308, Fig 1a (K=16, equal budgets))
- Entropy of the continuous optimal allocation distribution is considerably higher than for discrete allocations, across K and budget ratios. The gap widens as the passive controller spreads over more nodes. (Results p.5-6, Fig 1b, lines 310-336 (entropy normalised by log N))
- Continuous allocation improves vote share over the best discrete allocation. Gain is about 5-10% for passive K < 100 and falls below 5% for K > 100. (Results p.6, Fig 1c, lines 337-345; measure (X_cont - X_disc)/X_disc)
- Discrete optima are driven mainly by node degree (hub targeting); in the continuous regime shadowing plus shielding is the main explanatory factor, with degree effects weaker across scenarios. (Results p.12, lines 737-755; Discussion p.13, lines 792-795)
- Shielding explains the group structure of continuous optima: very high (H) and zero (Z) allocations fall mainly on the opponent's targets (Tb); above-average (A) on their neighbours (Nb); below-average (V) on the rest (R). (Results p.10, lines 607-613, Fig 4a (K=16); Fig 4 caption p.9)
- Shielding is second-order and weakens as K grows. With budget disadvantage, anti-shielding (avoiding neighbours of opponent targets) also appears. (Results pp.9-10, lines 579-581 and 614-628, Fig 4b-c)
- Degree correlation of optimal allocations is a by-product of shielding. Kendall tau between allocation and degree is above 0.5 for K > 30 in both optimal and pure-shielding strategies, but below |0.25| for optimal allocations when the passive controller targets low-degree-neighbour nodes. Shielding then gives tau down to about -0.5. (Results pp.10-11, lines 646-681, Fig 5b-c)
- Heuristic vote-share gaps relative to the numerical optimum (K=16 passive, discrete). Budget advantage: continuous shadowing+shielding best at 1.89e-3 (shadowing alone 2.55e-3; discrete shadowing 1.58e-2; discrete degree 2.41e-2). Budget equality: discrete degree 8.99e-3 closest; continuous shadowing+shielding 2.97e-3; other continuous heuristics 5.5e-3 to 7.0e-3. Budget disadvantage: discrete degree 2.12e-3; continuous shadowing+shielding 7.28e-4; other continuous 2.33e-3 to 4.44e-3. (Results pp.11-12, lines 711-734, Fig 6 (caption p.12))
- Analytical low-allocation limit (w << d): optimal w_ai is linear in opponent allocation w_bi and degree d_i with coefficient (Ba/Bb - 1). Positive coefficient (Ba > Bb) gives shadowing; negative (Ba < Bb) gives avoidance. Degree dependence only enters when positivity constraints bind, and then it favours low-degree nodes. (Results p.7, Eq 5, lines 414-465)
- Vote share in the low limit is X_L = Ba/(Ba+Bb) + Bb/(4N^2) * sum_i (1 - w_bi/<w_b>)^2 / d_i, with <w_b> = Bb/N. A passive controller is more exploitable the more her allocation deviates from uniform, and the penalty is larger on low-degree nodes. (Results p.7-8, Eq 6, lines 466-486)
- High limit (w >> d): optimal allocation depends on opponent allocation non-linearly and on budget, with no degree dependence. Vote share X_H = 1 - (Bb/(Ba+Bb)) * (1/N) sum_i (sqrt(w_bi/<w_b>) - 1)^2 (sic; as printed). Exploitability is independent of which nodes are misallocated. (Results pp.7-8, Eqs 7-8, lines 487-539)
- Two active controllers: the unique pure-strategy Nash equilibrium is that both controllers target all nodes equally, w_ai = Ba/N and w_bi = Bb/N, regardless of network type and budgets. Numerically, normalised std of allocations converges to zero (S1 Appendix Fig 7). (Results p.13, lines 758-782; Abstract p.1 lines 20-22)
- Gradient ascent is guaranteed to reach an epsilon-approximate solution in O(1/epsilon) iterations. Each step needs an N x N inversion, so cost is O(N^3/epsilon). Experiments used mu = 50, epsilon = 1e-10; all runs converged within 1e5 iterations. (Methods p.15-16, lines 942-982)

## Limitations

- Strategies are time-invariant; campaigns that vary allocation over time are not modelled (p.14, lines 849-852; extensions cited to refs 54, 57).
- Only one empirical network (URV email, N = 1133), described as well-mixed, without strong communities, and with little degree assortativity (p.14, lines 846-848). Generalisation to other topologies rests on S1 Appendix, which is not in the text file.
- Only the voter model is studied. Ising and SIR extensions are proposed but not done (p.14, lines 844-846).
- No noise in opinion dynamics. The authors note degree dependence might arise with noise, citing Lynn & Lee (p.14, lines 833-841).
- The main analysis assumes a passive opponent. Two-active-controller results are limited to the uniform Nash equilibrium (p.13).
- The HMF approximation is not always valid, ignores higher-order correlations, and is only reasonable for not overly sparse networks (p.15, lines 886-889). Analytical expressions rest on series expansions in the low and high allocation limits (p.7, lines 409-413).
- Discrete optima come from stochastic hill-climbing, which only guarantees a global optimum for convex problems (p.16, lines 993-995). Discrete comparisons may therefore be sub-optimal.
- Internal inconsistency: the count of zero-allocation nodes is |Z| = 9 at p.5 line 302 and |Z| = 7 at line 307 on the same page.
- Political application is framed only qualitatively (winner-takes-all elections, p.6). No empirical election, lobbying or campaign data is used.
- Heuristics need network structure, opponent strategy, or parameter tuning that a real campaigner may lack (Table 1, p.17).

## Gaps and flags

- Dollars do not propagate in the flip model or the joint optimizer: CU dollars change only the funded member's pflip (optimize.py:64-72); only conversion events (shift_pro) alter neighbours' camp totals. The paper's main mechanism, shielding the neighbours of opponent-targeted nodes, therefore cannot appear in any allocation the system produces.
- Opponent allocation is fixed (ind = fm.ind0 at optimize.py:59), so the allocator best-responds to a frozen opponent. The system has no test of how its plan fares if industry PACs re-optimise; the paper's two-active-controller result says that equilibrium is uniform allocation.
- An opponent-share field w_b (build_opposition_prior.py:1425-1440) is labelled for a 'shadowing layer', and contested_bills.py ships a counterparty registry, but no engine consumes either (contested_bills.py:9 says 'no consumer exists today').
- The paper says the direction of the best response depends on budget ratio Ba/Bb. The system never computes per-bill or per-topic CU vs counterparty budget ratios, so it cannot tell whether shadowing or avoidance would apply.
- The paper's exploitability result (Eq 6) weights each node's deviation by 1/d_i, so misallocating low-degree nodes matters more: a controller is more exploitable when it neglects low-degree nodes. The system has no diagnostic of where industry money departs from uniform relative to network degree.
- Flag: Citation scope: a registry citation of 'Romero Moreno 2021' could not be verified in the repo. The paper is an abstract, unnoised voter model on one 1,133-node academic email network, with no lobbying, election or legislative data. It supports the concept of an opponent-aware allocation, not any parameter or effect size.
- Flag: D-025 wording: the paper's 'influence' and 'vote share' are steady-state model outputs. Any shadowing or shielding score built from it must be described as a heuristic model output, not closeness, access or the probability of a vote.
- Flag: D-021: an opponent allocation w_b for shadowing must come from FEC money (build_opposition_prior.py's current basis is relational associated exposure on a 10-company-per-industry sample, not FEC opposition money as such), never from LDA spend used as a weight.
- Flag: DE-062: the claim that emergent shadowing would follow from the b term of a/(a+b) in the optimizer is this agent's inference.
- Flag: The paper's internal inconsistency (|Z| = 9 at line 302 vs 7 at line 307) and its reliance on an unread S1 Appendix for other topologies limit how far its generality claims can be relied on.
- Flag: No conflict with the rejected SNA proposals: the paper argues neither for dropping regular cosponsorship edges nor for per-edge volume normalisation.

The paper gives opponent-aware allocation a formal shape: the response to a rival should depend on where the rival spends and on the budget ratio. It goes beyond the current system in two ways. First, the right response can be to avoid the rival's targets when CU is outspent. Second, a frozen-opponent plan is exploitable once the rival also optimises. The system holds an unconsumed w_b 'shadowing layer' (a relational associated-exposure basis on a 10-company-per-industry sample, not industry-complete) and a display-only registry, but nothing expresses the paper's main mechanism, shielding, in its node-local money term.
