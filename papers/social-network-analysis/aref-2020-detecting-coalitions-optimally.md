# Detecting coalitions by optimally partitioning signed networks of political collaboration

**Samin Aref, Zachary Neal (2020).** *Scientific Reports, 10:1506 (published online 30 January 2020).* [10.1038/s41598-020-58471-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-58471-z). Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Aref and Neal build one signed network per chamber per Congress (96th to 114th, 1979-2016). Each network comes from a stochastic-degree-sequence (SDSM) backbone of bill cosponsorship: a dyad that cosponsors together significantly more often than the null predicts gets a positive edge, and one that does so significantly less often gets a negative edge. They then solve the two-group frustration-minimization problem exactly. An LP relaxation with triangle constraints gives a lower bound, a party-label warm start gives an upper bound, and a binary program returns the exact frustration index and the optimal partition. Both balance measures are high and rising over the period (the triangle and normalized frustration indices correlate at 0.95 in the House and 0.91 in the Senate), which the authors read as polarization. Exact solves are cheap for the Senate (about 4 s) and expensive for the House (up to about 13.5 h for the 113th Congress, which has 75,771 edges). The larger side of the optimal partition, the controlling coalition, became more partisan over time. In two structural equation mediation models, each built from bivariate regressions (19 sessions per chamber; association, not cause), coalition partisanship partly offsets the House decline in passage rates (indirect beta 0.510). Majority size does not mediate it, and the Senate shows no effect.
>
> For Paul's systems the paper matters mainly for the diagnostic signed-coalition layer. That layer cites the 2021 follow-up, runs a heuristic local search with no lower bound on an LLM-valenced mention graph, and pools both chambers. At its size (520 members; 7,153 edges in the Jul 19 artifact snapshot, 6,242 unique signed pairs in a fresh recount) the paper's exact binary program looks tractable. That estimate is derived here, not run. The paper does not discuss the project's earlier vote-SDSM-backbone result; reading it as expected under structural balance is analyst inference from the paper's qualitative polarization finding. The paper does not license the SDSM null as a fix for the network's degree artifact. That would amount to the per-edge volume normalization the project already measured and rejected.

## Study

**Question:** Can the members of internally cohesive, mutually antagonistic coalitions in a signed network of legislators be identified exactly by optimally partitioning the network to minimize frustration? And does the partisan composition of the largest such coalition explain changes in legislative effectiveness (bill passage) better than party control or party affiliation does?

**Theory:** Structural balance theory (Heider 1944; Cartwright and Harary 1956), with the frustration index (Harary 1959) as the global balance measure, and coalition theory (Riker 1962). Substantive framing draws on the legislative effectiveness literature (Volden and Wiseman; Frantzich; Olson and Nonidez) and on Mayhew's claim that parties are not sufficient analytic units. Central substantive hypothesis: a more partisan, ideologically unified controlling coalition improves passage rates (protective factor), while party majority size does not.

**Data:** Signed networks of US House and Senate members, one network per chamber per Congressional session, sessions 96th to 114th (1979-2016), i.e. 19 sessions per chamber and about 38 networks (derived count). Edges are signed dyads built from bill co-sponsorship: a negative edge is a dyad whose joint co-sponsorships are significantly few under a stochastic degree sequence model null (two-tailed alpha 0.05); a positive edge is significantly many. Unit of analysis: legislator dyads within a session-chamber network, with session-level aggregates for the regressions. Network data are from Neal (2019, figshare, reference 4). Legislator counts per chamber are not stated in the main text. Example scale: the 113th House graph has m = 75,771 edges and 7,102,625 triangles. Pre-96th sessions were excluded because of the 25-cosponsor House rule before 1979. Bill passage rate is the share of introduced bills that become law, per chamber per session. Party data come from exogenous legislator party affiliation. The printed size bound for the dense-graph method appears as "|E| ... 50000" with the relation symbol lost in extraction, so the exact bound is unverified.

**Method:** Two partial-balance measures on each network: the triangle index T(G) (Eq. 3, trace-based, fraction of positive triangles) and the normalized frustration index F(G) = 1 - 2L(G)/m (Eq. 6), where L(G) is the frustration index, i.e. the minimum number of intra-group negative plus inter-group positive edges over all two-group partitions (Eqs. 4-5). Exact computation uses (a) a continuous LP relaxation with triangle inequality constraints (Eq. 7) to give a lower bound Y*, solved in Gurobi 8.0; (b) a warm start that initializes each node's group from party affiliation, which also gives an upper bound; and (c) a binary linear program (Eq. 8, XOR-style frustration variables with the lower bound added as a constraint) that returns the exact frustration index and the optimal partition X*. The larger side of X* is the controlling coalition, and coalition partisanship is the fraction of its non-independent members who belong to its dominant party. Inference is cross-sectional, session by session. Two models: a commonsense model (party control = absolute difference between Republican and Democratic seat counts) and a coalition model (coalition partisanship). Each is estimated as a structural equation mediation model with time as the predictor and passage rate as the outcome, using bivariate regressions and standardized betas (unstandardized percentage-point slopes reported for context).

**Identification:** None causal. The mediation models use time and session-level cross-sections, so the indirect effect (time to coalition partisanship to passage rate) is a statistical decomposition, not an identified causal effect. Exogenous party labels are used to warm-start the solver and as a comparison benchmark, not as an instrument.

**Scope:** US Congress (House and Senate), 96th-114th sessions, 1979-2016. Population is legislators in co-sponsorship networks; the inferred ties cover only that period. Results are claimed for the House only on the effectiveness question; the Senate shows no support for the coalition model. The computational method is general to signed graphs with up to a stated dense-graph size. The Senate networks are small enough that exact solves took 4 seconds on average.

## Key findings

- Triangle index and normalized frustration index both show high partial balance that rose over 1979-2016 in both chambers, read as increasing polarization. The two measures are highly correlated. (Correlation of T(G) and F(G) is 0.95 for the House and 0.91 for the Senate (as printed, 'respectively for House and Senate').; Results, Partial balance (p.5), Fig. 2; Summary (p.7))
- Exact frustration index and optimal partition are computable for dense House networks, but the cost is large. The Senate is cheap. (113th House: LP bound (Eq. 7) takes 5,300 s with 28,486,271 constraints; binary model (Eq. 8) has n+m = 76,218 binary variables and 2m+1 = 151,543 constraints and takes 43,523 s; total 48,823 s (about 13.5 h), the longest House instance. House average 17,763 s (SD 15,411 s). Senate average 4 s (SD 6 s).; Results, Computation (p.5-6); Bounding (p.4-5))
- Controlling coalitions (largest side of the optimal partition) became more partisan (more homogeneous) over 1979-2016 in both chambers. (No regression coefficient reported in the text for this descriptive trend; Fig. 3 shows counts of the two main parties in the controlling coalition.; Results, Partial balance and optimal partitioning (p.6), Fig. 3)
- Bill passage rates declined over time in both chambers. (House: -0.11 percentage points per session (standardized beta = -0.528, p < 0.05). Senate: -0.35 percentage points per session (beta = -0.852, p < 0.01).; Results, Mediation in bill passage (p.6), Fig. 5(A))
- The commonsense explanation fails: party control (absolute majority size) does not mediate the time-passage relation in either chamber. (No significant mediation reported; text says party control does not mediate the relationship between time and passage rate.; Results (p.7), Fig. 5(B))
- In the House, coalition partisanship mediates the decline in passage rates. Greater partisan homogeneity of the controlling coalition is a protective factor, so the decline would have been worse without it. (Time to coalition partisanship: beta = 0.771, p < 0.01. Coalition partisanship to passage: beta = 0.661, p < 0.05. Indirect effect of time on passage via coalition partisanship: beta = 0.510, p < 0.05. Direct effect: beta = -1.038, p < 0.01 (as printed in the paper text).; Results, Mediation (p.7), Fig. 5(C); Conclusions (p.7-8))
- The coalition-partisanship mediation is not supported in the Senate. The authors tie this to Senate procedure, where lack of majority control of procedure negates party or group effects. (No significant Senate mediation effect reported.; Results (p.7); Conclusions (p.8), citing Monroe, Roberts and Rohde (ref 43))
- The composition of the largest cohesive coalition matters more than the size of the majority party's majority in a chamber where a large group can shape procedure. The authors frame this as a claim about analytic units beyond parties. (Qualitative conclusion; no additional statistics.; Conclusions (p.8))

## Limitations

- The paper has no separate limitations section. Limits stated in the text: (1) legislator ties are inferred from co-sponsorship and are not observed collaboration; co-sponsorship can also indicate avoidance (p.8). (2) Networks are analyzed cross-sectionally, one per session, because of joiners and leavers and the difficulty of estimating dynamic models; the authors argue each session's effectiveness can be evaluated independently (p.9). (3) Pre-96th sessions are excluded because of the 25-cosponsor House rule, and the co-sponsor data do not separate sponsors from co-sponsors (p.8-9). (4) The coalition effect holds only for the House, not the Senate (p.8). (5) The exact method is computationally heavy for large House graphs (13.5 h for one instance), and the authors note heuristic methods are not guaranteed to be optimal (p.2). (6) The mediation models are bivariate and session-level, so they support association and not causation. (7) Figure values were read from captions and printed text only; the large direct-effect coefficient (beta = -1.038, p<0.01) is printed in the paper text.

## Gaps and flags

- No certified optimum. signed_coalitions.py reports only local-search upper bounds, with no lower bound or exact solve, although the paper's binary program (Eq. 8) with a party warm start appears tractable at 520 nodes (7,153 edges in the Jul 19 artifact, 6,242 unique signed pairs on recount). That scale estimate is derived here, not run.
- No comparable balance metric over time. The layer does not compute the normalized frustration index F(G) = 1 - 2L/m or the triangle index T(G) per Congress or per window, so balance trends cannot be tracked.
- No separation by chamber. The House and Senate go into one partition, although the paper's central substantive result differs between the chambers.
- No signed cosponsorship backbone in the Python chain. The SDSM signed backbone exists only for roll-call votes in Draft Systems (_vote_backbone.R), and the Phase 2 cosponsor x stance signed blend lives only in the workbook 'Signed Coalitions' sheet.
- No coalition-level outcome test. Nothing links partition composition (for example, the partisanship of the largest coalition) to CU-bill advancement or passage, which is the paper's validation step for using a partition at all.

- REJECTED-PROPOSAL OVERLAP: the SDSM backbone conditions each tie on member cosponsorship volume and bill size, so in effect it is a per-edge volume and degree correction. The project measured and rejected per-edge volume normalization (STATE.md ~908-912; layer-toggles note line 41). Do not recommend SDSM as a fix for the cascade or Katz weights. At most it is a significance-filtered signed overlay.
- DOC DRIFT: the component card says 'R backbone sdsm (alpha .05, signed) ... seeded by positive Louvain'. The live fec/network/signed_coalitions.py:24-31,74-77 partitions an LLM-valenced MENTION graph from party and random seeds. The SDSM backbone exists only for votes (Draft Systems/_vote_backbone.R, with a BH correction), and the Louvain seed belonged to the June workbook Phase 2 version.
- CITATION: the code docstring cites Aref & Neal 2021. This 2020 paper covers only the two-group case and the controlling coalition, not the 2021 'hidden third coalition'.
- WORDING: the k=2 splinter appears on the member desk as an 'antag' marker. It is an uncertified heuristic partition of mention valence, pooled across chambers, and should carry an UNSTRATIFIED label. It is not measured antagonism or closeness (D-025 spirit).
- EVIDENCE GRADE: the paper's mediation results are bivariate and session-level (19 sessions per chamber), with no causal identification. The direct effect, beta = -1.038, is printed in the paper text (p<0.01).
- Tractability estimates in this note (about 7.7k binary variables and about 14.3k constraints for Eq. 8; about 23.5k triangles and about 100k LP rows for Eq. 7 by a read-only recount) are derived here, not run. About 23M is the complete-graph count C(520,3), not the live graph.

Paul has not annotated this paper, and no Paul notes on it exist. The exact method is cheap for Senate-sized graphs (about 4 s) and very costly for dense House graphs (up to about 13.5 h). The system's graph (520 nodes) falls on the cheap side, though the 7,153-edge count comes from a Jul 19 artifact snapshot and a fresh recount gives 6,242 unique signed pairs. The paper's one-network-per-session design and normalized frustration index allow balance to be compared across sessions, which the system still lacks. The live signed layer runs on mention valence rather than cosponsorship. This paper's 'controlling coalition' is the large partisan bloc, not an underdog set; the 'hidden third coalition' belongs to the 2021 paper.
