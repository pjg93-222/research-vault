# Network analysis of House committees

**Mason A. Porter, Peter J. Mucha, M. E. J. Newman, Casey M. Warmbrand (2005).** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), vol. 102, no. 20, pp. 7057-7062 (published May 17, 2005; approved April 5, 2005). Classified Applied Mathematics; submitted via PNAS Track II..* DOI: [10.1073/pnas.0500191102](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0500191102). Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Porter, Mucha, Newman and Warmbrand (PNAS 2005) build a two-mode network of House members and committees for the 101st-108th Congresses and project it onto committee pairs. Each edge is weighted by a "normalized interlock": the number of shared members divided by the number expected if committees of those sizes were filled at random. Single-linkage clustering then finds about four levels of structure: subcommittees, committees, groups of committees, and the whole House. Subcommittees of the same parent form tight cliques; the Intelligence cluster has interlocks of 14.4-26.6 times chance. A separate rank-2 SVD of the 107th House roll-call matrix (990 votes) splits into a bipartisan axis (45.3% of the sum of squares) and a partisan axis (29.6%). It reproduces 92.7% of individual votes, close to Poole-Rosenthal's Optimal Classification at 92.8%. Committees differ in partisan extremism: Rules is the second most extreme of the 19 standing committees (after Judiciary), Select Homeland Security is 4th of all 113, and Intelligence and its subcommittees are among the 10 least extreme. The analysis is descriptive. No interlock, cluster or the "not random" conclusion has a significance test.
>
> The paper bears on the built system in three places. (1) The committee pillar adds every subcommittee seat on top of the parent seat at one-sixth weight, and Porter's one example (Intelligence, 107th House) has each subcommittee holding at least half of the parent committee's members. (2) In the say-do gate, one recorded Yea on a roll-call vote can corroborate a Champion cell, yet the leading structure in the 107th House roll-call matrix is near-unanimous bipartisan agreement. (3) The path-finding template cites Sotoudeh-Porter-Krishnagopal, not Porter 2005, and no code uses committee co-membership or a community partition. Melange has no committee co-membership layer; committees enter only as a scalar pillar. Porter's normalized interlock reweights each edge by size. That must not be carried over to cosponsor edges, because per-edge normalization of cosponsor edges was measured and REJECTED.

## Study

**Question.** Are U.S. House committee assignments random, or are important committees "stacked" with partisan members? Can network theory applied to committee interlocks (common membership), combined with roll-call SVD, reveal organizational structure and correlations with political positions without using outside political information?

**Theory.** No formal theory is tested. The introduction contrasts several political-science theories of committee assignment (qualitative and some quantitative; refs 1-6) and notes there is no consensus on how assignments are set or changed between Congresses. The authors draw an analogy to corporate board interlocks, where shared membership is linked to diffusion of political donations, investment strategies and stock-market effects (refs 9-18). Working assumption: a committee-pair connection's strength equals shared membership relative to chance, so structure can be read off the membership data alone.

**Data.** Sample: U.S. House of Representatives, 101st-108th Congresses (1989-2004), committee and subcommittee assignments (Clerk of the House site). Roll calls: 102nd-107th from Voteview; 101st from ICPSR. Units: Representatives (two-mode nodes) and committees/subcommittees (called "committees"). Detailed example is the 107th House (2001-2002): 444 Representatives including midterm replacements (432 used in the SVD ranking; 443 in the Poole-Rosenthal comparison), 990 roll-call votes (749 used in the Optimal Classification comparison after removing votes with under 0.5% minority), and 113 committees (19 standing plus subcommittees and select committees). The 108th Congress is mentioned only as "data not shown" (Homeland Security grew to 50 members). Intelligence committee has 20 members.

**Method.** 1) Build a two-mode Representative-by-committee assignment network. 2) Project onto a one-mode committee network; edge weight = "normalized interlock" = observed common members / expected common members if same-size committees were assigned independently at random from the House. Value 1 = chance, 2 = twice chance, 0 = no overlap. 3) Single-linkage hierarchical clustering on normalized interlocks; dendrogram (Fig. 2) read as levels. 4) Visualization by a variant of Kamada-Kawai spring embedding that uses link strengths (Fig. 1). 5) Voting matrix B (n Representatives x m votes; +1 yea, -1 nay, 0 absent/abstain); SVD gives leading eigenvectors u(k), v(k) and singular values; a rank-2 approximation (Eq. 3) is used. Partisan coordinate = eigenvector that tracks party; bipartisan = eigenvector that groups nearly everyone. 6) Extremism e_i = |p_i - mean p| on the partisan coordinate; committee extremism = mean over members; clusters averaged likewise. 7) Vote-outcome reconstruction: sum the rank-2 approximated votes per vote; positive = pass. 8) Compare individual-vote classification with Poole-Rosenthal Optimal Classification (107th only).

**Identification.** None. The design is descriptive. The random-assignment baseline in normalized interlock is the only null model; no significance test or randomization distribution is reported for any interlock value, extremism ranking, or SVD component. The conclusion that committees are "stacked" rests on departures from that baseline, not on an estimated test.

**Scope.** Population: all House members and committee assignments in the 101st-108th Congresses (1989-2004); detailed illustration uses the 107th House only. Institution: U.S. House; Senate, party leadership decisions and assignment rules are not modeled. Era: 1989-2004. Partisan control: Democrats 101st-103rd; Republicans 104th-108th (Table 1 lists leadership).

## Key findings

- In the 107th House, the four Intelligence subcommittees and their parent committee form a tight clique. Their normalized interlocks fall in 14.4-26.6, far above chance, and the five nodes sit close together in the Fig. 1 layout. normalized interlock 14.4-26.6; five nodes (Networks of Committees, PNAS p. 7057; Fig. 1 caption p. 7058)
- Select Committee on Homeland Security (formed June 2002, nine members) is strongly tied to the 13-member Rules Committee, via two shared members. Its tie to the Legislative and Budget Process Subcommittee of Rules, via the same two members, is stronger still. Rules: normalized interlock 7.4 (2 common members); Legislative and Budget Process Subcommittee: 13.7 (same 2 members) (Networks of Committees, PNAS pp. 7057-7058)
- In the 108th Congress the Homeland Security Committee grew to 50 members but kept a close association with Rules. The paper reports this only as data not shown. 50 members; normalized interlock 4.1 from 6 common members (Networks of Committees, PNAS p. 7058)
- Homeland Security has only weak ties to the Intelligence Committee and none to its subcommittees, despite overlapping-jurisdiction expectations. 1 common member with Intelligence, normalized interlock 2.4; interlock 0 with all four Intelligence subcommittees (Structure of the House, PNAS p. 7059)
- Single-linkage clustering of committee interlocks yields essentially four hierarchical levels (subcommittees, committees, groups of committees, entire House), with a possible weaker fifth level of subcommittee groups inside large standing committees. 4 levels, possible 5th (weak) (Structure of the House, PNAS p. 7059, Fig. 2 (dendrogram, 107th House))
- A rank-2 SVD captures most of the 107th House voting structure. The leading eigenvector accounts for 45.3% and the second for 29.6% of the sum of squares of the voting matrix; the paper says no other eigenvector exceeds a bound it gives as 1.6% (inequality glyph lost in extraction). 45.3% + 29.6% = 74.9%; remaining eigenvectors reported at a small bound of 1.6% (Voting Patterns, PNAS p. 7059-7060, Eqs. 1-3)
- The partisan coordinate is the first eigenvector in the 101st-105th Houses and the second in the 106th-107th Houses. The other (bipartisan) coordinate groups nearly all members together and tracks motions most of the House approves or disapproves together. partisan = 1st eigenvector (101st-105th), 2nd (106th-107th) (Voting Patterns, PNAS p. 7060 (Fig. 3 caption and text))
- The two-coordinate reconstruction predicts the outcome of 984 of 990 votes (99.4%). Excluding absences and abstentions it gets 975 of 990. Under the most conservative test, individual yea/nay signs are correct for 92.7% of votes and 939 (94.8%) vote outcomes are right. Always guessing pass gets 74.2% (735 of 990). Similar results hold for the 101st-106th Houses. 984/990 (99.4%); 975/990 excl. absences; 939 (94.8%) conservative; 92.7% individual votes; baseline 735 (74.2%) (Voting Patterns, PNAS pp. 7060-7061)
- Against Poole and Rosenthal's Optimal Classification on the 107th House, the SVD ranking classifies 92.7% of individual votes correctly (432 Representatives, all 990 roll calls). Optimal Classification classifies 92.8% (443 Representatives, 749 roll calls). SVD 92.7% vs OC 92.8%; 432 vs 443 Representatives; 990 vs 749 roll calls (Voting Patterns, Table 2 caption, PNAS p. 7060-7061)
- By mean extremism, the Select Committee on Homeland Security ranks above all 19 standing committees and is 4th of 113 committees. Rules is the second most extreme standing committee (after Judiciary) and 18th of 113 overall. The Intelligence Committee and its four subcommittees rank among the 10 least extreme of 113. Homeland Security 4th of 113; Rules 18th of 113 (2nd of 19 standing); Intelligence + 4 subcommittees in bottom 10 of 113 (Voting Patterns, PNAS p. 7062 (Fig. 2 color coding))
- Fig. 4 projects votes onto the two leading eigenvectors. Passed and failed measures separate cleanly. The authors read the four corners as broad bipartisan support, support mostly from the right (passed because Republicans held the majority), support mostly from the left (failed under Democratic minority), and near-universal opposition. qualitative; no separation statistic reported (Voting Patterns, PNAS p. 7060, Fig. 4 caption)
- Authors conclude that committee assignments are not random and that some committees are stacked, and that the interlock and voting analysis indicates the degree of departure from randomness. No statistic for that departure is reported. no test statistic reported (Conclusions, PNAS p. 7062)

## Limitations

- Stated: the 108th Congress results are shown as data not shown.
- Stated: the authors discuss only a few observations in detail and say a rich variety of other results can be derived from similar analyses.
- Stated: the SVD reconstruction is imperfect (92.7% of individual yea/nay signs correct under the strictest measure; 94.8% of outcomes under the conservative test).
- Stated: the partisan coordinate "corresponds closely" to party affiliation, not perfectly.
- Stated: extremism is measured relative to the House mean, which is usually skewed slightly toward the majority party.
- Stated: the Optimal Classification comparison is applied only to the 107th House.
- Stated: the Fig. 1 layout is a variant of Kamada-Kawai, so spatial closeness is approximate.
- Observation (not stated by the paper; the paper has no limitations section): no significance test or randomization null is reported for interlock values, clusters, or the stacking conclusion; the 'not random' claim is therefore descriptive. Single session emphasis (107th) and no analysis of assignment mechanisms, leadership, or seniority rules.

## Gaps and flags

- The system has no committee co-membership (two-mode member x committee) layer. Committees reach the engines only as a scalar pillar, so the hierarchy and cross-committee interlocks Porter documents never enter cascades or path-finding.
- The roll-call vote evidence (vote_category_lean.json) has no contestedness or minority-share filter. Porter and Poole-Rosenthal show that near-unanimous votes dominate the roll-call matrix and should be screened out before votes are used to tell members apart (the Optimal Classification comparison drops votes with a small minority share).
- The committee pillar ignores partisan composition and stacking, and treats subcommittee seats as additive, although Porter's one example (Intelligence, 107th House) shows subcommittees overlapping heavily with the parent.
- Nothing in the system builds a member ideal-point or partisan/bipartisan coordinate from roll calls. Porter's SVD gets 92.7% individual-vote reproduction from two coordinates, which would be a cheap baseline for checking whether stance labels add anything beyond party or ideology on CU votes.
- There is no random-assignment null for any co-membership edge (committee, caucus or staffer container) against which to judge whether a tie is above chance.
- Porter's normalized interlock (observed/expected shared members) is a per-edge size normalization. Per-edge normalization was measured and REJECTED for the cosponsor network (brain topics/2026-06-feature-cosponsor-and-sector-networks.md:101). Do not recommend porting it to cosponsor edges; at most it belongs on a new committee layer.
- Porter's SVD is an unnormalized SVD benchmarked against Optimal Classification, not DW-NOMINATE. Paul rejected DW-NOMINATE vote edges for the signed coalitions (same brain note, line 93). Do not re-propose SVD-derived vote edges without a new ruling.
- Citation drift: the path-finding template cites Sotoudeh-Porter-Krishnagopal (line 738), not Porter 2005, and the built code has no community partition; Porter 2005 analyzes committee co-membership. Whether the component records cite Porter 2005 for community structure was not located. The Porter-lineage paper on cosponsorship is Sotoudeh-Porter-Krishnagopal 2024.
- Committee 'extremism' and 'stacking' in Porter are descriptive, with no significance test or randomization null. Any wording that committees 'are stacked' should carry that label and its window (107th House, 113 committees).
- The say-do bearing is a candidate check, not a measured defect. The share of lopsided votes behind Path V corroborations has not been computed (DE-062).

Paul's takeaways broadly match the paper (strong within-committee ties, a general hierarchy, and partisan alignment correlating with committee selection), with one misreading. He describes "a two-mode committee network and a one-mode representative network", but the paper projects the two-mode network onto a committee-to-committee network; representatives are not projected. His Weakness 1 (chicken-or-egg causality) is correct and stronger than he states: the paper has no identification and no significance test at all. His Weakness 2 (can members and committees share one graph?) is answered by the paper itself, since the two-mode assignment network is exactly that graph. His inferred system link, "multi-layer committee network for the present research", is stale against the built system. Melange has no committee co-membership layer; committees enter only as the Influence pillar scalar (committee_scores.py), and the code that cites Porter-lineage work for community structure (the path-finding template, line 738) implements no community partition. His committee-pillar position that "committees are the structural layer of the House" holds, but as built the pillar uses neither the interlock structure nor partisan composition.
