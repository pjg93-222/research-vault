# Strategic identity signaling in heterogeneous networks

**Tamara van der Does, Mirta Galesic, Zackary Okun Dunivin, Paul E. Smaldino (2022).** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), vol. 119, no. 10, e2117898119; published March 3, 2022.* [10.1073/pnas.2117898119](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2117898119). Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> van der Does, Galesic, Dunivin and Smaldino (PNAS 2022, 119(10) e2117898119) test covert signaling theory: people send identity signals that the ingroup reads but the outgroup cannot, and do so more when the audience is mostly outgroup. They built a tweet set of covert and overt left and right messages from rater gaps (1,992 MTurk raters; tweets from 2020), then ran a randomized 8-round experiment (n = 481 analysed) that varied outgroup size (1 to 9 of 10) and the cost of dislikes. With cross-partisan audiences, going from 1 to 9 outgroup members raised covert sharing (AME 0.73, 95 percent CI 0.47 to 0.99) and cut total sharing (AME -5.38). Copartisan audiences showed no effect (BF01 = 43.15), and the cost manipulation did little (BF01 = 16.83). A calibrated payoff model (R = 0.8, r_I = 0.6, r_o = 0.5) puts the covert advantage only at high outgroup share. The authors warn that automated political-speech classifiers probably catch only overt speech. For Paul's systems, the paper bears on the stance and press layers, not the network. Members facing mixed audiences may hedge, go quiet or code their messages, so the press pillar and the DEBATE stance reader could understate them. The say-do gate only lowers tiers, so it cannot correct for members who act more than they talk. The salience-shock backfire term ignores audience composition. Every transfer is UNVERIFIED (DE-062). The subjects were ordinary partisan Twitter users on a left-right axis, not legislators speaking about a largely bipartisan credit-union agenda. This paper offers nothing on graph edges, weights or reciprocity. ## Study

**Question.** Do people strategically switch from overt to covert political identity signals (signals readable by ingroup but obscured from outgroup) depending on the share of outgroup members in their audience and on the cost of being disliked? (p.3, lines 309-314 and 320-323)

**Theory.** Covert signaling theory (Smaldino, Flamson and McElreath 2018; Smaldino and Turner 2021): identity signals are accurately received by ingroup but obscured from outgroup; covert signalling is favoured when interactions with dissimilar others are likely and costly (p.2, lines 174-189). Linked to audience design and multiple-audience theory (Clark and Schaefer; Fleming and Darley) and to identity signalling (Donath; Berger) (p.2, p.7).

**Data.** Three linked samples. (1) Twitter: 73,869 follower lists of 8 news accounts; 16,398 engaged seed accounts (top 20 percent by media diet); 1,834 far-left and 1,446 far-right accounts labelled homogeneous or heterogeneous; 6,594 tweets, 5,215 randomly sampled for rating, 4,752 rated by at least 3 raters per group; tweets downloaded September 15 to October 12, 2020 (p.8-9). (2) Raters: 2,695 invited Mechanical Turk workers, 1,992 responded (483 far left, 533 moderate left, 461 far right, 515 moderate right); each rated 50 tweets (p.9). (3) Behavioral experiment: 240 far-left/right and 240 moderate participants recruited (p.9), analysed as n = 481 (238 copartisan audience, 243 cross-partisan audience, Fig. 5); each saw 40 same-side tweets over 8 rounds. Unit: tweet and participant-round. Period: experiment September 15 to November 1, 2020 (p.8). Note the 480 recruited vs 481 analysed gap is not explained in the text. Data and code: OSF doi 10.17605/OSF.IO/XMZRA, anonymized (p.8).

**Method.** Covertness is measured by rating differences: tweets are marked potentially covert when copartisan raters show a large difference in perceived orientation and affect (five criteria, SI Table S6), moderates give neutral orientation and affect, and far copartisans recognise political content; potentially overt tweets use four criteria (p.9). Selection: about 30 candidates per category, then 20 tweets in each of covert left, covert right, overt left and overt right, chosen by author judgement (p.9). Experiment: between-subject audience type (copartisan vs cross-partisan) and within-subject randomized 8 rounds varying outgroup size (1, 4, 6 or 9 of 10) and cost of dislikes (0.5 or 1 cent) (p.9). Analysis: mixed-effects Poisson models of covert and total tweets shared per round, random intercepts per individual, controls for age, gender, race, education and political group; average predictive margins and average marginal effects via the Spost13 package; Bayes factors for null effects from BIC differences (p.5). Mathematical model: expected payoffs of pure overt and covert strategies, with reception probabilities R, r_I and r_o, calibrated to the experiment, then plotted as a payoff advantage over outgroup share d (p.4, Fig. 4). Rater reliability: multilevel regression with Shapley decomposition of McFadden pseudo-R2 (p.9).

**Identification.** The Twitter stage is observational: covertness is inferred from ingroup-outgroup rating gaps, not from the author intent. The experiment is randomized: audience group, outgroup size and cost are manipulated within subject across rounds, with random ordering (p.9). Authors state they cannot separate signaler intent from receiver understanding (p.7).

**Scope.** United States only. Twitter users identified as politically engaged far-left or far-right, selected by news diet. Experiment participants are politically engaged, social-media-literate Americans across the spectrum, selected via the SI criteria (p.5). Time window: 2020 pre-election period, including the Black Lives Matter protests, Barrett confirmation and the 2020 election, with signals mostly about cross-partisan disputes (p.2, p.6, p.7). Political identity treated as left-right, unidimensional (p.8).

## Key findings

- Cross-partisan audiences: moving from 1 to 9 outgroup members raised the number of covert tweets shared, controlling total shared, with AME 0.73, 95 percent CI 0.47 to 0.99 (p.5, Fig. 5B).
- Under high cost, moving from 4 to 6 cross-partisan outgroup members gave AME 0.42, 95 percent CI 0.08 to 0.77 (p.5).
- Copartisan audiences (moderate vs radical): no effect of outgroup size on covert tweets; Bayes factor for null BF01 = 43.15 (p.5, Fig. 5A).
- Cost of dislikes: no strong effect on the outgroup-size relationship; BF01 = 16.83; maximum payout gap only 20 cents (p.5, p.6-7).
- Participants shared fewer tweets overall as cross-partisan outgroup size rose: AME -5.38, 95 percent CI -6.04 to -4.72; the authors read this as cutting overt tweets while keeping a few covert ones (p.6, Fig. 5D).
- Audience reception: covert tweets were liked less by copartisans and disliked less by cross-partisans than overt tweets, but covert tweets did not eliminate outgroup dislikes (p.3-4, Fig. 3, n = 481).
- Calibrated model: receiving probabilities R = 0.8 (overt), r_I = 0.6 (covert, ingroup) and r_o = 0.5 (covert, outgroup), against the prior-model assumption r_o = 0; covert payoff advantage is positive only when the outgroup share is high (p.4, Fig. 4).
- Model predicts the outgroup-share effect should be much larger than the cost effect; experiment matched this, with the cost effect smaller than expected (p.4-6).
- Rater reliability: multilevel model explains 50.0 percent of total variance; 89.3 percent of that is tweets, raters 10.3 percent of explained variance (5.2 percent of total) (p.9).
- Strategy change paid: cross-partisan participants who shifted toward covert tweets with more outgroup members received higher bonuses (SI Fig. S8, cited p.5).
- Political groups differ slightly: copartisan right participants used slightly more covert tweets; the moderate-to-radical gap was larger on the right; far-right participants changed strategy least (p.6-7). The authors could not explain these with groups this small.
- Observational check: a greater fraction of political tweets from heterogeneous networks were classified as covert than from homogeneous networks (SI Fig. S5, cited p.9).

## Limitations

- Network heterogeneity is operationalised by engagement with copartisan extreme news, because cross-partisan follower ties were rare and cross-partisan embeddedness practically nonexistent (p.8).
- Covertness is inferred from rater reactions, not from author intent or receiver understanding; the authors say they cannot discern intent (p.7).
- Covert and overt are treated as a dichotomy in design but are likely continuous and heterogeneously distributed (p.7-8).
- The cost manipulation (max 20 cents) may be too small to change behavior, so the cost prediction is not supported (p.6-7).
- Experiment fixes audience composition and gives no feedback between rounds; networks do not co-evolve with signalling (p.7).
- Only a left-right dimension is used; participants could not signal about other identities (p.8).
- Authors chose the final 20 tweets per category by judgement after automated preselection; two right-leaning tweets were wrongly included as covert and recoded as neither (p.9).
- Experiment ran in the 2020 pre-election period when cross-partisan disputes dominated; copartisan null may reflect the period (p.6).
- Rater noise: raters explain 5.2 percent of total variance (p.9); the paper reports reliability as high.
- Recruited 480 participants but analysed 481, unexplained in the text (p.5, p.9).
- Participants may have maximised payoff rather than shared identity; the design does not capture a genuine identity-sharing motive (p.7).
- Automated political-speech classifiers are likely to detect only overt speech and miss covert signals (p.7).

## Gaps and flags

- The stance readers have no hedged or coded class. DEBATE's neutral bin merges 'not about CUs' with ambiguous support (stage5_debate.py:29), so strategically muted statements read as sign 0.
- The press pillar and media blend carry no audience-composition covariate. District or state partisan mix is never used to interpret press volume or LSD intensity, although the paper predicts both fall with outgroup share.
- The say-do gate is one-directional, catching talk without action but never action without talk. Members who code or suppress rhetoric for mixed electorates have no detection path.
- The salience-shock backfire probability uses policy uncertainty only, not the outgroup share of the audience. The paper's model makes outgroup share the main lever.
