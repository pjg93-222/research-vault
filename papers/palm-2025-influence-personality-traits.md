# Palm and Tims: persuasion strategies and personality traits

**Sander Palm, Maria Tims (2025).** *Personality and Individual Differences, vol. 247, article 113412 (online 20 Aug 2025).* [10.1016/j.paid.2025.113412](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2025.113412). Theme: [[themes/adjacent|Adjacent]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Palm and Tims (2025) is a PRISMA-guided systematic review of 80 studies published between 1982 and September 2024. It asks whether Big Five traits moderate the effect of persuasion strategies, nearly always Cialdini's six principles. Most included studies are one-off self-report surveys of perceived persuasiveness. Samples are mainly the general public (62.5%) and students (31.25%), and the main domains are health (41.25%) and business (25%). The synthesis is narrative only: it counts studies and their reported directions, with no pooled effect sizes and no risk-of-bias appraisal.
> 
> **Main claims:**
> - Strategies matched to a trait outperform no strategy, and mismatched strategies can backfire.
> - Ranked by susceptibility to persuasion, agreeableness is highest, then extraversion, conscientiousness and openness, with neuroticism lowest (Table 2).
> - Even so, one included study (Oyibo & Vassileva 2019) found neuroticism the trait most consistently correlated with responsiveness to social-influence strategies.
> - Only a few political studies are included (Zarouali 2022, Gerber 2013, Decker & Kramer 2023), and they disagree.
> 
> **Why it matters here:** the receptivity layer turns personality into a single susceptibility scalar. Neuroticism gets a positive weight (+.30) and extraversion or dominance a negative one (-.10). The review's ranking runs against both signs, while it supports the +.40 agreeableness slot. The caveat is that the constructs differ. Byrne's composite, which the code cites, is about decisions breaking down under pressure, not about responding to a persuasion message. The review's evidence also comes from consumers and students, not legislators. So the review is weak evidence against the N and E signs, not a calibration source.
> 
> **What it exposes:** the engine has no strategy dimension. It models one generic pressure, so the review's main result, which is about matching a strategy to a trait, has nothing in the system to attach to. This analysis is UNVERIFIED until a second path reproduces it (DE-062).

## Study

**Question.** What is the influence of personality traits on the effect of persuasion strategies on influencing individual behavior? (Abstract; Sec 1, p.2)

**Theory.** Congruence theory (message-receiver matching; Rimal & Adkins 2003; Dodoo & Wen 2019). Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo 1984; central vs peripheral route, depends on motivation and ability). Big Five / five-factor model as trait taxonomy (McCrae & John 1992). Cialdini's six principles as strategy taxonomy (Sec 2.1-2.3, pp.1-2). The review does not test these theories.

**Data.** Unit: study/article. N = 80 included articles, published 1982 to September 2024, from 59 journals (Sec 3.3; App. B). Identification: 236 unique Web of Science hits plus 48 snowballed = 284 screened; 83 excluded at title/abstract (82 not relevant, 1 not English); 201 read in full; 121 excluded; 80 included (Sec 3.2-3.3, Fig 1, p.3). Coded on 12 dimensions (Sec 3.4). Per-study sample sizes are not reported in the review except Jankovic et al. (2022), n=27 (Sec 4.1). Authors state they do not have permission to share data.

**Method.** PRISMA-guided systematic review of Web of Science using AND-combined keyword pairs (Persuasion Strategies/Technologies with Personality Traits/Big Five/Five Factor Model) plus reference-list snowballing (Sec 3.2). Title/abstract then full-text screening; English only; journal ranking not used (Sec 3.3). Deductive coding of 6 dimensions (strategy type, author, moderators, mediators, outcomes, results) plus search term, method, research type, domain; inductive coding of study duration and sample (Sec 3.4). Synthesis is narrative: counts of studies and direction of reported effects. No pooled effect sizes, meta-analysis, or formal quality/risk-of-bias appraisal is described.

**Identification.** Not applicable: review with no causal identification strategy. The review notes that much of the included research is correlational and that some designs (e.g., Gerber et al. 2013) do not allow firm causal inference (Sec 4.2.4, p.5; Sec 5.3, p.8).

**Scope.** Population: mainly general population (62.5% of studies) and students (31.25%), patients 1.25% (Sec 4, pp.2-3). Domains: health 41.25%, business 25%, sustainability 7.5% (Sec 4). Era: 1982 to September 2024, most studies 2015-2024 (Sec 3.3). Language: English. Methods: quantitative 91.25%, mixed 8.75%; surveys 80%, field experiments 12.5%, real-world data 1.25%; 80% under 1 hour (Sec 4, p.3). Culture is discussed in Sec 5 but is not one of the 12 coding dimensions.

## Key findings

- Matching trait-tailored strategies are more effective than non-matching (no strategy); contra-matching (random, non-ideal strategy) is often counterproductive, not merely ineffective. No effect sizes reported. (Count-based narrative; Jankovic et al. 2022 found no significant difference (n=27); Abstract p.1; Sec 4.1 p.3; Sec 5 p.6)
- Susceptibility differs by Big Five trait: agreeableness most susceptible, neuroticism least. (Table 2: agreeableness High; extraversion Above average; conscientiousness Average; openness Below average; neuroticism Low; Abstract p.1; Table 2 p.4; Sec 4.2.1 and 4.2.5 pp.4-5)
- Extraversion positively associated with susceptibility (Gkika 2016; Ndulue 2022); Decker & Kramer (2023) found no high-vs-low difference in political microtargeting. (n/a; Sec 4.2.2 p.5)
- Conscientiousness generally average; Halko & Kientz (2010) found it most strongly correlated with responsiveness in health mobile apps. (n/a; Sec 4.2.3 p.5)
- Openness generally below average in health and tech contexts but context-dependent: positive in education (Ghorbani & Rashvand Semiyari 2022) and voter mobilization (Gerber et al. 2013). (n/a; Sec 4.2.4 p.5)
- Neuroticism least susceptible overall, yet Oyibo & Vassileva (2019) found it most consistently correlated with responsiveness to social-influence strategies; Gkika et al. (2016) found greater acceptance of social proof. (n/a; Sec 4.2.5 p.5)
- Trait-specific matches: agreeableness with reciprocity and liking; extraversion with liking, commitment and consistency, scarcity, reciprocity (social proof and authority not significant); conscientiousness with reciprocity (liking least influential); openness with no specific match (studies conflict); neuroticism most persuaded by commitment and consistency, and positively by scarcity (Alkis & Temizel 2015). (Table 3 cells garbled in extraction; prose used; Sec 4.3.1-4.3.5 pp.5-6; Table 3 p.7)
- Review composition: Big Five is the most-used moderator; Cialdini is author in 17.5% of studies; authority and scarcity each in 15%; susceptibility/perceived persuasiveness is the most common outcome. (Big Five 33.75%; Cialdini 17.5%; authority 15%; scarcity 15%; susceptibility 25%; keyword search 56.25% vs snowballing 43.75%; Sec 4 p.3; Table 1 p.4)
- Political persuasion: Zarouali et al. (2022) found personality-tailored political ads influenced voting intentions and attitudes toward parties; Gerber et al. (2013) found openness linked to positive response to voter mobilization, with no firm causal inference. (n/a; Sec 4.1 p.3; Sec 4.2.4 p.5)
- Practical implication: random strategy use risks counterproductive effects; profile the audience, estimate susceptibility, then choose one strategy for the majority or a matching strategy per subgroup. (WHO 2021 context cited: 650 million obese; 1.9 billion overweight adults (context, not a review finding); Sec 5.2 p.7)

## Limitations

- Personality mostly measured by self-report, susceptible to social desirability bias (Sec 5.3 p.8).
- Many studies used one-off surveys of perceived persuasiveness; behavioral change not confirmed; field experiments recommended (Sec 5.3).
- Dominance of Cialdini's six principles narrows conceptual range (Sec 5.3).
- Much research is correlational; relationships may be bidirectional or confounded (Sec 5.3).
- Not pre-registered (Sec 3.1 p.2). Journal ranking deliberately not a criterion (Sec 3.3).
- Narrative synthesis only: no pooled effect sizes or formal quality appraisal in the text, so the strength of the matching claim cannot be weighed from this paper alone.
- Internal conflicts: openness results disagree (Alslaity & Tran 2020 found liking most persuasive for high openness, Sec 4.3.4), and the Table 3 openness row does not clearly match the prose.
- Data not shareable per authors (Data availability).

## Gaps and flags

- The engine has no strategy or appeal-type dimension. Lobbying pressure is a single scalar, so the paper's central result, that strategies should match traits and mismatches can backfire, cannot be represented. A wrong approach can only reduce gain, never cause a negative effect.
- Peer or social-proof influence (edge_mult) and direct pressure (susc_mult) share one susceptibility scalar. The review hints that the N sign may differ between those two channels.
- The signs of the N, C and E or dominance weights in the susceptibility composite have no literature support for persuasion as opposed to choking under pressure. This review weakly opposes the N and E signs, and nothing in the system tests them against a behavioural outcome. The H2 prospective contract is unspent.
- There is no context or domain moderator. The review finds that openness and extraversion effects change sign across contexts (education, voter mobilization, political microtargeting), while the system applies one fixed composite across all CU issues.
- Terminology clash: the paper's 'reciprocity' is Cialdini's message strategy (returning favours). It must not be cited as evidence for the system's competitiveness and reciprocity assumption, which remains NOT validated (D-104..D-108).
- Do not use the Table 2 susceptibility ranking as a weight calibration source. It is a count-based narrative synthesis with no effect sizes, Table 3 is garbled, and openness results conflict within the paper.
- The review's evidence comes from consumers and students, with self-reported traits and perceived-persuasiveness outcomes. The system has no validated N susceptibility signal (Nokken-Poole MISS; H5 null), and the psych audit reports 3/5 traits validated. Any claim built on this paper needs a status label and stays UNVERIFIED (DE-062).
- Any change to the receptivity weight signs moves ranks and needs a ruling from Paul. PERSONA_W 0.30 is kept by owner ruling and is not questioned here.

The paper partly supports Paul's framing ("very agreeable, or submissive to authority ... should be very susceptible", quoted at receptivity_layer.py:11): agreeableness is the most persuadable trait in the review, which supports the +.40 A or cooperation slot. It cuts against the second leg of his susceptibility composite. The review ranks neuroticism least susceptible to persuasion, and gives extraversion above-average susceptibility rather than resistance. The code is already honest that the C and E dampeners are design priors, and the N weight rests on Byrne's choking result, a different construct from persuasion. His position note for the receptivity layer (traits placed as edge parameters, Oropeza; 10 personalities collapse into 4) does not engage this literature, and he has no annotation on this paper. In this respect his note is not stale, since it never made a claim this review could overturn. The live system has moved further from Big Five text traits to behavioural proxies (coop_v2, affective N, diligence, dominance), which weakens any direct transfer.
