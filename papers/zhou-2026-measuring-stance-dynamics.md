# Measuring stance dynamics in political debate using temporal graph neural networks

**Zhiwei Zhou, Erick Elejalde (2026).** *Information Processing and Management, vol. 63, article 104705 (published online 4 March 2026; open access, CC BY). Metadata hint corrected: full author names, volume and article number, and DOI taken from the text..* [10.1016/j.ipm.2026.104705](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ipm.2026.104705). Theme: [[themes/neural-networks|Neural Networks]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Zhou and Elejalde build TWLGCN, a temporal graph network that infers whether a Twitter user favours or opposes a referendum. It works on a user-hashtag graph rebuilt every 28 days, plus optional user-user edges. The model is trained on only 80 to 106 hashtags labelled by side, from Chile's 2020 and 2022 referendums (about 1.21M tweets; 12,237 and 4,934 users). Each period's embeddings carry into the next. This beats re-starting each period by 1.3% to 9.8% on the 2022 dataset. Mention, quote and reply edges add about 3% macro-F1; follower edges add little. Labels taken from hashtags agree with human coders (kappa 0.81 to 0.94, on 100 snapshots per dataset). The second half is descriptive: seven classes of movement per transition, with a "static" band of median + MAD; flip curves that mirror each other; and neighbours who tend to shift in the same direction (Fig. 6). There is no null baseline and no causal test.
> 
> This matters because `fec/stance/stance_dynamics.py` is an explicit port of this paper, without the GNN. It uses 91-day windows on the CU press corpus, carries a member's last stance through silent windows, uses the seven-dynamics taxonomy, tests whether neighbours move together, and proposes stances for silent members from their neighbours. Checked against the live output (`data/stance/stance_dynamics.json`), the port has three problems. The paper's static band assumes a continuous probability. On pooled stance signs it comes out at 0.708, so 91.6% of transitions count as static. The neighbour co-movement test is null (134 same / 122 opposite, 52%). The 77.5% sign agreement for imputation (n=1,087, only cells with |est| at least 0.75, not a macro-F1 figure) has no party or majority-class baseline, while the paper scores with macro-F1 because its classes are imbalanced. The paper backs the design ideas. It does not validate carrying a label forward, the 91-day window, or the imputation. The paper offers no evidence for any of those.

## Study

**Question.** Can a temporal graph neural network infer user-level stance (favor vs. against) toward a referendum from user-hashtag and user-user interaction graphs at each 28-day period, including users who never post about the target, and can the resulting trajectories characterize opinion shifts at population, individual and neighborhood levels? (Sec. 1.1, p.2-3.)

**Theory.** Opinion change is treated as incremental, path-dependent and embedded in social context rather than abrupt reversal (Sec. 6, p.16). The model is motivated by social-influence and network-evolution theory (Flache et al., 2017; Bianchi & Squazzoni, 2015) and by the claim that inactive "silent" users keep stance information through embedding persistence across snapshots (Sec. 2 and 6). Social influence and homophily in temporal movement are asserted in the discussion (p.15-16) but are not tested beyond descriptive correlations. No formal opinion-dynamics model is estimated.

**Data.** Two Twitter (now X) datasets on the Chilean constitutional referendums, drawn from users following 384 Chilean news outlets, self-reported in Santiago, with at least 10 tweets across topics (Sec. 3.2, p.4-5). Original pool of about 9.2M users. Entry_DS (referendum of 25 Oct 2020): period 1 Jan to 24 Oct 2020; 849,171 tweets; 12,237 users; 171,615 hashtags; 80 labeled referendum hashtags (23 support, 57 oppose) used by 5,540 users; 11 periods of 28 days. Exit_DS (referendum of 4 Sep 2022): period 1 Jan to 3 Sep 2022; 356,214 tweets; 4,934 users; 57,211 hashtags; 106 labeled hashtags (50 support, 56 oppose) used by 1,897 users; 9 periods of 28 days (Table 1, p.5; Sec. 3.4, p.6). Unit of analysis: user-period snapshot (user stance per 28-day window). Totals across both datasets: about 1.21M tweets and 17,171 user rows (not deduplicated). Ground-truth stance comes from labeled-hashtag interaction counts (Eqs. 1-2). More than 95% of users interact with hashtags from one side only (p.6). Human validation: 100 user snapshots per dataset, two annotators (p.6-7). Word embeddings: FastText CBOW trained on all user-generated content, with preprocessing (Sec. 3.2). Data availability: "on request" (p.17).

**Method.** TWLGCN (Temporal Weighted LightGCN), an extension of WLGCN (Zhou & Elejalde, 2024). Each 28-day snapshot is a bipartite user-hashtag graph with count-weighted edges (Sec. 3.3). Four GCN propagation layers with symmetric degree normalization (Eq. 3), then multi-head attention over layer outputs (Eq. 4). User embeddings go to an MLP classifier with batch norm and dropout. Loss = focal loss (Eq. 5-6, class imbalance) + lambda1 * SSCL + lambda2 * ||H0||^2 (Eq. 11), with lambda1 = 1e-3 and lambda2 = 1e-6 (Sec. 4.1). SSCL, the Supervised Stance Contrastive Loss (Eq. 9-10), treats same-stance users and hashtags as positives and uses the adaptive reweighting alpha(x) = (rho + x)^(-lambda) from DynaDeno (Eq. 8). Noise-based augmentation of embeddings (Eq. 7). Sequential warm start: Xavier initialization at t=0; later periods inherit embeddings and MLP weights; hashtag initial embeddings from period-specific FastText (Sec. 3.3). User-user graphs: Pas (static follower/friend edges) and Act (per-period directed mention/quote/reply edges, Sec. 3.6, Eq. 12). Adam optimizer; max 1000 epochs; early stop after 50 epochs without validation macro-F1 gain. Stance probabilities from hashtag counts: p+ = |R+| / (|R+| + |R-|) (Eq. 1); ties are assigned by larger count, and ambiguous users are kept in training (p.6).

**Identification.** Not a causal design. Leakage control: 5-fold CV on annotated users (80% train / 20% validation). For every validation user, all edges to referendum-labeled hashtags are removed before training and inference, so validation users are "referendum-silent" (Sec. 4.1, p.9). Macro-F1 is computed per period on that period's active validation users. Shift analysis (flip categories, t-SNE distances, neighbor-dynamics heatmaps) is descriptive. Neighbor correlations are described as "potential social influences" (p.14) but no identification strategy is applied. Validation of labels: Cohen's kappa for human vs. human and human vs. hashtag labels (p.7). Open concern (not addressed in the text): FastText embeddings are trained on all user-generated content (Sec. 3.2), which may include validation users' text, and the paper does not say whether that text was restricted to training users.

**Scope.** Population: Twitter/X users in Santiago, Chile, who follow Chilean news outlets and meet activity filters (not representative; the authors say Twitter leaned liberal and supportive of the process, p.12). Institution: the two national constitutional referendums (2020 entry, 2022 exit). Era: January 2020 to September 2022, in 28-day periods. Language: Spanish hashtags (cultural use noted as a generalizability concern, p.16). Scope limited to a single platform and two referendums; no other elections, legislators, lobbying or policy venues.

## Key findings

- TWLGCN variants keep macro-F1 steady across periods, while the non-TWLGCN baselines (WLGCN, EvolveGCN, DynaDeno) fluctuate much more sharply. (Steady 70%-90% macro-F1 over observed periods; exact baseline values are in Fig. 2 and Table S4 (supplement, not in text).; Sec. 4.3.1, p.10; Fig. 2)
- The abstract claims the model beats network-based baselines in macro-F1 by more than 5% at each period using only 0.2% labeled hashtags. The main text does not report the baseline gap numerically, so the claim cannot be checked from the text. (Claimed >5%; labeled hashtags 80/171,615 (about 0.05%) in Entry_DS and 106/57,211 (about 0.19%) in Exit_DS (Table 1, p.5).; Abstract, p.1; Sec. 4.3.1, p.10)
- Carrying embeddings forward across snapshots matters: the full model beats the discrete variant that re-initializes embeddings each period. (On Exit_DS, the full TWLGCN outperforms the discrete variant by 1.3% to 9.8%.; Sec. 4.3.1, p.10)
- Adding active user-user relations (mentions, quotes, replies) gives the best overall performance. Passive follower/friend relations add little, and combining both does not help. (Act improves the base model by about 3% on both datasets.; Sec. 4.3.1, p.10; Sec. 6, p.15)
- The supervised contrastive loss (SSCL) adds accuracy on top of focal loss. The authors attribute this to same-class contrastive signal. (TWLGCN vs. TWLGCN w/o SSCL: about 2% gain in macro-F1.; Sec. 4.3.1, p.10-11)
- Human annotators agree substantially with each other on the stance of user snapshots, and with hashtag-derived labels, which supports hashtags as a stance proxy. (Human-human Cohen's kappa 0.836 (Entry_DS) and 0.842 (Exit_DS); human vs. hashtag-derived kappa 0.81 to 0.94; 100 user snapshots per dataset.; Sec. 3.4, p.6-7)
- Population-level stance: the Entry_DS median stays slightly positive (about 0.2) with a small IQR. The Exit_DS median starts below zero and drifts toward positive values, with a widening spread near the referendum date. (Entry_DS median about 0.2; opposing views about 25% of users; Exit_DS median initially negative (no exact value given).; Sec. 5.1, p.12; Fig. 4)
- Sudden flips between opposing and supporting: the number of O-to-S and S-to-O flips mirror each other across periods, with a sharp rise in supporter-to-opponent flips between periods 7 and 8 in Entry_DS. (No exact counts given in text.; Sec. 5.2, p.13 (Fig. 5a-b); Sec. 6, p.15-16)
- Flipping users move in the expected direction in embedding space relative to the annotated-hashtag centroids (periods 10 and 11, Entry_DS). (O-to-S users increase average distance from the opposing-hashtag centroid by over 28%. S-to-O users reduce distance to the opposing centroid by over 10% and increase distance to the supporting centroid by 27%.; Sec. 5.2.1, p.14 (Fig. 5c-d))
- Neighbors' stance shifts are correlated: users tend to shift in the same direction as their 1-hop and 2-hop neighbors (diagonal dominance). Authors describe this as tight-knit communities moving together; it is a descriptive correlation, not a tested influence effect. (Heatmap values only in figure; no numeric summary in text.; Sec. 5.2.2, p.14-15; Fig. 6; Sec. 6, p.15)
- Coverage of users present from the first snapshot (users remain predictable in later snapshots even with no activity). (Entry_DS 8,029 of 12,237 (65.6%); Exit_DS 3,315 of 4,934 (67.2%).; Sec. 5, p.12)
- Filtering did not distort the temporal or activity distribution of tweets relative to the raw data. (Cosine similarity > 0.99 and Jensen-Shannon distance < 0.05 for both datasets.; Sec. 3.2, p.5 (Fig. S1))
- Ambiguous users (interacting with both sides) are kept in training because they sit on the semantic boundary. (More than 95% of users interact with one side only.; Sec. 3.4, p.6)
- Window length: 28-day windows are chosen by a sensitivity analysis. Shorter windows fragment the graph (low giant-component fraction); longer windows mask rapid polarity shifts. (M = 28 days; 11 periods (Entry_DS) and 9 periods (Exit_DS).; Sec. 3.3, p.5 (Fig. S2))

## Limitations

- Supervision is narrow: stance labels come only from a small set of referendum hashtags (80 and 106 labeled), so users who avoid those hashtags can only partly be validated (Sec. 6.1, p.16).
- Labels are hashtag-derived proxies, with human agreement checked on only 100 snapshots per dataset (Sec. 3.4, p.6-7).
- Generalizability: one platform (Twitter/X), two Chilean referendums, Spanish hashtags with cultural use; replication across platforms, elections and languages is needed. Hashtag hijacking is not systematically evaluated (p.16).
- Fixed 28-day windows: shorter windows are sparse, longer ones over-smooth event-driven shifts; adaptive windows or continuous-time TGNNs are left for future work (p.16).
- Sequential warm-start training (DTDG, online) rather than joint end-to-end temporal optimization, which the authors call a trade-off in favor of streaming use (p.17).
- Sample is not representative: Santiago self-reported users following Chilean news outlets; Twitter leaned liberal and mostly supportive of the process (p.12).
- Vote outcomes are compared only qualitatively (the entry vote passed, the exit vote did not); no quantitative validation against the vote or polls (p.12).
- Neighbor-correlation and 'influence' claims are descriptive; the authors say leaders of shifts are future work (p.15).
- Main-text performance gap versus baselines is not given numerically; full CV results are in the supplement (Table S4), not in the extracted text.
- Data available only on request (p.17). Multimodal inputs, multitask learning and LLM-based user-level stance are unexplored (p.17).

## Gaps and flags

- No null or permutation baseline for neighbour co-movement (for example, shuffled neighbours within a window, or a control for common shocks per window). The 0.55 'diagonal dominance' threshold is arbitrary (stance_dynamics.py:106).
- The imputation is scored by raw sign agreement on confident cells only (|est| at least 0.75; 77.5%, n=1,087). There is no macro-F1 and no party-only or majority-class baseline, even though the component's own open question asks for a party baseline.
- The static band is 0.7076 live on pooled sign data, so 91.6% of transitions are static. It mostly measures how coarse the labels are. The within-side classes are rare because a move must exceed about 0.71, but not empty (S-to-MS 88).
- The 91-day window has no sensitivity analysis. The paper picks its window from giant-component fraction against shift masking.
- Carried (silent) windows enter the transition counts, inflating static counts and moving flips to the window where posting resumes.
- Co-movement and imputation are never run on the repost/mention interaction layer, which is the nearest analogue to the paper's best-performing Act edges (reposts are not among the paper's Act edge types).
- Documentation drift: the print at stance_dynamics.py:152 says '>=3 informative neighbors' while MINNB defaults to 5.
- The neighbour co-movement result (in the paper's Fig. 6, and in any re-run here) is a descriptive correlation. It must not be described as influence or contagion, and the paper's 'potential social influences' wording should not be carried into system text.
- The paper's symmetric degree normalisation inside GCN propagation (Eq. 3) is message-passing normalisation, not an argument for per-edge volume normalisation of SNA edges. That proposal was measured and REJECTED in this project, so it must not be cited as support for reviving it.
- Imputed stances remain proposals only. Stance labels are Paul's domain, and the module correctly leaves the matrix untouched.

Paul's annotation is a bare link ('Neural Networks link, sciencedirect S0306457326000968') with no takeaways. stance_dynamics.py is an explicit port of this paper's design, and its cosponsor co-movement test came back null (52%; the HCoI re-run gives 50.5%). The paper supports treating stance as a trajectory and inferring stances for silent actors. But it gives no change-likelihood model; its shift analysis is descriptive. Its gains come from a trained GNN on sparse per-window user-hashtag matrices, which the system deliberately did not build. Read correctly, the paper calls for better validation of the existing port (baselines, a window sensitivity run), not more machinery.
