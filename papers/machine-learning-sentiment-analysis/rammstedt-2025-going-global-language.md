# Going Global: 39 Language Versions of the BFI-2-XS

**Beatrice Rammstedt, Lena Roemer, Dorothee Behr, Matthias Bluemke, Clemens Lechner, Steve Dept, Laura Wayrynen, Christopher J. Soto, Oliver P. John (2025).** *Measurement Instruments for the Social Sciences, 2025, Vol. 7, Article e14067.* [10.5964/miss.14067](https://doi.org/10.5964/miss.14067). Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Rammstedt and colleagues document how the 15-item BFI-2-XS (and the 30-item BFI-2-S where fielded) was translated into 39 versions for 29 countries in 24 languages for the PIAAC 2023 social-emotional skills module. The procedure is a modified TRAPD: reuse and review of pre-existing translations for 15 languages, double translation plus reconciliation for nine languages (12 versions), national and expert review, independent linguistic verification, and Field Trial pretesting on nearly 30,000 respondents. Change requests were coded into a customized MQM error typology. Before the Field Trial, gendering drove 49 percent of requests and, on average, 10 percent of BFI-2-S items changed per version (range 0 to 80 percent). After it, 13 of 39 versions changed, averaging 6 percent of XS items. The paper is instrument documentation. It reports no reliability, factor structure or invariance results, because Field Trial data are confidential, and the authors say the versions are not yet fully validated. Their comparability claim rests on the procedure, not on data. For Paul's systems the bearing is thin. Melange reads Big Five traits from English political text with closed-vocabulary rates and a 20-item IPIP-style LLM rater, not with self-report questionnaires, and it does no translation. Two transferable lessons follow. First, small wording changes are treated as material to comparability, which matches the itemset versioning the LLM reader already does. Second, comparability across administrations is asserted procedurally rather than shown, which is the gap the system itself hit when interview-derived affective N failed to transfer to floor and hearing text. The paper offers no validity evidence that the system could borrow.

## Study

**Question:** How were the BFI-2-S and BFI-2-XS translated and culturally adapted for the PIAAC 2023 social-emotional skills module, so that the 39 resulting language versions are maximally comparable across countries, and can these versions be shared for wider research use?

**Theory:** Big Five trait framework with a hierarchical domain-facet structure (BFI-2). The paper invokes cross-cultural comparability as the rationale for standardized parallel adaptation. It tests no theory.

**Data:** No individual respondent data (Ethics Statement, p.20). Documentary data: 39 BFI-2-XS/BFI-2-S language versions across 29 countries and 24 languages (Table 1, pp.4-11); 15 XS items per version drawn from 30 S items; 1 source instrument. Field Trial data (nearly 30,000 respondents total, roughly 450 to 1,800 per country, pp.3 and 17) were analysed centrally by the PIAAC Consortium but are confidential and not reported. Period: PIAAC Cycle 2 fieldwork 2022/2023, paper received 2024, published January 2025.

**Method:** Descriptive documentation of a modified TRAPD2 procedure (Harkness 2003) with double translation and reconciliation, translation notes, national-centre and domain-expert review, independent linguistic verification, and empirical pretesting. Pre-existing translations (Table 2) were reviewed and adapted. Change requests were coded into a customized MQM error typology (accuracy, accuracy flagged in Field Trial, linguistic convention, style, survey terminology, gender, other). Field Trial psychometrics (descriptives, reliability, EFA, CFA, multi-group CFA alignment) were used to flag national deviations. Results are counts and percentages only; no inferential tests.

**Identification:** None. Descriptive documentation without a causal or comparative inferential design.

**Scope:** Population: adults assessed in the PIAAC 2023 Survey of Adult Skills in the 29 countries that fielded the social-emotional skills module (31 countries participated; Korea, Japan and the United States did not field it in the Main Study). Instrument-level scope: BFI-2-XS in all 39 versions, with BFI-2-S versions for the countries that fielded the longer form. Era: second PIAAC cycle, 2022 to 2023.

## Key findings

- The paper documents 39 language versions of the BFI-2-XS (and BFI-2-S where fielded) for 29 countries in 24 languages, developed for the PIAAC 2023 Big Five module. (39 versions; 29 countries; 24 languages; Abstract p.2; Results and Table 1 pp.4 and 6-11)
- The source instrument is the English BFI-2-XS of 15 short-phrase items, three per Big Five domain, rewritten as full sentences. Responses use a five-point scale with labels strongly disagree to strongly agree, with neutral labeled neither agree nor disagree. (15 items; 3 per domain; 5 points; Source Instrument p.5)
- Of 31 participating PIAAC countries, Korea, Japan and the United States did not field the Big Five module in the Main Study. Korea fielded only the BFI-2-S in the Field Trial. Of 28 Main Study countries taking the Big Five, 12 fielded the full 30-item BFI-2-S. (31 countries; 3 opted out; 12 of 28 took full BFI-2-S; Countries and Language Regions p.4; Aims p.3)
- For 15 of the 24 languages, pre-existing BFI-2 translations were adapted. For nine languages (12 versions) there were no pre-existing BFI-2-S adaptations, so new translations were produced by double translation and reconciliation. (15 of 24 languages pre-existing; 9 languages (12 versions) new; Development of the Initial Language Versions pp.15 and 13)
- Before the Field Trial, change requests on item formulations were coded by MQM category. Gendering accounted for 49 percent (n=37), linguistic conventions 24 percent (n=18) and accuracy 21 percent (n=16). On average 10 percent of BFI-2-S items changed per version, ranging from 0 to 80 percent. (10% mean item change (0-80% range); 49% gender, 24% linguistic, 21% accuracy; Review of the Initial Versions pp.16-17; Figure 2)
- Three change requests were judged preferential and not implemented. Requests were approved only when they addressed major issues such as mistranslations that caused Field Trial errors. (3 requests rejected; Review of the Initial Versions p.16; Final National Versions p.14)
- After the Field Trial, 13 of the 39 versions requested changes. On average 6 percent of XS items changed per version (range 0 to 53 percent). 46 percent (n=15) of the requested and implemented changes concerned items flagged in the Field Trial; 27 percent (n=9) were other accuracy issues. (13 of 39 versions changed; 6% mean XS item change (0-53%); 46% flagged, 27% other accuracy; Review of the Tested Versions pp.18 and 17)
- The authors describe the procedure as fully standardized and run in parallel, and argue this makes the versions more likely to be comparable than independent national translations. They do not report any psychometric evidence supporting that claim, because Field Trial data are confidential. (no psychometric results reported; Discussion pp.19 and fn.3 p.14)

## Limitations

- Field Trial data are confidential under OECD guidelines, so no empirical psychometric results for the language versions are reported (pp.5 and 19).
- The authors state that the versions have not yet been fully validated. The exact psychometric properties of each adaptation, and their comparison with the English source, remain to be studied (p.19).
- For languages with both a pre-existing and an adapted version, the paper does not compare them, so it cannot say which is best for research use (p.19).
- Table 1 change counts can refer to items not fielded in the Field Trial, and some countries fielded only domains C and O in the Field Trial (Table 1 notes a and c, p.11).
- Country-level item-by-item adaptation details, translation notes and the comment spreadsheet sit in OSF appendices, not in the article (pp.3 and 15).
- The authors describe the process as time and resource intensive and do not quantify its cost (p.19).

## Gaps and flags

- The system never assesses formal measurement invariance (multi-group CFA or alignment) of its text-derived traits across speech genres or party groups. It relies on within-corpus z-scores and on one-off genre-transfer checks.
- The LLM reader's item battery is IPIP-style and written for the project. It has no external item-level validation of the kind that established inventories such as BFI-2 have, and this paper would not supply any.
- Flag: The item metadata tag measurement-invariance overstates the paper. It reports no invariance results, only that alignment analyses were run centrally on confidential data.
- Flag: There is an internal inconsistency on language count: p.2 says 23 languages, while the abstract and p.15 say 24.
- Flag: Editorial conflict: the first author is Editor-in-Chief of the publishing journal, and two co-authors are Associate Editors (p.20).
- Flag: None of the brain rules are triggered. The paper makes no SNA, LDA, reciprocity or influence claims.
