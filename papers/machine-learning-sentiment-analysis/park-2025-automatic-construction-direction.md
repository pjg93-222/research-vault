# Automatic construction of direction-aware sentiment lexicon using direction-dependent words

**Jihye Park, Hye Jin Lee, Sungzoon Cho (2025).** *Language Resources and Evaluation, vol. 59, pp. 843-869 (accepted 28 Mar 2024; published online 25 May 2024).* [10.1007/s10579-024-09737-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10579-024-09737-9). Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Park, Lee and Cho build a financial sentiment lexicon in which each entry pairs a directional word ("up" or "down" family) with a noun whose polarity depends on that direction. Their example is "cost", which is negative alone but positive in "cost decrease". Sentences in the Financial PhraseBank are tagged proportional or inversely proportional from an up-count minus down-count score. PMI between nouns and the two tags picks out 56 direction-dependent words, and these yield 1,767 pairs. Adding the pairs to Loughran-McDonald raises weighted F1 on the PhraseBank subsets from about 0.59-0.62 to 0.71-0.82; those gains are in-sample for the labels used to build the lexicon, not held-out. On SemEval (960 messages) and FiQA (436 sentences) it stays below 0.5, Sentiment140, VADER and AFINN beat it there, and fine-tuned RoBERTa is far ahead (about 0.96 on DS100). The construction set was heavily imbalanced (28 inversely proportional sentences against 691 proportional), which produced noisy words. Only the construction domain is headline-style, and there are no significance tests.
>
> This paper has already been used in Paul's system. The brain notes say he adapted it as a "directional verb x CU-valenced target" override for the CFPB sign-flip problem. A whole-text version made 46 sign changes on gold: 3 good and 43 bad. It was rejected because releases defending the CFPB describe the attack with "shrink" verbs. The idea that survives in the built system is a curated, direction-separated target registry (direction-separated CFPB families; cfpb_institution and cfpb_general are fixed at 0 by policy, the reform and expansion signs are gold-derived), not an automatic lexicon. The paper explains both results. Its pairs work on short headlines where the direction word modifies the noun, and press releases are long texts. The dry-run churn came from verb counts over whole release text, not from lexicon class imbalance, so the match with the paper's failure cases is in mechanism only. The open question in the system (restrict the rule to the sentence or headline that names the bill) is the condition under which the paper's evidence would actually transfer. Nothing here concerns networks, money or allocation.

## Study

**Question:** Can a financial sentiment lexicon be built automatically so that each entry is a pair of a directional word (up/down) and a direction-dependent word, so that sentiment flips correctly with direction (e.g., "cost" negative, but "cost decrease" positive)? Does adding these pairs to the Loughran-McDonald (LM) word list improve sentence-level sentiment classification of financial headlines? (Sect. 1, pp. 845-846)

**Theory:** Lexicon-based explainability: sentiment lexicons are preferred in finance because they expose the words that drive a decision, whereas supervised deep models are black boxes (Sect. 1, pp. 845-846). Domain-specific polarity: a word's sentiment depends on domain and on directional modifiers. Direction-dependency is operationalized as a word whose sentiment orientation changes with "up" or "down" words (Sect. 1, p. 846; Sect. 3.1, p. 850). No formal economic theory is stated beyond the market-sentiment and media literature cited in the introduction (Tetlock 2007; Engelberg and Parsons 2011; Garcia 2013).

**Data:** Unit: sentence or economic news headline. Construction corpus: DS50 subset of the Financial PhraseBank (FPB), 4,835 English sentences labelled by 16 finance experts; 691 sentences tagged "proportional" and 28 tagged "inversely proportional" after the direction-score step; 56 direction-dependent words extracted; Senti-DD lexicon of 1,767 pairs (1,077 positive, 690 negative) (Sect. 4.1, p. 853; Table 1, p. 848). Evaluation corpora: FPB subsets DS50 (4,835), DS66 (4,209), DS75 (3,447), DS100 (2,259); SemEval-2017 Task 5 subtask 2 (960 messages, continuous scores converted to three classes); FiQA Task 1 (436 sentences) (Sect. 4.3, pp. 855-856; Table 6, p. 856). Directional word lists: 20 "up" and 11 "down" terms seeded from HGI word lists (Table 3, p. 853). Time period: not stated for the corpora; the appendix examples are dated roughly 2006-2010. Evaluation uses stratified five-fold cross-validation repeated five times (Sect. 4.3, p. 856).

**Method:** Stage 1 (direction tagging, Sect. 3.1): each polar sentence gets a direction score = count of "up" words minus count of "down" words (Porter stemming, NLTK). Sentences are tagged "proportional" (positive with score > 0, or negative with score < 0) or "inversely proportional" (the reverse). Neutral sentences are excluded. Stage 2 (association, Sect. 3.2): nouns are lemmatized (NLTK); nouns occurring in fewer than 1% of sentences are dropped. PMI(w,t) = log2 [p(w,t) / (p(w) p(t))] is computed for each noun and tag. Dependency score (Eq. 1) keeps PMI(w,tp) if PMI(w,tp) - PMI(w,ti) > delta, uses -PMI(w,ti) if the difference is below -delta, and 0 otherwise; delta = 0 in this study. Stage 3 (extraction, Sect. 3.3): from each sentence, the highest-scoring proportional word or the lowest-scoring inversely proportional word is kept as the direction-dependent word; words with non-alphabetic characters or fewer than k = 3 letters are removed. Stage 4 (Senti-DD, Sect. 3.4): pairs of directional and direction-dependent words are labelled positive-context or negative-context. Stage 5 (evaluation, Sect. 4.2): LM sentiment score = PosScore - NegScore (Eq. 2); ContextScore = ContPosScore - ContNegScore (Eq. 3); RefinedScore = SentimentScore + ContextScore (Eq. 4); sign of the refined score gives positive, neutral or negative. Baselines: Sentiment140, SentiWordNet, SO-CAL, MPQA, TextBlob, VADER, SentiStrength, AFINN, LM. Pretrained comparators: Word2Vec with logistic regression (word2vec-google-news-300), BERT-base-uncased and RoBERTa-base fine-tuned (Adam, weight decay 0.01, batch 8, 500 warm-up steps, 3 epochs; Sect. 4.4, pp. 856-857). Hyperparameters were set heuristically, and the authors state that tuning is left to future work (p. 857).

**Identification:** None in the causal sense. The paper does not identify any causal effect. Validity rests on the downstream classification F1 of the augmented lexicon against baselines, with the explainability of individual pairs checked by inspection (Appendix 1, pp. 863-866; Table 9, p. 861). The paper says that evaluating lexicon quality directly needs inter-annotator agreement that it did not collect (Sect. 4.2, p. 854).

**Scope:** Population: English-language economic and financial news headlines (FPB, SemEval-2017 Task 5, FiQA), mainly company-performance headlines from Finnish and other European firms that reflect the FPB corpus. Institution: Seoul National University (Industrial Engineering). Era: the corpora are dated to the mid-2000s to 2010s; the paper does not state a period for its sample. The method is claimed for other domains such as electronics reviews, and for central-bank communications (FOMC) as future work (Sect. 6, pp. 862-863). These extensions were not tested.

## Key findings

- Senti-DD construction yields 56 direction-dependent words (49 proportional, 7 inversely proportional), e.g., proportional: profit, revenue, growth, net; inversely proportional: cost, plant, traffic. (56 words; 49 proportional, 7 inversely proportional; Sect. 4.5 text / Table 9, p. 861)
- The lexicon has 1,767 directional/direction-dependent pairs, 1,077 positive and 690 negative. (1,767 pairs; 1,077 positive; 690 negative; Sect. 1, p. 846; Table 1, p. 848)
- Worked PMI example for profit: PMI(profit, proportional) = 0.05 and PMI(profit, inversely proportional) = -3.0, giving dependency score 0.05. (PMI 0.05 and -3.0; dependency score 0.05; 217 sentences contain profit; Table 2, p. 851)
- On the four FPB subsets, LM + Senti-DD raises weighted F1 over the LM list alone. The reported F1 values are DS50 0.5914 to 0.7057, DS66 0.6023 to 0.7322, DS75 0.6174 to 0.7788 and DS100 0.5982 to 0.8233. The paper says it beats all lexicon baselines, which holds on FPB only; on SemEval and FiQA Sentiment140, VADER and AFINN beat LM+Senti-DD. (F1 LM 0.5914/0.6023/0.6174/0.5982 vs LM+Senti-DD 0.7057/0.7322/0.7788/0.8233 (DS50/DS66/DS75/DS100); Table 7, pp. 858-859; Sect. 5, p. 857)
- On DS100, LM+Senti-DD roughly doubles the negative-class F1 of LM (text says nearly twice). Table values: negative F1 0.4087 (LM) to 0.7260; positive F1 0.2596 (LM) to 0.7361. (negative F1 0.4087 -> 0.7260; positive F1 0.2596 -> 0.7361; Table 8, p. 860; text p. 860)
- On the SemEval and FiQA sets, LM+Senti-DD is still below 0.5 F1 (0.4789 SemEval; 0.4937 FiQA). Pretrained models do better on nearly all measures, and the authors attribute this to lexicon methods needing explicit sentiment words. (F1 SemEval LM 0.3632 -> LM+Senti-DD 0.4789; FiQA LM 0.3635 -> LM+Senti-DD 0.4937; Table 7, pp. 858-859; text p. 858)
- The DS100 pretrained models score higher than LM+Senti-DD (e.g., RoBERTa F1 0.9620 on DS100 in the Table 7 text), so the lexicon does not reach state-of-the-art accuracy. The paper says this is an explainability trade-off. (RoBERTa DS100 weighted F1 0.9620 as extracted; Table 7 p. 859; Sect. 5 p. 858; Sect. 6 p. 862)
- Worked example: LM labels 'Profit for the period was EUR 10.9 mn, down from EUR 14.3 mn in 2009' neutral, but LM+Senti-DD labels it negative via the (profit, down) pair (refined score -1). (SentimentScore 0; ContextScore -1; RefinedScore -1; Sect. 5 p. 857; Table 5 p. 855; Fig. 3 p. 858)
- In a 20-sentence DS100 sample, 10 predictions are correct and 10 are wrong. Failures mainly come from context pairs the lexicon counts as non-neutral, e.g., (company, increase). (10 of 20 correct; Sect. 5 p. 862; Appendix 1 pp. 863-866)
- Only 28 of 719 sentences in the construction corpus are inversely proportional, and this imbalance produces noisy direction-dependent words such as beer and day. (28 inversely proportional sentences; 691 proportional; Sect. 4.1 p. 853; Sect. 5 p. 862)

## Limitations

- Directional word lists are chosen manually by the researchers, which the authors call time-consuming and subjective; the directional vocabulary may also go out of date (Sect. 6, p. 863).
- Imbalance between proportional (691) and inversely proportional (28) tagged sentences yields noisy words such as beer and day (Sect. 5, p. 862).
- Neutrality is detected passively (equal positive and negative counts), not by a dedicated neutrality step (Sect. 6, p. 863).
- Lexicon methods degrade when sentiment is not explicitly expressed; SemEval and FiQA F1 stay below 0.5 (Sect. 5, p. 858).
- Pretrained model inference is treated as black box; the paper's statements about why pretrained models succeed are speculative (Sect. 5, p. 858).
- Hyperparameters, including the number of epochs and delta = 0, k = 3, were set heuristically; no hyperparameter tuning was done (Sect. 4.4, p. 857).
- Evaluation relies on downstream classification F1 without inter-annotator agreement measures; the authors say lexicon quality is hard to assess directly (Sect. 4.2, p. 854).
- Sentence-level only, English-only, with one-sentence headlines as the construction domain; generalization to other domains (electronics reviews, FOMC minutes) is proposed but untested (Sect. 6, pp. 862-863).
- The reported results have no significance tests or confidence intervals, and the lexicon was built from labels on all 4,835 DS50 sentences and scored on DS50 and DS100 with no described fold-wise rebuild.
- Appendix examples show the lexicon can flag a context pair (company, increase) as a negative cue, which reduces accuracy on some headlines (Sect. 5, p. 862).

## Gaps and flags

- No sentence- or headline-scoped direction rule. Every directional-lexicon test in the system counted over whole release text, while the paper's evidence is sentence-level and its pairs require the direction word to govern the noun.
- No representation of speaker attitude toward the direction ('defend against efforts to dismantle the CFPB'). The paper does not model this either, and it is the dominant failure mode in Paul's dry run. A direction-pair feature would need dependency or clause scoping before it could help DEBATE.
- The direction-pair signal was never tried as a soft feature into the stacked stance readers. That was the recorded pivot option, and it has no held-out measurement.
- LSD intensity ignores direction-dependent polarity. Whether this distorts |net| on CU press releases is untested, and there is no human-coded intensity gold.

- The system has already tested and rejected this paper's method as adapted: naive directional-verb x target override, 3 good / 43 bad on gold. Do not re-propose a whole-text direction-aware override. Any sentence-scoped revival needs a held-out gold look.
- The paper's F1 values parse cleanly from Tables 7 and 8 but have no significance tests or confidence intervals, and the FPB gains are in-sample. Attach the corpus and denominator, e.g. 'weighted F1 on FPB DS100, 2,259 sentences, 5x5-fold CV'.
- The paper's domain (European company-performance headlines, mid-2000s to 2010s) is not congressional press. Its evidence does not transfer to the multi-topic releases that dominate the Stage 5 corpus.

There are no annotations, but Paul acted on the paper. He adapted it for the CFPB sign-flip problem (memory note cu-signflip-directionality; brain two-hop note). The built system agrees with the paper's core idea, that polarity belongs to the pair and not the word: the direction-separated CFPB families in derive_valence_registry.py are a hand-curated Senti-DD. It disagrees on automation, because the automatic and naive version churned (3 good / 43 bad), and the paper's headline-style construction domain and class imbalance are consistent with that.
