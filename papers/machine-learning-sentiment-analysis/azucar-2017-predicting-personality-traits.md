# Azucar, Marengo, Settanni: Big 5 from social media footprints (meta-analysis)

**Danny Azucar, Davide Marengo, Michele Settanni (2018).** *Personality and Individual Differences, vol. 124, pp. 150-159 (online 22 Dec 2017).* [10.1016/j.paid.2017.12.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2017.12.018) Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Azucar, Marengo and Settanni (Personality and Individual Differences 124, 2018; online December 2017) pooled 16 independent studies from 14 papers. They ran one random-effects meta-analysis per trait on untransformed Pearson r. The criterion in every study was a self-report Big Five inventory (BFI, TIPI or IPIP) from ordinary users of Facebook, Twitter, Sina Weibo or Instagram (two included studies, Farnadi 2016 Study 1 and Kosinski 2013, used MyPersonality data), with literature searched up to May 2017. The pooled correlations were: Openness .39, Conscientiousness .35, Extraversion .40, Agreeableness .29 (the lowest) and Neuroticism .33. The authors found no publication bias. Heterogeneity was very large (I² ≥ 93.15, every Q(15) significant). In univariate meta-regressions, using several types of footprint raised accuracy for O, C and N. Adding demographics raised it for A and N. Activity statistics (friends, posts, network density) raised it for E only marginally (β .19, p .06). Text and pictures alone added nothing. Each study contributed its best model or strongest feature, which may inflate the pooled r.
> 
> For Paul's systems this is a calibration paper, not a validation of them. Melange scores members of Congress, and the paper's criterion is self-report from lay users, which is not what the system's targets are. The repo's held-out r in data/members/big5_v2.json are against behavioral targets (A .059, C .281, E .563, O .135; ES has none), so Azucar's pooled r is not a benchmark for them. Whether Melange's congressional data are staff-written, and how the system is validated against expert panels, are not in the paper and were not checked here (unverified). The paper offers three lessons:
> - Agreeableness is the least recoverable trait from digital footprints. The paper tests no system; this is at most consistent with the system's move from lexical A to behavioural cooperation.
> - Activity counts are at best a marginal Extraversion signal.
> - Demographics raised accuracy for A and N in univariate meta-regressions. The paper cites survey literature on demographic differences in self-report, so one hypothesis is that group membership correlates with self-reported traits; the paper does not establish that mechanism. The system's affective-N residualization controls for party and E only, not age or gender.
> 
> These pooled figures cover 16 studies of lay users and are UNSTRATIFIED across platforms. The authors could not test differences between platforms.

## Study

**Question.** Estimate the mean predictive validity (Pearson r) of social-media digital footprints for each Big 5 trait; test whether the types of footprint used, the platform's default privacy setting (public vs. private), and study quality moderate prediction accuracy; check for publication bias; and assess whether accuracy is consistent across traits (Abstract; Sec. 1.3, p.151).

**Theory.** Social-media content is an extension of "one's self" and reflects actual personality rather than idealized self-presentation (Back et al., 2010; Seidman, 2013), so digital footprints should carry personality signal (Sec. 1.1, p.150). Benchmark: the typical behavior-personality correlation of about 0.30-0.40, the "personality coefficient" (Meyer et al., 2001; Roberts et al., 2007), which the authors call the "correlational upper-limit" (Abstract; Sec. 4, p.155). The Big 5 (McCrae & Costa, 1987; McCrae & John, 1992) is the target framework (Sec. 1.2, p.151). No formal theory of why particular footprints predict particular traits is developed; the moderator hypotheses come from survey literature on demographics and from the extraversion-activity link.

**Data.** Unit: independent study-level effect size. Literature (Scopus, ISI Web of Science, PubMed, ProQuest, plus Google/Google Scholar and citation chasing), search finalized May 2017 (Sec. 2.1, p.151-152). 24 papers and 28 studies identified (Sec. 3.1, p.154). Removed 12 studies for non-independence: 11 used MyPersonality (Facebook) data, plus Golbeck (2016) Study 3 (shared data with Golbeck et al., 2011). Final set: 14 papers, 16 independent studies, 80 effect sizes (16 per trait). Platforms in the 16: Facebook 7, Twitter 5, Sina Weibo 3, Instagram+Twitter 1. Footprints: 9 single-type studies, 7 multiple-type. Five papers lacked usable effect sizes; author contact recovered one; four were excluded (Sec. 2.4, p.153). Participant N and study years are not reported in the extracted text; the pooled N is not stated. Coders: the authors, with full agreement reported (Sec. 2.4, p.154).

**Method.** Five separate random-effects meta-analyses, one per Big 5 trait, using untransformed Pearson r (Fisher z explicitly rejected, citing Schmidt & Hunter, 2014; Sec. 2.4, p.153). Conversions: R^2 to r by square root (n=2); AUROC to Cohen's d to r (n=1); sensitivity/specificity or PPV/NPV to odds ratio to d to r (n=1); non-r reports converted (n=4). Where a study had several models or features, the best model or highest feature effect was used (Sec. 2.4, p.152-153). Outliers: Grubbs test (none found). Heterogeneity: Q, tau^2, I^2. Publication bias: funnel plot, Begg-Mazumdar and Egger tests, trim-and-fill, classic fail-safe N. Moderators: univariate random-effects meta-regression, REML, alpha 0.05, p<0.10 discussed; a moderator was tested only if at least 4 studies per group (Fu et al., 2014). Quality groups were pooled as low+medium (2+2) vs high (12) to reach the minimum. Software: Comprehensive Meta-analysis v3.3.070 (Sec. 2.4, p.153-154; Sec. 3.2.3, p.154).</method>
<parameter name="identification">None causal. Observational meta-analysis. Moderators are coded study characteristics entered one at a time (univariate), so no moderator is adjusted for another. Coefficients reflect between-study associations, not within-study or individual-level effects.

**Scope.** Population: social-media users in the 16 included studies, mostly English-speaking users or Sina Weibo (Chinese) users; the authors state that cultural comparison was not possible (Sec. 5, p.157). Platforms: Facebook, Twitter, Sina Weibo, Instagram (combined with Twitter). Era: literature up to May 2017. Institutions: not applicable (no institutional sampling). Measures are from 2012-2017-era studies.

## Key findings

- Mean meta-analytic correlations between digital footprints and each Big 5 trait (random effects, 16 effect sizes per trait). Extraversion highest, agreeableness lowest; the authors report no significant differences across traits, though no formal cross-trait test is named. (O r=0.39 [0.30, 0.48]; C r=0.35 [0.29, 0.42]; E r=0.40 [0.33, 0.46]; A r=0.29 [0.21, 0.36]; N r=0.33 [0.27, 0.39]. Table 2 Z: 7.81, 9.63, 10.32, 7.07, 9.96 (all p<0.001).) [Sec. 3.2.1, p.154; Abstract p.150; Table 2 p.157; cross-trait claim Sec. 4, p.155]
- Heterogeneity is large and mostly true between-study variance. Q is significant for every trait, but tau^2 is small. (Q(15): O 590.00, C 281.91, E 320.91, A 350.77, N 219.01 (all p<0.001). I^2: O 97.46, C 94.68, E 95.33, A 95.72, N 93.15 (text: I^2 >= 93.15). tau^2: O 0.04, C 0.02, E 0.02, A 0.02, N 0.01.) [Sec. 3.2.1, p.154; Table 2, p.157]
- No evidence of publication bias. Funnel plots symmetric; trim-and-fill added no studies; fail-safe N exceeds the 5k+10 rule of thumb for all traits. (Begg-Mazumdar p: O .39, C .21, E .50, A .24, N .26. Egger p: O .31, C .14, E .49, A .44, N .29. Fail-safe N: O 12,210; C 7,688; E 11,933; A 6,053; N 7,197 (threshold 90).) [Sec. 3.2.2, p.154]
- Grubbs outlier test flagged no studies. (0 studies removed.) [Sec. 3.1, p.154]
- Multiple types of footprints are associated with higher accuracy for O, C and N (significant). E shows a non-significant trend. A shows no increase (the text says 'except agreeableness' but gives no coefficient). The Abstract's 'accuracy improves' claim rests on these trait-level results. (O beta=0.27, R^2=0.16 (sig.); C beta=0.25, R^2=0.20 (sig.); N beta=0.21, R^2=0.14 (sig.); E beta=0.18, R^2=0.12, p=0.08 (n.s.).) [Sec. 3.2.3, p.154; Discussion Sec. 4, p.155]
- Demographics (age, gender) are associated with higher accuracy for agreeableness and neuroticism, and a marginal increase for openness. (A beta=0.25, R^2=0.19; N beta=0.25, p<0.05, R^2=0.19; O beta=0.26, R^2=0.12, p=0.09.) [Sec. 3.2.3, p.154-155; Discussion p.155]
- Activity statistics (friends, posts, network density) are associated with higher accuracy for extraversion only at a marginal level. (E beta=0.19, R^2=0.18, p=0.06.) [Sec. 3.2.3, p.155; Discussion p.155]
- Language/text features and pictures did not improve accuracy over other footprint types. Public vs. private default platform setting had no significant effect. Study quality had no significant effect. (Platform: private (Facebook) vs public (Twitter, Weibo, Reddit, Instagram): no significant effect. Quality: high n=12 vs low+medium n=4 (2+2): no significant effect.) [Discussion, Sec. 4, p.155]
- Likes were not testable as a moderator, since only one study used them. The one Likes study in the pool is coded but not moderator-tested. (Likes moderator: 1 study.) [Sec. 3.2.3, p.154]
- Non-independence screening removed about 43% of identified studies, mostly MyPersonality Facebook studies. Facebook dominates the remaining evidence base only partly: 7 of 16 included studies. (12 of 28 studies excluded (11 MyPersonality + 1 Golbeck 2016 Study 3). Included: Facebook 7, Twitter 5, Sina Weibo 3, Instagram+Twitter 1.) [Sec. 3.1, p.154; Table 1 p.155]

## Limitations

- Few studies per platform: no thorough cross-platform comparison of accuracy (Sec. 5, p.157).
- Heterogeneity in features and analytic approaches prevented estimating the contribution of individual features to accuracy (Sec. 5, p.157).
- Cultural differences could not be tested; samples are mostly English-speaking or Sina Weibo (Chinese) users (Sec. 5, p.157).
- Visual footprints: only a minority of studies used pictures, none used video, and only one (Skowron et al., 2016) used Instagram or Snapchat, so visual results are preliminary (Sec. 5, p.157).
- Privacy: only default platform settings were coded; the users' actual privacy settings were not collected, so the null platform result should be treated with caution (Sec. 4, p.155).
- Study quality is an approximation from source rank (Scopus CiteScore quartile; CORE 2017 and Microsoft Academic conference ranks), because no standard quality guidelines exist for this field (Sec. 2.3.3, p.152).
- Moderator tests are underpowered: groups need at least 4 studies, and effects with p<0.10 are discussed (Sec. 2.4, p.154; Sec. 3.2.3, p.154).
- Five papers lacked usable effect sizes; one author supplied data and four were excluded. The text does not say whether those four were among the 16 included studies (Sec. 2.4, p.153).
- Only 16 independent studies were included, and 11 of the 12 non-independent studies were excluded for overlap with MyPersonality data (Sec. 3.1, p.154).
- Coverage and era: search finalized May 2017; default privacy settings are expected to change over time (Sec. 4, p.155).

## Short quotes

- "Overall, the present meta-analysis demonstrates that Big 5 personality traits can be inferred using digital footprints extracted from social media with remarkable accuracy." (Sec. 6 Conclusions, p.157)
- "digital records of behaviors on social media may represent a quite reliable source of information for the prediction" (Sec. 4 Discussion, p.155)

## Gaps and flags

- No self-report criterion exists for legislators. The system's references (RKH text estimates, Senate expert and insider panels) are not the criterion the footprint literature validates against, so the paper's accuracy figures cannot be imported as a benchmark.
- Authorship: the system does not model staff or ghost-writing in social posts and press output, although the meta-analysis's theoretical warrant (content as an extension of the self) assumes the person wrote it.
- Per-platform and per-genre accuracy: the paper could not stratify by platform, and the system pools tweets, floor speech and interviews into one corpus (BIG5_SOURCES restricts genres for validation only). No stratified accuracy by genre is reported against an external criterion.
- Upward bias from model selection: the paper's best-model-per-study rule inflates pooled r. The system's own improvement rounds guard against this with single looks, but no comparison to a lay-user benchmark accounts for it.
- Do not quote 'remarkable accuracy' or pooled r ≈ .35 as evidence that Melange's lexical Big Five is valid. The criteria differ (self-report versus the system's behavioral targets), and the pooled figure spans I² ≥ 93% heterogeneity. Any citation must carry the label UNSTRATIFIED (16 studies, 4 platforms, lay users, 2012-2017).
- The demographics-moderator finding must not be used to justify adding party or gender as trait predictors. That conflicts with the affective-N gate (party-controlled, party r = .000) and would make a group proxy look like a personality measure.
- The big5_traits.py docstring treats social posts as validated personality-cue carriers (citing Birkenmaier). Azucar's evidence concerns self-authored lay-user content, so it does not extend that claim to staff-managed congressional accounts.
- No conflict with the rejected SNA proposals, D-021 or D-025: the paper makes no network-edge or influence claims.

Paul has not annotated this paper, so no view of his is attributed to it here. The paper's platform heterogeneity is large and it could not test differences across platforms. Even among lay users with authentic self-report, A is the least recoverable trait (r = .29), and the paper says nothing about whether traits predict yielding to pressure, so it gives no support for using traits as receptivity-layer parameters.
