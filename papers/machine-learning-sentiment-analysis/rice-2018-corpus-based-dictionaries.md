# Corpus-Based Dictionaries for Sentiment Analysis of Specialized Vocabularies

**Douglas R. Rice (Dept. of Political Science, University of Massachusetts Amherst), Christopher Zorn (Dept. of Political Science, Pennsylvania State University) (2018).** *Not named in the text. Title page dated July 20, 2018, and the text says materials will be posted to the Political Science Research and Methods Dataverse. The PSRM revise-and-resubmit status comes from the file name and metadata only; the text names no journal..* Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Rice and Zorn (2018, a draft dated July 20, 2018; the text names no journal) argue that off-the-shelf sentiment dictionaries break on specialized vocabularies. Their alternative starts from 10 positive and 10 negative seed words, trains GloVe vectors on the domain corpus (300 dimensions, 50-token window, terms seen fewer than 90 times dropped), keeps the 500 nearest words on each side, weights counts by tf-idf and cosine similarity, and scores polarity as (Wp-Wn)/(Wp+Wn). On the 100,000-review IMDB dataset, accuracy on 25,000 labeled in-sample reviews is 80.4% and on 25,000 held-out reviews 79.5%, against about 69-71% for LIWC and AFINN. It already beats them at 5,000 documents. On about 26,000 Supreme Court majority opinions (1792-2010), justice-level polarity ranks correlate only 0.29 with LIWC and 0.16 with AFINN, while those two correlate 0.64 with each other. The choice of dictionary therefore reorders the units. The paper calls its court validation suggestive, not conclusive, and notes that document-level polarity cannot say which subject the sentiment is about.
> 
> For Paul's system the sharpest point is Stage 5b. press_value = DEBATE stance sign x stock Lexicoder LSD2015 intensity. LSD lists "credit" as positive and "debt*", "charge*", "risk*", "oversight*" and "fraud*" as negative, and run_stage5.R scores every release with no masking of those domain terms. A one-path local check on 1,563 text-bearing releases was not reproduced in verification and is UNVERIFIED. Its figures suggested a small effect: 4.5% of positive mass and 7.0% of negative mass come from those terms, the release-level intensity rank correlation is 0.953 with and without them, and the LSD net sign flips on 4.1% of releases. The legacy regex scorer cites Rice and Zorn for its polarity formula, but its lexicon is hand-weighted. Its corpus-expansion helper departs from the paper's parameters and was never run. The paper is a measurement template the press layer has not yet applied, not a reason to change rankings.

## Study
**Question.** Can a "minimally-supervised" sentiment dictionary built from the corpus itself (seed words plus GloVe word-vector similarity) yield valid sentiment measures for specialized vocabularies where standard dictionaries (LIWC, AFINN) fail? The paper then applies the method to U.S. Supreme Court majority opinions to measure tone across 1792-2010, including over time and per justice.

**Theory.** Distributional hypothesis: words used in similar contexts have similar meanings (p.4-5). Word vectors trained on a domain corpus place sentiment-laden words near seed words, so a small seed set grows a domain-appropriate dictionary. Standard dictionaries embed fixed valences that may be wrong in specialized contexts (e.g., "love" and "bagel" in tennis, p.3-4). Semantic drift over time is handled by re-estimating dictionaries on temporal subsets (p.19-20). The paper argues sentiment in court opinions is a substantive proxy for justices' disagreement and the norm of consensus (p.17-18).

**Data.** Movie reviews (benchmark): Large Movie Review Dataset, 100,000 IMDB reviews (25,000 positive, 25,000 negative, 50,000 unlabeled). Word vectors were trained on 75,000 documents (12,500 positive, 12,500 negative, 50,000 unlabeled); 25,000 held-out test documents were used for an out-of-sample check (p.9-11). Mean review length 184 tokens, max 2,085 (p.13). Supreme Court: all cases 1792-2010 from justia.com, split into opinion-level files; only majority opinions retained; matched to the Supreme Court Database (Spaeth et al. 2012). About 26,000 unique majority opinions spanning 217 years (p.18-19). Mean majority opinion length 2,097 tokens. Justice-level analyses cover justices with more than 50 majority opinions; the text cites 84 justices (p.23). Unit of analysis: document (review or majority opinion), aggregated to justice-level means.

**Method.** Minimally-supervised dictionary construction. (1) Train GloVe word vectors (text2vec in R) on the corpus: 300 dimensions, Xmax = 10, context window 50 tokens, drop the 20 most frequent tokens, tokens in more than 90% of documents, and tokens appearing fewer than 90 times, with AdaGrad (p.6-9). (2) Seeds: 10 uncontroversial positive terms (e.g., "superb", "brilliant", "great") and 10 negative terms (e.g., "bad", "awful", "horrible") (fn.3, p.7). (3) Extract the top 500 positive and top 500 negative words by cosine similarity to the seed-sum vectors, removing SMART stop words and AFINN opposite-valence terms (fn.4, p.7-9). (4) Weight token counts by tf-idf and by cosine similarity to get similarity-weighted positive and negative counts (Wp, Wn). (5) Polarity = (sum Wp - sum Wn) / (sum Wp + sum Wn), centered and scaled for comparison (fn.6, p.8). Accuracy uses a zero cutpoint. Sensitivity tests: corpus size (Figure 2), number of extracted words (appendix), and temporal re-estimation. Validity: benchmark accuracy against assigned ratings (with comparison to LIWC, AFINN, and supervised baselines in Table 1), convergent validity (polarity vs. number of dissenting votes, Figure 3), and discriminant validity (justice-level differences from LIWC and AFINN, Figures 4-5).

**Identification.** None in the causal sense. The paper is a measurement-validity paper. Validity is assessed by (a) classification accuracy against the benchmark review ratings, in-sample and on a held-out 25,000-review set; (b) convergent validity with voting divisions (number of dissenting votes); and (c) discriminant validity against LIWC and AFINN justice-level estimates and against historical narratives. The Supreme Court validity evidence is described as "suggestive - though not conclusive" (p.25).

**Scope.** Population: English-language movie reviews from IMDB (benchmark), and all U.S. Supreme Court majority opinions available from justia.com for 1792-2010 (about 26,000 opinions). Institution: the U.S. Supreme Court. Era: 1792-2010, split into pre-1891 (Evarts Act), 1891-1925 (Judges Act and the start of the consensus norm's decline), and post-1925 windows (p.19-20). Claims are limited to document-level polarity. The paper says the method is not a universal substitute for supervised learning (p.15).

## Key findings
- Corpus-based dictionary polarity classifies movie reviews more accurately than standard dictionaries. In-sample accuracy is 80.4% (12,004 negative, 12,663 positive, 332 neutral) versus 69.4% for LIWC (with scaling) and 71.3% for AFINN. (80.4% vs 69.4% (LIWC) vs 71.3% (AFINN); 12,004 neg / 12,663 pos / 332 neutral; Section 3, p.10; Figure 1, p.11-12)
- Out-of-sample (held-out 25,000 reviews) accuracy is consistent with in-sample accuracy: 79.5% for the corpus-based approach, 69.7% for LIWC, and 70.6% for AFINN. (79.5% (ours, held-out) vs 69.7% (LIWC) vs 70.6% (AFINN); Section 3, p.11-12; Figure 1 bottom row)
- Accuracy gains hold across corpus sizes. Even 5,000-document corpora beat the LIWC and AFINN accuracy figures, and accuracy approaches standard machine-learning levels by about 15,000 documents. Accuracy rises with corpus size, with diminishing returns after about 30,000. (5,000 docs beats 69.4%/71.3%; ~15,000 docs reaches roughly 80%; mean review 184 tokens (max 2,085); Section 3.1, p.13-14; Figure 2; p.16)
- Compared with supervised classifiers on the same movie-review benchmark, the approach matches Naive Bayes, MaxEnt, and SVM (about 79-80%) but falls short of Pang and Lee (2004, 87.15%) and Maas et al. (2011, 88.05%). The paper stresses it uses no classification labels. (Ours 80.4; Pang, Lee and Vaithyanathan (2002) NB 79.7, MaxEnt 79.7, SVM 79.4 (mean); Pang and Lee 2004 87.15; Maas et al. 2011 88.05; Section 3.2, Table 1, p.15-17)
- Dictionaries estimated on temporal subsets of the Court's opinions differ sharply. Only 34.0% of negative and 27.7% of positive terms carry over from the early Court to the turn-of-century Court; only 24.2% of negative and 19.6% of positive terms carry over from the turn-of-century Court to the modern Court. (34.0% neg / 27.7% pos (early to turn-of-century); 24.2% neg / 19.6% pos (turn-of-century to modern); Section 4, p.19-20)
- Convergent validity: only the corpus-based polarity measure declines consistently as the number of dissenting votes rises. LIWC and AFINN show increases in polarity at various points, most notably in 6-3 decisions. In the modern era, the corpus-based measure treats unanimous opinions as generally positive. (Qualitative pattern across dissent counts; no coefficient reported; Section 4, Figure 3, p.21-22)
- Discriminant validity: the corpus-based justice-level polarity is only moderately correlated with LIWC and AFINN. The Spearman rank correlation between LIWC and AFINN is 0.64, but the corpus-based measure correlates 0.29 with LIWC and 0.16 with AFINN. The standard deviation of justice-level mean polarity is 0.25 (ours), 0.22 (LIWC), and 0.16 (AFINN). (rho(LIWC,AFINN)=0.64; rho(ours,LIWC)=0.29; rho(ours,AFINN)=0.16; SD 0.25 / 0.22 / 0.16; Section 4, p.23-24)
- Divergence between the corpus-based measure and LIWC/AFINN shows an inflection at 1925. The paper treats this as the point after which the measures diverge less. (Inflection around 1925 (no coefficient reported); Section 4, Figure 4, p.23)
- Justice-level examples: Harold Burton ranks as the most positive justice in the corpus-based measure, while AFINN ranks him 14th of 84 and LIWC 50th. The corpus-based measure gives Chief Justice White a substantial drop in polarity. Chief Justice Waite is marginally negative in the corpus-based measure, versus marginally positive in LIWC. Chief Justice Taney wrote significantly more negative majority opinions than under either comparison dictionary. Historical narratives are cited as support, but the paper calls the evidence suggestive rather than conclusive. (Burton: 1st (ours) vs 14th of 84 (AFINN) and 50th (LIWC); Section 4, Figure 5, p.23-25)
- Sample scale: about 26,000 majority opinions across 217 years, averaging 2,097 tokens per opinion, which the paper calls longer than the longest movie review. (~26,000 opinions; 217 years; mean 2,097 tokens; Section 4, p.19-20)

## Limitations
- Not a substitute for supervised learning when labeled benchmarks exist; the paper says it does not argue for universal replacement (p.15).
- Measurement is at the document level. It obscures which subjects receive sentiment, and it does not capture sentiment within documents, which matters for long opinions (p.27).
- Generalizability to other specialized vocabularies is untested. The paper asks whether the method 'travels well' and flags within-document variation in sentiment-laden word use as a concern (p.27).
- Dictionaries may include noisy non-subjective terms; the authors plan to adopt Pang and Lee (2004) subjectivity filtering in future work (p.27).
- The Supreme Court validation relies on voting divisions, which the authors call an imperfect proxy because consensual norms mask disagreement, and on historical narratives; the paper calls the result 'suggestive - though not conclusive' (p.17, p.20, p.25).
- Temporal splits (pre-1891, 1891-1925, post-1925) are chosen on theoretical grounds; the authors note rolling windows as an alternative for semantic-change studies (fn.14, p.19).
- Accuracy is computed with a zero cutpoint, and neutral reviews (332) are excluded from accuracy; LIWC is reported with and without scaling (p.10).
- Unlabeled reviews are retained in the word-vector corpus, which the authors describe as a conservative test (p.9, p.14).
- Majority opinions only are analyzed; concurrences and dissents are dropped for comparability across eras (p.18).
- Terms were not stemmed (fn.13, p.19).
- The appendix (corpus-size and seed-set robustness, opinion-length density) is referenced but not in the PDF.

## Gaps and flags
- No domain stoplist or masking before LSD scoring: 'credit union', 'interchange charges', 'NCUA oversight', 'debt' and 'risk' count as tone in every CU release (stage5_lsd.R:48-61).
- LSD intensity on congressional press releases has never been validated against human-coded intensity; the brain-side 3-case smoke test was not reproduced here. Rice & Zorn's three-part design (benchmark accuracy, convergent validity against a behavioural proxy, discriminant validity against alternative dictionaries) is a ready template.
- No dictionary-sensitivity test for the Press pillar: LSD intensity vs a corpus-derived dictionary vs stance sign only, measured as Kendall tau on Press% and on the top 133.
- expand_tone_lexicon() was never run, and the CU-relevant corpus (1,563 text-bearing releases) is below the ~5,000-document size where the paper shows gains. A viable corpus-based dictionary would need the full unfiltered press corpus as the unlabeled training set, as the paper does with its 50,000 unlabeled reviews.
- Document-level tone cannot say which target the tone is about (paper p.27). The system already handles this by separating stance (DEBATE, two-hop), but press_value still multiplies a target-specific sign by a target-blind magnitude.
- Citation fidelity: cu_press_release_scraper.R:131-132 and 2799 cite Rice & Zorn for the legacy polarity, but the weights are hand-assigned 1/2/3, not corpus-derived cosine/tf-idf. The expand helper departs from the paper on window (5 vs 50), dimensions (50 vs 300), minimum count (5 vs 90), seed geometry and corpus floor. Describe it as 'R&Z polarity formula over a curated lexicon', not as a Rice & Zorn dictionary.
- The LSD domain-term contamination figures (4.5% positive mass, 7.0% negative mass, intensity rho 0.953, sign flips on 4.1%, press_value rho 0.976) are new agent analysis from a single path and are single-path, unreproduced and UNVERIFIED. Each figure covers 1,563 text-bearing releases in cu_press_stance_tone.csv; member-level aggregates were not checked.
- Component record drift: melange-psychometrics.affective-n describes the measure as z(LIWC) + z(LLM) (not checked in verification), but emit_affective_n.py:39-43 uses LIWC only as a convergent anchor.
- The paper's court validity is 'suggestive - though not conclusive' (p.25), and its benchmark is movie reviews. Do not quote the 80.4% vs 69.4% gap as an expected gain for congressional press releases.
- No conflict with the barred SNA proposals, D-021, D-025 or the reciprocity rulings: the paper does not touch networks, lobbying or money.
