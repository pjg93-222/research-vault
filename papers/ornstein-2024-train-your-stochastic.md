# How to train your stochastic parrot: large language models for political texts

**Joseph T. Ornstein, Elise N. Blasingame, Jake S. Truscott (2025).** *Political Science Research and Methods, vol. 13, pp. 264-281 (Cambridge University Press, open access CC BY 4.0). Received 12 Sep 2023, revised 23 Jul 2024, accepted 3 Aug 2024. Authors at University of Georgia (Ornstein, Blasingame) and University of Florida (Truscott)..* [DOI 10.1017/psrm.2024.64](https://doi.org/10.1017/psrm.2024.64). Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Ornstein, Blasingame and Truscott (PSRM 2025, DOI 10.1017/psrm.2024.64) test few-shot prompts to GPT-3 and GPT-4 on four political text tasks. The tasks are sentiment in 945 SCOTUS tweets, tone in 935 campaign ads, ideology in 18,263 sentences from UK manifestos, and virtue-topic labels for 9,565 House one-minute speeches. On the tweets, GPT-3 got 88.4% of negative/positive signs right, against 64.3% for TweetNLP, 57.7% for Naive Bayes and 38.3% for the best dictionary. On ideology, LLM scores correlate with expert scores at rho 0.92 (economic) and 0.80 (social) across the 18 manifestos. LLM topic labels beat three random intruders in 80% of 400 blinded checks, where chance is 25%. For the 935 ad codings, LLM coding cost $0.60 against $565.20 for crowd coding (April 2024 OpenAI rates, footnote 6), about a 1000-fold reduction for that task.
> 
> Three method points matter more than the headline numbers:
> - Continuous measures are built from the label probability vector, not the argmax label.
> - GPT-4, tuned with human feedback (RLHF), is overconfident. For over 70% of posts it put more than 99% on one label, and it chose Neutral more often than the human coders.
> - Validation runs in three steps: tune the prompt on one hand-labelled set, check it on a second, then apply.
> 
> The authors also warn that the GPT-3 models they used were retired in January 2024, so the results cannot be reproduced.
> 
> For Paul's system, the paper does not bear on networks, money or allocation. It bears on the stance and text layer. Melange already supplies outside context in the prompt for its Claude stance rater, and its member-reference and persona layers cache LLM outputs by model (the Claude stance rater writes a resumable CSV with no model key). The gaps are elsewhere:
> - run_stage5.R keeps DEBATE's per-label probabilities as columns, but press_value is a sign multiplied by dictionary intensity and ignores them.
> - The Claude rater reports a confidence it states itself, not a calibrated probability.
> - Regex topic routing has never been checked against human labels.
> - The tweet gold queue has blank api_* placeholder columns next to the human gold columns, reserved for an optional pre-label pass.
> 
> The paper measures sentiment toward court rulings, not stance toward a target, so its accuracy figures should not be read as an expected accuracy for CU stance.

## Study

**Question.** Can few-shot prompts to large language models (GPT-3, GPT-4) be adapted, without task-specific training or large labeled sets, to political text-as-data tasks (sentiment classification, ad tone classification, ideology scaling, topic discovery), matching or beating conventional automated methods and approaching expert or crowd coding at far lower cost? The paper also asks what validation and prompt-design practices make such measures trustworthy.

**Theory.** Text-as-data measurement (Grimmer and Stewart 2013: "validation, validation, validation"). Contextualized word embeddings and transformer self-attention let word meaning depend on context (Vaswani et al. 2017), so context-dependent labels such as sarcasm can be learned from a prompt rather than from a trained classifier. Few-shot in-context learning (Brown et al. 2020). For topics, the expectation that conservatives praise loyalty, patriotism and hard work while liberals praise fairness, compassion and charity follows Moral Foundations Theory (Graham et al. 2009), p.275.

**Data.** Four applications. (1) 945 tweets referencing the US Supreme Court within 24 hours of Masterpiece Cakeshop (2018) and of Trump v. Mazars and Trump v. Vance (2020); unit = tweet; author-coded 7-point sentiment from -1 to +1 after two-stage manual coding (1000 labeled, 55 dropped, 945 kept; Fleiss kappa 0.72). Benchmark classifier trained on 1.2 million tweets from Go et al. (2009). (2) 935 political ads from Carlson and Montgomery (2017); unit = ad; expert ratings and crowd pairwise comparisons (9420 tasks at 6 cents, $565.20 total). (3) 18,263 sentences from 18 British party manifestos, 1987-2010; crowd-coded per Benoit et al. (2016), with an expert-score benchmark. (4) 9,704 one-minute US House floor speeches in the 113th Congress (2013-2014) from Wilkerson and Casas (2017); 9,565 analyzed because 139 speeches over 6,000 characters were omitted for API token limits (fn. 8, p.275). Models: GPT-3 (175B parameters) and GPT-4; GPT-3 was removed from the public API in January 2024 (p.278).

**Method.** Few-shot prompting. The LLM returns a next-word probability vector over labels, and continuous measures are built from it. Sentiment (tweets): six few-shot examples, two per class (positive, neutral, negative), with a preamble describing the ruling; the continuous measure is the first principal component of the probability vector (pre-registered via AsPredicted 92341, 92422, 92666, 100718, 125217; p.267-268). Ad tone: one-shot prompt (Figure 3), continuous tone from the probability vector. Ideology: one-shot Policy Prompt applied per sentence; sentence score = P(Conservative) minus P(Liberal); averaged to economic and social passage means per manifesto (p.273). GPT-4 ideology deviated from the pre-registered protocol and used the Le Mens and Gallego (2023) whole-document prompt (p.273). Topics: prompt asks GPT-3 for virtue labels; 51,483 labels returned, about 5-6 per speech, grouped by synonyms (p.275-276). Benchmarks: dictionary sentiment, Naive Bayes (bag of words), TweetNLP (RoBERTa fine-tuned on tweets, Camacho-Collados et al. 2022). Appendix A has zero-shot and one-shot variants (not in the text file).

**Identification.** None in the causal sense. The design is measurement validation: each LLM measure is correlated with or compared against human gold labels (author codes, expert ratings, crowd codes), and topic labels are validated by an optimal-label task in which a blinded coder picks the best of four labels for 400 speeches (80 percent agreed with GPT-3's label, chance 25 percent; p.276).

**Scope.** Population and era: US tweets about SCOTUS decisions (2018-2020); US campaign ads from the Carlson and Montgomery (2017) corpus (dates not stated in text); UK party manifestos 1987-2010; US House one-minute floor speeches 2013-2014. Models: GPT-3 and GPT-4 (2023-2024 API). The paper argues about political science text-as-data generally, and cautions about closed proprietary models and about tasks needing current or factual information (p.278-279).

## Key findings

- Sentiment on 945 SCOTUS tweets: GPT-3 few-shot correctly predicts negative/positive in 88.4 percent of cases; TweetNLP 64.3 percent; best dictionary method 38.3 percent; Naive Bayes trained on 1.2M tweets 57.7 percent. (Section 3.1, p.268 (lines 280-285))
- GPT-4 performs slightly worse than GPT-3 on the sentiment task; its probabilities are poorly calibrated and overconfident; for over 70 percent of posts GPT-4 gives a single-label probability above 99 percent; it returns Neutral more often than the authors. (Section 3.1, p.268 (lines 287-292))
- Ad tone: LLM coding of 935 ads took under one minute and cost $0.60, against $565.20 for crowd pairwise coding (9420 tasks at 6 cents), about a 1000-fold cost reduction. The LLM tone measure is said to correlate with expert ratings as strongly as the crowd measure; the text gives no coefficient for this. (Section 3.2, p.269 (crowd cost lines 326-327) and p.271 (lines 355-358))
- Ideology scaling of 18 UK manifestos: LLM scores correlate with expert ratings at rho = 0.92 on the economic dimension and rho = 0.8 on the social dimension; better at between-party than within-party variance (true for crowd codes too). (Section 3.3, p.273 (lines 441-445), Figure 6)
- Cost of ideology scoring: about $2.50 for 18,263 sentences with GPT-3, against about $3,226.70 for the original crowd coding (footnote assumes 1.5 cents per sentence and 215,107 crowd evaluations). (Section 3.3, p.273 (lines 446-451, fn. 7))
- Topic labeling of 9,565 House speeches returned 51,483 labels (about 5-6 per speech). Democrats more often praised advocacy (+3.5 pp), charity (+1.5), compassion (+3.9), education (+3.3), fairness (+3). Republicans more often praised bravery (+2), patriotism (+4.2), loyalty (+2.1), sacrifice (+2.8), success (+7.7). (Section 3.4, p.275 (line 514) and p.276 (lines 525-533), Figure 8)
- Topic label validation: for 400 random speeches a blinded coder picked GPT-3's label over three random intruders in 80 percent of cases, against 25 percent chance. (Section 3.4, p.276 (lines 534-542))
- The model is not a dictionary method: 5,332 speeches contain the word honor, but GPT-3 labels only 145 as praising honor, nearly all about soldiers or veterans. (Section 3.4, p.276 (lines 543-549))
- Cost trend: computing cost of the paper's applications fell 97 percent between fall 2021 and publication. (Section 5 Conclusion, p.279 (lines 648-650))
- Reproducibility: GPT-3 models used were removed from the public API in January 2024, so exact results cannot be reproduced unless the weights are released. (Section 4 Discussion, p.278 (lines 598-605))
- Bias: GPT-3 answered 58 percent of 817 misconception questions truthfully (Lin et al. 2021), cited as a reason for caution on factual-accuracy tasks. (Section 4 Discussion, p.278 (lines 614-615))

## Limitations

- Results depend on researcher choices, especially prompt design; the authors recommend a three-step validation: hand-label a small set for prompt design, validate on a second hand-labeled set, then apply to the rest (p.277, lines 567-575).
- GPT-4 is worse-calibrated than GPT-3 for next-word classification because of RLHF (p.268, lines 293-296; p.278, lines 587-597).
- Reproducibility depends on proprietary, closed models that vendors may stop serving; the GPT-3 models used were retired from the API (p.278).
- LLMs inherit human biases and falsehoods, including stereotyped embeddings, and can generate hate speech; RLHF mitigates this at some cost to accuracy (p.278, lines 611-624).
- Authors advise against tasks that need up-to-date factual information (p.278-279).
- Ad tone: model diverges from experts on attacks against unnamed targets, and on ads needing outside context; the authors argue one expert label is arguably miscoded (p.271, lines 359-368).
- Sentiment: tweets whose meaning depends on outside political context can be misread unless the prompt supplies that context.
- Pre-registration deviation: GPT-4 ideology used a different, non-pre-registered protocol (p.273, lines 433-440).
- Speeches over 6,000 characters (139) were dropped because of API token limits (p.275, fn. 8).
- Topic labels are checked only by the 400-speech intruder task; the LDA comparison is conceptual and cited, not estimated in this paper.
- GPT-4 showed little or no improvement over GPT-3 on the ideology and sentiment tasks, so the best-model choice is not settled across tasks (p.273, lines 438-440).

## Gaps and flags

- Probability-based continuous stance is unused. DEBATE's p_support and p_oppose are written but not aggregated (run_stage5.R:98,140), and press_value takes direction from an argmax sign alone.
- The Claude stance rater has no calibrated probabilities: its confidence is stated by the model in its JSON reply, and API errors silently become neutral at confidence 0 (claude_stance.py:80,89-91).
- The regex category router (CATKW, build_media_blend.py:50) has no human-label validation. The paper's 400-item intruder task is a ready, cheap protocol for this.
- Gold queues carry model_stance and blank api_* placeholder columns next to the gold_* columns (build_tweet_gold_queue.py:92-96). Nothing records whether gold labelling was blind to the model, so the assumption that gold is independent of the pipeline is untested.
- No plan exists for retiring a model. The caches keep outputs, but no documented rule says how a successor model's scores are bridged or re-validated before they replace cached ones.
- Flag: The paper measures SENTIMENT toward SCOTUS rulings, not target-specific STANCE. The brain separates sentiment from stance, so do not quote the 88.4% figure as an expected accuracy for CU stance.
- Flag: The paper's numbers come from GPT-3 and GPT-4 (2023-2024 API), and GPT-3 was retired in January 2024. Its figures cannot be reproduced and say nothing directly about Claude or DEBATE.
- Flag: The cost figures ($0.60 vs $565.20; $2.50 vs about $3,226.70) are OpenAI prices from that period. They describe the window and denominator stated in the paper (935 ads; 18,263 sentences), not Melange's current costs.
- Flag: Any replacement of a live reader needs a pre-registered single look against held-out gold.
- Flag: Nothing in the paper touches the rejected SNA proposals, LDA edges, reciprocity, graph-rank or influence wording.

The 88.4% figure is a sentiment task, not CU stance. Context-dependent items fail unless the prompt supplies context, which the system's Claude rater already does.
