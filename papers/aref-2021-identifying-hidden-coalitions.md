# Identifying hidden coalitions in the US House

**Samin Aref, Zachary P. Neal (2021).** *Scientific Reports 11:19939.* [10.1038/s41598-021-98139-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-98139-w) Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Aref and Neal build signed House networks from co-sponsorship for 19 sessions (1981-2017, about 445 members each). They use an SDSM backbone, so an edge is positive when a pair co-sponsors significantly more than chance and negative when significantly less (alpha .01, Holm). They then solve two binary linear programs to global optimality: the minimum frustrated edges for a fixed k, and the smallest k that reaches the overall minimum. In every session the party split leaves more frustrated edges than the optimal 2-partition, and that leaves more than the optimal 3-partition. k*min runs from 3 to 7, but gains past k=3 are small. The third coalition is small (4 to 69 members) and its ideology shifts from session to session. It is the most effective coalition in most sessions on the CEL LES. It also has an unusual sign pattern: 21.18 negative ties per positive tie, against 2.68 for the two main blocs, and 25.6% of its negative ties go to co-partisans, against 8.44%. All of this is descriptive. There are no controls and no causal design.
>
> What this means for the system as built: `fec/network/signed_coalitions.py` cites this paper but works on a different graph. It partitions the LLM-valenced member-mention layer, which mixes House and Senate and is 83% positive by edge count (5,959 of 7,153 edges), using a heuristic local search capped at k=3. Its stored artifact (2026-07-19) shows a single main bloc plus a 14-member splinter at k=2. At k=3 the third cluster has one member, and frustration barely moves (1040.79 to 1039.79 weighted). So the system has not reproduced the paper's finding and has not tested it. The paper's real substrate, a signed co-sponsorship backbone, is not built in the live code. The June vote backbone dropped its roll-call negative ties, and whether they carry the same structure as the paper's co-sponsorship negatives is untested. Only the CEL LES join is cheap, because that data is already on disk; a House-only reproduction would also need a signed co-sponsorship backbone and a near-exact k=3 partition, neither of which is built. The signed-coalitions output is not purely diagnostic: the member desk reads its k=2 splinter and sets an 'antag' flag, so this heuristic mention-layer result reaches a display surface (D-025 wording applies).

## Paul's annotation

From [[annotations/Lobbying - Lobbying - Annotated Bibliography|Lobbying - Lobbying - Annotated Bibliography]]:

> Aref, Samin, and Zachary P. Neal. “Identifying hidden coalitions in the US House of Representatives by optimally partitioning signed networks based on generalized balance.” *Scientific Reports*, vol. 11, no. 1, 7 Oct. 2021, [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-98139-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-98139-w).
>
> This article by Stanford Professors Aref and Neal examines the use of three-cluster partitioned networks to identify hidden and unofficial partnerships and coalitions within the House of Representatives, highlighting highly effective legislative groups and duos whose actions otherwise avoid typical headlines. The two main models utilized are an optimal k-partition and k-clusterability index. These methods, when used in tandem, allow for frustrated edges, a result of at times 30,000 variables, to be smoothed over. This methodology is highly valuable to the present research as it both presents an alternative layer that can be substituted or added into future network models, but more importantly allows for massive sets of data to be made computationally efficient, something that will become necessary as additional pipelines are worked into future models. Additionally, the main finding of this paper was a third general coalition within the House of Representatives, one whose median ideology is highly unstable yet highly effective. This coalition can be seen as the truly vulnerable or responsive seats, those like Brian Fitzpatrick who are forced to swing ideologically toward their constituents because their districts are so competitive. Finally, the fundamental weakness of this article is that there is no path-finding algorithm as of yet, and the authors did not measure the shifts of this coalition over time to see how this coalition's ideology responded to changes in national mood.

## Study

**Question.** (1) Can globally optimal partitions of a signed network into k mutually divisive, internally cohesive clusters (generalized balance / k-clusterability) be computed for realistic social networks? (2) What is the smallest k that minimizes frustrated edges? (3) Does partitioning US House legislators by their signed collaboration and opposition ties fit the chamber better than partitioning by party, and what does the resulting structure say about legislative effectiveness?

**Theory.** Generalized (k-) balance and clusterability theory (Davis 1967; Cartwright and Harary 1976), extending classic structural balance (Heider 1944; Cartwright and Harary 1956). A signed network is k-balanced if its nodes split into k coalitions with positive ties within and negative ties between. Distance from balance is the count of frustrated edges. Coalitions are clusters and need not be cohesive or collaborating (p.5). The political reading is that a third, ideologically fluid splinter coalition may organize the chamber beyond party.

**Data.** Sample: 19 US House networks, one per two-year session, with session start years 1981 through 2017 (the text also says the study covers 1981 to 2018, p.2). Unit: legislator pairs, each edge signed positive (significantly more co-sponsorships than chance) or negative (significantly fewer). About 445 nodes per network (paper: n ~ 445). Edges: 4,954 to 31,936 (p.4). Source: bill co-sponsorship data, bipartite legislator-bill network, then bipartite projection, then signed backbone (p.7). Ideology: 1st-dimension Nokken-Poole scores from Voteview, -1 liberal to +1 conservative, varying by session (p.8). Effectiveness: Center for Effective Lawmaking scores from 15 indicators, normalized to mean 1 per session (p.8). The paper says code and data are public in the SI (p.8).

**Method.** Two binary linear programs, solved to global optimality with Gurobi 9.1 on a 32-core Windows Server VM (p.4). Eq. (1), the k-partition model: binary x_ic assigns node i to cluster c, and f_ij flags frustrated edges. The objective is the minimum frustrated edges for fixed k, C_k(G). Constraints are O(n^2). Eq. (2), a reformulation of the correlation clustering / clique partitioning model (Demaine et al. 2006) for non-complete graphs: binary y_ij marks same-cluster pairs, with constraints over connected triads T. It returns C(G), the minimum over all k, and k*min. Constraints are O(n^3). Signed networks come from the Stochastic Degree Sequence Model in the R backbone package (v1.5.0), with family-wise error alpha = 0.01 via Holm-Bonferroni (p.7-8). Comparison: frustrated-edge counts for party partitions vs optimal k-partitions for k = 2..7. Then coalition mean legislative effectiveness and median ideology, with 95% CI bands (Fig. 4). Analysis is descriptive and per session, with no regression reported.</method>
<parameter name="identification">None in the causal sense. The design is descriptive. The signed network is inferred from co-sponsorship, and optimal partitions are compared with party partitions on frustrated-edge fit. Coalition effectiveness is compared in descriptive plots with CI bands, with no model controlling for party, majority status or committee assignment. The authors discuss the majority-party effect qualitatively (p.6).

**Scope.** Population: members of the US House of Representatives, sessions starting 1981 through 2017 (text says 1981 to 2018). Institution: the lower chamber only. Era: 1981 to 2018, with contrasts between the 1981-1993 and 2003-2017 sessions (p.5). The optimization methods are general for signed networks up to about 30,000 edges (p.4, p.8). The substantive claims are limited to the House.

## Key findings

- Globally optimal k-partitions (Eq. 1) and the minimal-k clusterability index (Eq. 2) are computationally feasible for the 19 House networks, up to about 30,000 edges. Numbers: Eq. 1 averages 3.3 h; Eq. 2 averages 14 h for networks up to ~30,000 edges. n ~ 445; 4,954 <= m <= 31,936. (p.4 (Methods); p.7-8 (Discussion))
- In every session the party partition leaves more frustrated edges than the optimal 2-partition, which leaves more than the optimal 3-partition. Three coalitions fit the chamber better than two or party. Numbers: C_party(G) > C_2(G) > C_3(G) in every session. (p.5, Fig. 2 and text)
- Beyond k = 3 the frustrated-edge count declines only marginally and then stagnates. For all 19 networks, C(G) = C_k(G) for every k <= 7, so the minimal clusterability index is reached at small k. Numbers: k*min ranges from 3 to 7. Fig. 2 legend: 1981 (6), 1983 (7), 1985 (6), 1987 (5), 1989 (6), 1991 (6), 1993 (4), 1995 (3), 1997 (4), 1999 (5), 2001 (4), 2003 (4), 2005 (5), 2007 (4), 2009 (5), 2011 (4), 2013 (4), 2015 (4), 2017 (4). The authors use k = 3 as the parsimonious choice. (p.5 (Fig. 2 discussion); p.6)
- The third coalition's size and median ideology vary across sessions, so it is an ideologically fluid group and not a fixed ideological bloc. Numbers: Third coalition ranges from 4 members (113th, 2013) to 69 (111th, 2009). Its ideology ranges from very liberal (98th-102nd, 1983-1991) to center-left (103rd, 111th) to center-right (105th-110th, 1997-2007). (p.6 (Fig. 3 discussion))
- The third coalition is the most effective coalition in most sessions. Its inclusion explains why the two dominant generalized-balance coalitions are less effective than their party-defined counterparts. Numbers: No pooled effect size or test reported; effectiveness is shown as session means with 95% CI bands. (p.6 (Fig. 4 discussion))
- Third-coalition effectiveness is anchored by a few very effective members during stable party control. Effectiveness dips during transitions when the majority changes. Numbers: 99th-102nd (1985-1990): Pat Williams 4.49, Barney Frank 4.02, Daniel Glickman 3.68. 106th-108th (1999-2004): Christopher Smith 8.44, Bill Young 4.41, Nancy Johnson 2.98. 114th-115th (2015-2018): Edward Royce 5.46, John Katko 5.36, Dave Reichert 2.30. (p.6-7)
- The third coalition has a distinctive sign structure: far more negative (opposition) ties per positive tie, and more of its opposition ties run to co-partisans. Numbers: Traditional coalitions: 2.68 negative edges per positive edge. Third coalition: 21.18 (SI Fig. S3). Co-partisan share of negative edges: 8.44% for traditional coalition members, 25.6% for third coalition members. (p.7 (Results, coalition effectiveness, final paragraph))
- Party, classic balance (k = 2) and generalized balance (k = 3) all show a majority-party effectiveness advantage. The authors tie the majority party's effectiveness to agenda control and majority vote share. They also say part of the advantage comes from the highly effective third coalition, which is usually ideologically aligned with the majority. They do not directly credit the third coalition's own effectiveness to agenda control. Numbers: No numerical effect size given for the majority advantage. (p.6 (Results) and p.7 (Discussion))
- Party-based coalitions show increasing ideological polarization over time, and classic-balance coalitions look similar. Generalized balance adds a smaller, fluid third group between two diverging dominant coalitions. Numbers: No regression or correlation values reported. Descriptive only. (p.5-6, Fig. 3 discussion)

## Limitations

- No dedicated limitations section. In-text caveats: the models can yield multiple optimal solutions, so they do not return a unique partition (p.3-4).
- Scale: the models are demonstrated on networks up to about 30,000 edges. The authors call this a minor limitation because many empirical signed networks are sparser, and backbone methods can sparsify dense ones (p.8).
- The median ideology of the third coalition is unstable across sessions, so it is not a fixed ideological category (p.7).
- Coalition membership 'does not necessarily imply cohesion with other members of the same coalition' (p.5).
- The effectiveness comparisons are descriptive plots with 95% CI bands. No controls or inferential tests are reported in the main text (p.6-7).
- Signed ties come from a statistical backbone of co-sponsorship, so their validity depends on that inference step (p.7).

## Gaps and flags

- No signed co-sponsorship backbone exists in the live code. The only signed substrates are the LLM-valenced mention layer and a June vote backbone with its negatives dropped, so the paper's House result has never been tested on a comparable graph.
- There is no exact (ILP) partition. The local search returns upper bounds only. k is capped at 3, so k*min (3-7 in the paper) is never estimated, and there is no check for multiple optimal solutions.
- Partitions are not per Congress and not per chamber. The paper's result is per session and House-only, and it relies on that time-indexing to show the third coalition's fluidity. This is also Paul's 'no tracking over time' critique, which the system shares.
- Coalition membership is not joined to CEL LES, even though the data is on disk at fec/lep. The LES join is cheap, but the paper's central effectiveness comparison has not been run, because it also needs the unbuilt signed co-sponsorship backbone and k=3 partition.
- There is no member-level measure of the share of negative ties that go to co-partisans (25.6% vs 8.44% in the paper). bpf and cooperation_v2 capture only cross-party positive share.
- DOC CONFLICT: the component card for melange-sna.signed-coalitions says 'R backbone sdsm (alpha .05, signed)' and 'seeded by positive Louvain'. The live fec/network/signed_coalitions.py uses neither. It reads member_references.json (LLM mention valence), seeds with a party split plus random restarts, and searches only k<=3. The sdsm and Louvain description belongs to the June workbook Phase-2 sheet and the vote backbone (brain june-network-plan:39,54).
- REJECTED-PROPOSAL FLAG: SDSM keeps legislator and bill degree sequences in its null, so using it as an engine edge weight would amount to per-edge volume normalization, which was measured and REJECTED here. Keep any SDSM layer diagnostic and separate from kernel weights. The paper does not argue for dropping regular cosponsorship edges.
- The June decision to drop negative vote-backbone edges as 'maverickness' may differ from the paper's evidence that co-partisan negative ties mark the effective third coalition (p.7), but the paper's ties come from co-sponsorship and the dropped ones were roll-call votes, so whether they share that structure is untested. This is worth recording for Paul. It is not a ruling.
- Paul links this paper to competitive-seat money effects (competitiveness-multiplier, D-104..D-108). The paper says nothing about money, PVI or reciprocity and cannot be cited as support. Reciprocity is still not validated.
- The system's k=3 'third cluster' (1 member, mention layer, window through 2026-07-19 artifact, denominator 520 members across both chambers) must not be presented as the Aref-Neal third coalition or as a list of effective members. It is a heuristic model output and says nothing about closeness or access (D-025).
- Pooled cross-session comparisons should be stratified by majority party or labelled UNSTRATIFIED, because the paper itself says majority status drives LES.

The summary of the method and findings in Paul's annotation is broadly accurate. Two corrections: the roughly 30,000 figure is edges, not variables, and the method does not 'smooth over' frustrated edges, it counts and minimizes them exactly. He is right that the paper has no path-finding and no tracking against national mood. His reading of the third coalition as responsive or vulnerable swing seats (Fitzpatrick) is his own interpretation. The paper defines the bloc by signed-tie structure and LES, and never measures electoral vulnerability or persuadability. His note is now stale in one important way: it treats the paper as an available 'alternative network layer', but the built layer partitions a different graph (LLM mention valence, both chambers, k<=3 heuristic) and found no meaningful third coalition (one member; frustration changes by under 0.1% of weight from k=2 to k=3). The computational-efficiency point holds. The exact ILP is feasible at Melange's scale and has not been tried.
