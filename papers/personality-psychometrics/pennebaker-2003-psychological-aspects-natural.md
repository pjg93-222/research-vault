# Psychological Aspects of Natural Language Use: Our Words, Our Selves

**James W. Pennebaker, Matthias R. Mehl, Kate G. Niederhoffer (2003).** *Annual Review of Psychology, 54:547-77 (first published online as a Review in Advance, August 14, 2002).* [10.1146/annurev.psych.54.101601.145041](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.54.101601.145041) Theme: [[themes/personality-psychometrics|Personality & Psychometrics]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Pennebaker, Mehl and Niederhoffer (2003) is a narrative review with no new data. It argues that the way people use words, especially function words such as pronouns, articles and negations, works as a stable individual-difference marker. Their reliability evidence is moderate. Successive 2-minute segments of 5-minute talks correlate at r = 0.51 across 21 categories (Gleser et al. 1959). Four-week test-retest correlations of everyday speech among 52 college students are 0.41 for standard linguistic categories and only 0.24 for psychological-process categories (Mehl and Pennebaker 2002a). The link to personality is weak. Big Five self-reports correlate with word use at r = 0.10 to 0.16 in a few hundred students (Pennebaker and King 1999). Neuroticism goes with more negative-emotion words, fewer positive-emotion words and more first-person singular. Depression is tied to "I" specifically, not me, my or mine, in student personal essays (Rude et al. 2002). Emotion words make up under 5% of all words, so emotion counts are a weak readout of emotional state. In at least two studies, raters' judgments of a text's emotionality correlated more highly with its language than the writers' own self-reports did. The authors also note that word counts ignore context and irony.

## Study

**Question.** Can natural word use (open-ended speech and writing, read as style rather than content) serve as a reliable individual-difference marker and as a window onto social, situational, psychological and health processes; and which methods of counting words are available and useful (stated at p. 548)?

**Theory.** Word use is treated as a psychological marker, with the strongest emphasis on function words ("particles": pronouns, articles, prepositions, conjunctions, auxiliary verbs). The authors argue particles encode shared reference, social identity and self-focus, and cognitive style. Background traditions cited include psychoanalysis (Freud 1901; Lacan 1968), Ricoeur's account of how descriptions define meaning, Goffman's dramaturgy, politeness theory (Brown & Levinson 1987), communication accommodation theory, and verbal immediacy (Wiener & Mehrabian 1968). The review says no compelling psychological theory of word usage yet exists (p. 572). Narrative-writing and disclosure theory (Pennebaker & Francis 1996) is used to explain health effects through coherent narrative construction and shifts in perspective.

**Data.** None collected by the authors. The review summarizes evidence from many sources: General Inquirer and LIWC studies; Biber's corpus of 481 texts (about 1,000,000 words) across 23 genres (p. 554); Pennebaker & King (1999) writing samples from diaries, college essays and journal abstracts (p. 555); Mehl & Pennebaker (2002a) electronically activated recorder (EAR) samples of everyday conversation from 52 college students (p. 557); a cross-sectional age analysis of more than 3,000 participants from 45 studies, 21 labs and 3 countries, plus 10 authors (p. 556); Mulac et al. (2001) review of more than 30 gender studies (p. 557); Pennebaker & King (1999) Big Five correlations; Newman et al. (2002) five laboratory deception studies (p. 564); Giuliani's 14 press conferences during his first 5 years in office (p. 565); about 15 students wearing EARs after September 11, 2001 (p. 566); Princess Diana online chat groups (p. 566). Sample sizes for most cited health and clinical studies are not reported in the review. Periods covered span roughly 1941 to 2002, with many citations marked "submitted", "in press" or unpublished.

**Method.** Narrative review organized in sections: (1) six word-count and pattern-analysis methods (General Inquirer, TAS/C, Weintraub's verbal analysis, DICTION, LIWC, Biber factor analysis; LSA as a pattern approach), pp. 549-555; (2) psychometric reliability of word use, pp. 555-556; (3) demographic, personality, mood, need-state, self-esteem and psychopathology links, pp. 556-562; (4) situational, social, deception, upheaval and marital links, pp. 562-567; (5) word use and psychological and health change, including LIWC-based writing-intervention analyses and LSA pronoun-shift analyses, pp. 567-570; (6) discussion of which word classes to study and limitations, pp. 570-572. The review does not pool effects statistically and reports most effects as correlations, percentages or qualitative patterns.

**Identification.** Not applicable. The review contains no causal identification strategy of its own. Causal language is used loosely in places (e.g., pronoun use "predicting" health changes, p. 569), and the authors note bidirectional effects, particularly in social accommodation (p. 558).

**Scope.** Psychology, primarily English-language and largely US-based work, with some European and cross-cultural studies cited. Participants are mostly college students, clinical or patient samples, disclosure-study volunteers, public figures (Giuliani, Watergate transcripts, US presidents), novelists and poets, and online chat or everyday-conversation samples. The authors explicitly say they do not cover non-English languages, spoken versus written differences in depth, second-language learners or intelligence (p. 573). Political and campaign speech is discussed only briefly (Hart's DICTION, presidential speeches, Winter 1973, Zullow et al. 1988; pp. 552-553). Nothing on lobbying, legislators, elections, networks or allocation is presented as a finding.

## Key findings

- Word-use measures show temporal stability. Across 21 language categories in 5-minute talks, successive 2-minute intervals correlated on average at 0.51 (Gleser et al. 1959). Students' everyday conversation was re-sampled four weeks apart with test-retest r of 0.41 for standard linguistic variables and 0.24 for psychological-process variables. (r = 0.51 (21 categories, 2-min intervals); r = 0.41 standard variables, r = 0.24 psychological processes (EAR, Mehl & Pennebaker 2002a)) [Psychometric Properties of Word Use, pp. 555-556]
- Word use is consistent across topic and social context, so it can be treated as an individual-difference measure. Pennebaker & King (1999) found good internal consistency across text types for 36 LIWC dimensions, and word use was stable across home versus public and work versus amusement settings. (36 LIWC dimensions; text samples from diaries, essays and abstracts written across days and years) [Psychometric Properties of Word Use, pp. 555-556]
- Language changes with age. Older participants used more positive and fewer negative emotion words, fewer first-person singular references, more future tense and fewer past-tense verbs, and more cognitive-complexity words. Both the cross-sectional study and the novelist/poet longitudinal study converged. (More than 3,000 participants, 45 studies, 21 labs, 3 countries; 60 text dimensions; 10 authors) [Demographic Variables, Age, p. 556]
- Gender differences in word use are reported across studies, but the evidence is uneven. Mulac et al. (2001) found relatively unambiguous effects for 16 features; men used more quantity references, judgmental adjectives, directives and I-references, while women used more intensifiers, emotion references, uncertainty verbs, negations and hedges. Tag questions showed no consistent difference. In everyday conversations of 52 students, men used nearly four times as many swear words, and women used more filler words and positive-emotion references. (More than 30 studies, 16 features; 52 college students (EAR); men about 4x swear words) [Demographic Variables, Gender, pp. 557-558]
- Big Five self-reports correlate only modestly with word use. Pennebaker & King (1999) reported correlations of 0.10 to 0.16. Neuroticism related to more negative and fewer positive emotion words and more first-person singular; extraversion to positive emotion and social-process words; agreeableness to more positive and fewer negative emotion words. (r = 0.10 to 0.16 (Pennebaker & King 1999, multiple writing samples from several hundred college students)) [Traditional Personality Measures, The Big Five, p. 558]
- Judges' ratings of text emotionality correlated more strongly with language than writers' or speakers' self-reported emotionality did, which raises the question of which personality 'gold standard' to rely on. (At least two studies cited (Pennebaker & Francis 1996; Berry et al. 1997); no coefficients given in the review) [Traditional Personality Measures, p. 559]
- Depression and mania are linked to elevated first-person singular use. Currently depressed students used more I (not me/my/mine) in personal essays. Suicidal poets used fewer first-person plural and more first-person singular pronouns and more death words than nonsuicidal poets. Manic patients used first-person singular more often. The review reads this as self-preoccupation. (18 suicidal and nonsuicidal poets (Stirman & Pennebaker 2001); 10 manic patients (Lorenz & Cobb 1952); sample for Rude et al. (2002) not given in review) [Mental Health and Psychopathology, Depression and Suicidality, pp. 560-561]
- Physical health links are sparse but suggestive. First-person singular use in structured Type A interviews (self-involvement) related to coronary outcomes and remained significant after controlling for age, cholesterol, smoking and Type A behavior. Positive emotion words in autobiographical sketches from nuns predicted longer life expectancy. (Scherwitz and colleagues (no effect sizes given); 80 nuns (Danner et al. 2001; no effect sizes given)) [Physical Health and Health Behavior, pp. 561-562]
- Deception shows word-level signatures. Across five laboratory studies, truth-tellers used more first-person singular pronouns and more exclusive words (but, except, without) than liars, and liars showed slight but consistent elevations in negative emotion words. (Five laboratory studies (Newman et al. 2002); effect sizes not reported in review) [Deception and Honesty, p. 564]
- Shared and personal upheavals shift pronoun use. After Princess Diana's death, first-person plural in chat groups rose 135% and I fell 12% for about a week, returning to normal by 10 days. In the weeks after September 11, students' first-person plural rose and first-person singular fell for at least 5 days. Texas A&M bonfire coverage doubled first-person plural and negative emotion words and cut big words by more than 10%. During personal crises, Giuliani's first-person singular almost tripled in the period after his diagnosis and related events. (+135% first-person plural, -12% I (Diana chat groups); about 15 students (EAR, 9/11); doubling of first-person plural (Texas A&M); 14 press conferences (Giuliani, first 5 years)) [Emotional Upheavals, Personal and Shared Upheavals, pp. 565-566]
- Social interaction and marriage show word-level coordination and relational markers. Highly involved strangers used more certainty expressions, verbal immediacy and relational pronouns. Chat-room dyads matched each other's function-word and affect-word use turn by turn. Satisfied, traditional married couples used more we and less I. (120 participants (Cegala 1989); Sillars et al. (1997) large married-couple sample, numbers not given in review) [Social Interactions, pp. 566-567]
- Expressive writing about emotional upheavals improves health, and the best predictor is a rise in cognitive words over the writing days. Positive emotion words were linearly related to health gains, while negative emotion words showed an inverted-U relation. Changes in cognitive words explained more variance in health improvement than emotion words. Three independent labs reported related effects, including lymphocyte counts and working memory up to 12 weeks. (Writing typically 3-5 days, 15-30 min/day; six writing studies (Pennebaker et al. 1997); Petrie et al. (1999) medical students; Klein & Boals (2001) working memory) [Word Use as a Reflection of Psychological and Health Change, pp. 567-569]
- Pronoun style, not content, tracks health. Day-to-day pronoun shifts among trauma writers correlated 0.3 to 0.5 with changes in physician visits across three studies. LSA content similarity between days showed no relation to health in three writing studies. (r = 0.3 to 0.5 (three studies, Campbell & Pennebaker 2002); LSA: no relation (three studies)) [References to Self and Others, pp. 569-570]
- Particles dominate word use and are named as a research priority. Fewer than 200 common particles account for over half of the words people use, and emotion words are under 5% of words in speech and writing, so emotion-word counts predict emotional state weakly. (Under 200 particles = over 50% of words; emotion words under 5% of words) [Which Words Should We Be Studying?, pp. 570-571]
- Word-count methods differ in scope: Biber's factor analysis of 481 texts (about 1,000,000 words) from 23 genres yielded 6 factors; LIWC searches over 2,300 words in more than 70 dimensions; DICTION uses 10,000 search words with 5 master variables and 35 subfeatures, normed on 20,000 samples; TAS/C uses about 2,000 emotion words and 3,900 abstraction entries. Word-count methods are reliable and efficient but ignore context, irony and sarcasm. (Biber 481 texts / ~1,000,000 words / 23 genres / 67 variables / 6 factors; LIWC 2,300+ words / 70+ dimensions; DICTION 10,000 words / 5 master variables / 35 subfeatures / 20,000 norm samples; TAS/C 2,000+ / 3,900) [Methods, pp. 549-555 and Summary, pp. 554-555]

## Limitations

- Word counts cannot capture context, irony, sarcasm or multiple word meanings, and many communication constructs (ingroup-outgroup status, formality, requests) are not easily detected (pp. 572-573).
- No compelling psychological theory of word usage yet exists (p. 572); Zeldow & McAdams (1993) questioned whether lower-level word counts have psychological meaning.
- Gender findings rest on a few studies with small samples and one text source; several key claims come from exclusively nonpersonal writings (pp. 557-558).
- Gender effects may reflect social engagement or power rather than sex, and language use may show bidirectional effects, e.g., accommodation to partners (pp. 558, 555-558).
- Personality self-report correlations are small (r about 0.10-0.16) and are not clearly superior to judge ratings (pp. 559-560).
- Clinical studies are often small, use inconsistent inclusion criteria, and lack proper control groups (pp. 560-561).
- Health-linked evidence is sparse, often unpublished or submitted, and effect sizes are not reported for several key studies (pp. 561-562).
- Unclear whether a sample predicting one outcome (e.g., positive words in an essay) generalizes to psychological or physical phenomena more broadly (p. 562).
- Content-based dictionaries have performed poorly because topics are almost infinite and categories are culture-bound (p. 571).
- Review omits cross-language comparison, written versus spoken differences in depth, second-language learning, intelligence and stereotype communication (p. 573).
- Several cited studies are 'submitted', 'in press' or unpublished, so their results could not be checked against final publications from this text alone.

## Gaps and flags

- Function-word style is a gap. The paper names particles as a research priority and treats the I-to-we shift as a signal of shared upheaval, but the stance and press layers use only content dictionaries (LSD) and NLI. No style-based signal exists, and none has been validated. This is listed as a gap only and is not a recommendation.
- Transient event windows are not handled. In one online chat-room study after Princess Diana's death (Stone and Pennebaker 2002), first-person plural rose 135% and 'I' fell 12% for about a week, returning to baseline by day 10. The trait readers pool text across time with no exclusion window around shocks, so crisis-period speech could move member-level N or E scores.
- There is no linguistic-synchrony measure. The paper shows turn-by-turn function-word matching in dyads (Niederhoffer & Pennebaker 2002). The co-appearance layer records only that two members appeared together, not whether their speech converged.
- Genre authorship is not modelled. The paper's individual-difference claims rest on self-authored spontaneous text, and the system has no per-genre authorship weight beyond C_INT_W and RITUAL stripping. The review does not discuss ghost-writing.
- Brain note concepts/single-look-holdout-discipline-cases-2-3-n-slot-and-gold-template.md describes affective_n as z(Pennebaker LIWC base) + z(LLM facets), and it gives the emitter path as fec/emit_affective_n.py, which does not exist. The live code at fec/members/emit_affective_n.py:39-43,56 emits the E-residualized, party-controlled LLM score only, with LIWC as an anchor. The note is stale on both points and the component description repeats the error.
- The component assumption 'Negative-emotion and first-person-singular words track N (Pennebaker)' overstates the source. The paper reports r = 0.10 to 0.16 against student self-reports, and its depression evidence is specific to 'I', while the system's FPS regex pools me/my/mine/myself.
- big5_traits.py:177-178 labels W as 'Pennebaker–King / Yarkoni / Mairesse correlates', but those weights are dormant because mairesse_smoreg.json overrides them. Yarkoni and Mairesse are not in the review, so it cannot be credited for those weights.
