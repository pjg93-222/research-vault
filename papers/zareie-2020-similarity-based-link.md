# Similarity-based link prediction in social networks using latent relationships between the users

**Ahmad Zareie, Rizos Sakellariou (2020).** *Scientific Reports 10:20137 (received 2 Aug 2020, accepted 30 Oct 2020).* [DOI: 10.1038/s41598-020-76799-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-76799-4). Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Zareie and Sakellariou propose DICN, a link-prediction score that multiplies a direct term (1 + common neighbours) by an indirect term (1 + the Pearson correlation of two nodes' neighbourhood vectors, which include second-order neighbours). They motivate it with two observations. First, between 3.1% (UAL) and 53.7% (YST) of existing edges join nodes that share no common neighbour. Second, at the same common-neighbour count, linked and unlinked pairs overlap heavily. They test it on nine small networks treated as static (directed and weighted graphs are left to future work), holding out 20% of edges at random over 15 repeats and scoring with AUC only. DICN has the highest AUC in 8 of 9 networks. The margins over the best baseline range from about 0.007 (INF) to about 0.055 (NSC), and DICN loses on UAL. It also wins in 5 of 9 networks on pairs with no common neighbour, and in 5 of 9 on pairs with equal common-neighbour counts, where it is clearly worst on UAL (0.568 vs 0.792). No variances or significance tests are reported. Weighted and directed graphs are left to future work.
> 
> For Melange, the paper is a candidate source of dyadic features, not evidence about influence. The sharpest use is in the ridge-logistic cosponsor forecaster. Its only dyadic feature drawn from the cosponsor graph is the direct prior sponsor-to-member affinity count (other dyadic terms are attributes such as party, state, chamber and NOMINATE distance, plus an optional committee co-service term). Degree and eigenvector come from an undirected weighted projection, so common-neighbour and second-order similarity between the sponsor and the candidate are missing. The press HCoI layer already uses the correlation-of-profiles idea through Malaterre, but it uses correlation alone, without the direct term. Any DICN-style score in this system is a heuristic similarity output, not closeness, access or introduction probability (D-025).

## Study

**Question.** Can similarity scores for link prediction that go beyond common first-order neighbours (through a latent relationship captured by the correlation of second-order neighbourhood profiles) predict missing or future links more accurately than eight established similarity baselines?

**Theory.** No named social theory. The premise is that structural similarity between two nodes predicts link formation (pp.1-2, citing Ke-ke et al. / Shang et al., ref 18). Common neighbours are treated as an incomplete measure, and a latent relationship is defined as indirect connectivity through second-order neighbours (p.2). Hypothesis 1: if two nodes with a future link have no common neighbour but a significant latent relationship, the link can be predicted. Hypothesis 2: latent relationships separate existing from non-existing links even when pairs share the same number of common neighbours (p.4).

**Data.** Nine static real-world networks, each treated as one snapshot with no time dimension (Table 1, p.8): KRT Zachary karate club (34 nodes, 78 edges, social); DLN dolphins (62, 159, animal); UAL US airline traffic (332, 2126, airport traffic); NSC NetScience co-authorship (379, 914); INF face-to-face contacts at an exhibition (410, 2765); EML Rovira i Virgili email (1133, 5451); YST yeast biological network (2284, 6646); HAM Hamsterster social network (2426, 16630); KHN co-authorship (3772, 12718). Unit of analysis: node pairs (existing edges and non-existing pairs). No collection period is stated. Each network's sample is the full network as published, with no sampling step described.

**Method.** Similarity-based link prediction. For each node i, a neighbourhood vector N_i (Eq. 10, p.6) has entries d_i at the node itself, CN_iz for second-order neighbours, CN_iz + 1 for first-order neighbours, and 0 otherwise. The union set UN_ij (Eq. 11) is the set of indices where either vector is positive. The indirect similarity Corr_ij is the Pearson correlation of N_i and N_j over UN_ij (Eqs. 12-13). The DICN score is DICN_ij = (1 + CN_ij)(1 + Corr_ij) (Eq. 14, p.7). Algorithm 1 (p.6) gives the pseudo-code. Evaluation: edges are randomly split into a training set (beta = 80% by default, 50-90% swept in Exp. 2) and a test set; the process is repeated 15 times and averaged. AUC (Eq. 15, pp.7-8) is computed by comparing every test edge with every non-existing pair, counting ties as 0.5. Eight baselines were implemented in Java: CN, PA, JC, HPI, CNDP, NCC, CCPA and SHOPI.

**Identification.** None in the causal sense. The design is out-of-sample link prediction using random edge holdout. Hypotheses 1 and 2 are tested by restricting test and non-existing pairs to subsets defined by common-neighbour status (Exp. 3 and Exp. 4, pp.9-10).

**Scope.** Population: nine public real-world networks of mixed type (social, animal, airline, co-authorship, contact, biological, email), all treated as static. The paper does not claim a broader population. Era: not stated; published 2020. The paper names directed and weighted networks as future work (p.10), which implies none were analysed.

## Limitations

- Stated by the authors: when the training set is small, the latent relationship is harder to detect because the correlation is lower, so DICN may be less accurate (p.9).
- Stated by the authors: in UAL, and in Exp. 4 more generally, NCC, CNDP and SHOPI significantly outperform DICN (p.10).
- Stated by the authors: the latent relationship is restricted to second-order neighbours; extending it to path counts or node centrality is future work (p.10).
- Stated by the authors: directed and weighted networks, and more varied networks, are future work (p.10).
- Reviewer observation (not stated by the authors): AUC is the only metric. No standard deviation or significance test across the 15 random splits is reported, only averages.
- Reviewer observation: the Experiment 3 text calls the four non-DICN-best networks 'very close' to the best, but the INF gap is 0.8404 vs 0.7979 (SHOPI vs DICN).
- Reviewer observation: the self-entry d_i is part of the vector and therefore enters the Pearson correlation (Eq. 10). The effect of degree on Corr_ij is not separately examined.
- Reviewer observation: the paper gives 8 MB memory for the test machine (p.7). This is probably a typo for GB, but the text reads 8 MB as printed.

## Gaps and flags

- The cosponsor forecaster has no sponsor-candidate structural-similarity feature (common neighbours, Jaccard, Adamic-Adar, or second-order correlation). Its only dyadic cosponsor-graph signal is direct prior affinity (other dyadic terms are attributes), so pairs with zero prior history get only node-level and ideology terms.
- No link-prediction benchmark exists in the system. The engine kernel, recruitment paths and cascades treat a missing cosponsor tie as p = 0, with no estimate of how many ties are merely unobserved. The paper finds that up to 53.7% of edges lack common neighbours in some networks.
- There is no evaluation of any system tie-inference layer (HCoI correlation edges, co-appearance) as a predictor of later cosponsor ties by AUC under a temporal holdout. The paper's evaluation protocol (AUC with ties counted as 0.5, Eq. 15) is a ready template, adapted to temporal rather than random splits.
- D-025: any DICN or common-neighbour score added to the system is a heuristic similarity output. It must never be described as closeness, access or introduction probability.
- Rejected SNA proposals: the paper argues for neither dropping regular cosponsorship edges nor per-edge volume normalization. However, DICN is defined only on unweighted graphs, so a naive port would binarize the cosponsor graph and erase the 1.0/0.5 original/regular distinction. Do not let a DICN port quietly re-introduce the rejected edge changes; compute it on the existing graph or a documented weighted extension.
- Component list mismatch: Zareie & Sakellariou 2020 is listed as a citation for melange-sna.co-appearance-and-references, which concerns negative or signed ties. The paper is unsigned and gives no basis for negative-tie handling.
- Paul filed the paper under 'Path-Finding Algorithums'. It is a link-prediction (similarity) paper with no path-finding content.
- Evidence quality: the only metric is AUC, averaged over 15 random splits, with no variance or significance tests and static benchmark graphs.

Paul left no takeaway for this paper. Both bibliography entries are citation-only, and his PDF markup is 38 plain highlights with no notes. The highlights focus on the claim that common neighbours alone are insufficient, the neighbourhood-vector and correlation construction, and the no-common-neighbour experiment. The paper supports that emphasis, but the gains are modest (about 0.01-0.05 AUC) and come from small, static benchmarks. Filing it under path-finding understates its real use: it supplies dyadic similarity features for link and cosponsor prediction. The component list also cites it for negative ties in the co-appearance and reference layers, which it does not address. Nothing in his notes is stale against the built system, because the system never adopted this method. The closest built analogue is HCoI's profile correlation, which came from Malaterre, not from this paper.
