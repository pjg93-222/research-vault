# The Kernel of Truth in Text-Based Personality Assessment: A Meta-Analysis of the Relations Between the Big Five and the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC)

**Antonis Koutsoumpis, Janneke K. Oostrom, Djurre Holtrop, Ward van Breda, Sina Ghassemi, Reinout E. de Vries (2022).** *Psychological Bulletin, 148(11-12), 843-868. Correction to metadata hint: the hint said 2024; the PDF gives 2022 (received April 8, 2022; accepted November 2, 2022)..* [10.1037/bul0000381](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000381). Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Koutsoumpis et al. (Psychological Bulletin, 2022; the vault slug says 2024, the PDF says 2022) pooled 31 samples (N = 85,724). They correlated Big Five scores with the 52 LIWC categories shared by LIWC2001, 2007 and 2015.
> 
> **Self-reports (cue validity), 26 samples.** Effects are small. The strongest correlations run |rho| .08-.14, and all 52 categories together explain about 5.1% of trait variance (k = 25 samples, n = 83,243-84,084, about 87% of it from Schwartz et al. 2013).
> 
> **Observer ratings (cue utilization), only 5 samples (n = 1,637).** Effects are larger: |rho| .18-.39, with R2 averaging 38.5%.
> 
> **Overlap.** Only 20 of 260 trait-by-category markers agree in direction for both self and observer reports. The paper calls this the "kernel of truth". Examples: swear words, anger and negative emotion for low Conscientiousness and low Agreeableness,.
> 
> **Other results.** Heterogeneity is nearly universal: 96% of self-report effects and 82% of observer effects. Formal settings weaken some markers, for example Agreeableness with swear words is -.07 formal vs -.14 informal. Spoken text carries much more Extraversion signal through word count than written text (.23 vs .03). LIWC's post hoc best-category average (.10, Table 7) sits below Moreno et al. (.27), IBM-WPI (.13) and HTTP (.31), though the paper reports LIWC beating IBM-WPI for Conscientiousness and Extraversion.
> 
> **Why it matters for Melange.** The system's lexical Big Five (`big5_traits.py`) is this closed-vocabulary method applied to formal congressional speech. It uses short published-exemplar word lists, not licensed LIWC dictionaries. The paper sets a low ceiling on how much true trait signal such rates can carry. That supports two existing choices: changing the receptivity layer's default away from the lexical basis (RECEPT_TRAITS=lexical still reverts to it) and keeping status labels on any external claim. It says nothing about the square-root token shrinkage form.
> 
> The paper also gives a bivariate direction check for the active SMOreg weights, and some differ from it, though the paper does not test SMOreg. Finally, observer-panel passes need care. The paper says plainly that valid cues are not the same as true personality, so convergence with the Senate expert panels may partly reflect raters using the same cues the text model uses (utilization), not validity.

## Study

**Question:** Do self-reported and observer-reported Big Five traits correlate with the 52 LIWC categories shared across LIWC2001, LIWC2007 and LIWC2015 (cue validity and cue utilization)? Which categories form a "kernel of truth" that both self- and observer-reports link to? Do 10 study, sample and task moderators explain heterogeneity in these effects? (Introduction, pp. 843-847.)

**Theory:** Brunswik's (1955) lens model (cue validity vs cue utilization); Realistic Accuracy Model (Funder, 1995, 2012) for the kernel-of-truth idea; Self-Other Knowledge Asymmetry model (Vazire, 2010), which predicts observer reports will show larger effects for overt behavior such as verbal behavior; Trait Activation Theory (Tett and Guterman, 2000; Tett et al., 2021), used for predictions on language formality and task synchronicity; "words as attention" account of word use (Tausczik and Pennebaker, 2010).

**Data:** 31 eligible samples from 26 studies (k = 31 samples, 26 studies), total N = 85,724 participants. Unit of analysis: sample/study-level correlations between Big Five trait scores and LIWC category scores. Cue validity: 26 self-report samples (Table 3 note reports k = 25, n = 83,243-84,084 for the analysed effects; Schwartz et al., 2013 alone has n = 72,809). Cue utilization: 5 observer-report samples (Table 3 note: n = 1,637). Mean age 27.96 years (SD 10.50) for cue validity samples; 23.66 (SD 5.52) for cue utilization. Text length >40 words required. Period: literature searched September 8-10, 2020, from 1999 onward (LIWC publication year). Eligible: healthy English-speaking adults; LIWC2022 excluded because it was published after the search. Screening: 691 database records plus 22 additional records; 429 abstracts screened; 124 full texts; 26 studies included (Figure 2, p. 849). Missing data: 12% of values imputed (see method).

**Method:** PRISMA-reported systematic review followed by meta-analysis (random-effects model, R package "meta"). Effect size: Pearson r, Fisher z transformation and back-transformation; Spearman rho converted to r for one study. Corrected effects (rho) disattenuated for personality-measure reliability only (Cronbach alpha for self-reports; ICC for observer reports); LIWC categories not corrected. Missing effect sizes (12% of values) imputed with a self-devised "mean minus 1" rule (sum of available effects plus zeros, divided by k-1); analyses based on this rule (pp. 847-849). Heterogeneity: Cochran Q, I2, tau. Publication bias: Egger's test on uncorrected, nonimputed data. Moderators (five categorical: LIWC version, written-spoken, formality, synchronicity, sample composition; five continuous: sample size, text length, age, percentage women, publication year) tested with meta-regression. Explained variance per trait: meta-analytic linear regression of each trait on all LIWC categories in lavaan, using a 52-category weighted average correlation matrix from 14 studies (n = 6,405) and the harmonic mean n. Content vs function words analysed separately, using narrow categories only to avoid double counting. Two coders per study (first author coded all; four research assistants coded a quarter each).

**Identification:** None in the econometric sense. This is a correlational meta-analysis. Cue validity is the correlation between self-reports and LIWC categories; cue utilization is the correlation between observer reports and LIWC categories; the "kernel of truth" is the set of categories significant in the same direction for both (Figure 1). The authors explicitly state that "valid cues" does not mean "true" personality.

**Scope:** Population: healthy adults writing or speaking English (mostly students and general-population samples, US-centric social media). Era: studies from 1999 to 2020, LIWC2001, LIWC2007 and LIWC2015 only. Instrument scope: the 52 LIWC categories shared by these versions; Big Five (plus HEXACO Emotionality/Agreeableness treated as Big Five, one study). HEXACO Honesty-Humility not reported (single study). Text types: blogs, Facebook/Twitter posts, essays, dreams, interviews, daily speech (EAR), chats. Results are English-specific (p. 861).

## Key findings

- Self-reported traits correlate significantly with LIWC categories, but effects are small. Strongest |rho| ranged .08-.14; the 52 categories explain on average 5.1% of self-reported personality variance. (|rho| = .08-.14; mean R2 = 5.07% (cue validity, narrow categories, Table 4); Abstract, p. 843; Discussion, p. 854)
- Observer-reported traits correlate with LIWC categories with small-to-medium effects, explaining much more variance than self-reports. (|rho| = .18-.39; mean R2 = 38.52% (cue utilization); Emotional Stability lowest at 31.48%, Conscientiousness highest at 47.32%; Abstract, p. 843; Results, Cue Utilization, pp. 852-853; Table 4, p. 854)
- Cue validity effects for individual categories (Table 3). Examples: Openness with words longer than six letters rho = .11; Conscientiousness with negative emotions rho = -.12, swear words -.11, anger -.10; Agreeableness with anger -.14, swear -.10, negative emotions -.10, positive emotions .10; Emotional Stability with negative emotions -.12. No Extraversion category reached |rho| >= .10. (As listed; k = 25 samples, n = 83,243-84,084 (Table 3 note); Results, Cue Validity, p. 852; Table 3, pp. 852-853)
- Cue utilization effects for individual categories (Table 3). Examples: Agreeableness with swear words rho = -.39; Conscientiousness with swear words -.35, anger -.32, negative emotions -.30, sexual -.29; Extraversion with word count .31; Openness with word count .18 and leisure .17; Emotional Stability with first-person singular pronouns -.29. (k = 5 samples, n = 1,637 (Table 3 note); Results, Cue Utilization, pp. 852-853; Table 3)
- Kernel of truth: 20 of 260 linguistic markers (5 traits x 52 categories) were significant in the same direction for both self- and observer-reports (3 Openness, 4 Conscientiousness, 3 Extraversion, 4 Agreeableness, 6 Emotional Stability). Eight reached |rho| >= .10; four also had lower 95% CI >= .05 (Conscientiousness: swear, anger, negative emotions; Agreeableness: swear). Across the 20 markers, cue validity and utilization correlate r = .94; across all 260, r = .52. Four markers pointed in opposite directions. (20/260; r = .94 (20 markers); r = .52 (all 260); 8 at |rho| >= .10; 4 with lower CI >= .05; Results, Kernel of Truth, p. 853; Figure 3, p. 853; Discussion, pp. 856-857)
- Cue utilization was larger than cue validity in both effect sizes and explained variance, consistent with the Self-Other Knowledge Asymmetry model. (R2 mean 38.52% (utilization) vs 5.07% (validity); Discussion, Self-Other Knowledge Asymmetry Model, p. 857)
- Function words explained little variance but were valid and utilized markers for some traits; content words explained more. (Cue validity: function 1.31%, content 3.71%. Cue utilization: function 5.05%, content 28.68%.; Results, Explained Variance, p. 853; Discussion, Other Findings: Function Words, p. 858; Table 4, p. 854)
- Heterogeneity was pervasive and moderators explained part of it. Significant heterogeneity in 96% of cue-validity and 82% of cue-utilization effects; the 10 moderators explained some heterogeneity in 70% and 61% of effects. (96% (validity), 82% (utilization); 70% and 61% explained; Discussion, Moderators and TAT, p. 857)
- Language formality: low-Agreeableness individuals used more swear words in informal settings (rho = -.14) than formal (rho = -.07), a significant difference (QM = 4.64, p = .03), as TAT predicted. Synchronicity did not moderate Extraversion and positive emotion (synchronous rho = .07 vs asynchronous .01; QM = 1.03, p = .31), so the Chen et al. (2020) finding was not replicated. (rho -.14 informal vs -.07 formal (QM = 4.64, p = .03); QM = 1.03, p = .31; Results, Categorical Moderators, p. 854; Discussion, Regarding TAT, p. 858)
- Text mode: Extraversion with word count was much stronger for spoken than written language. (Spoken rho = .23 vs written .03 (word count, Extraversion); Results, Categorical Moderators, p. 854; Table 5, p. 855)
- LIWC version: older versions produced larger average effects. (average |rho| = .042 (LIWC2001), .039 (LIWC2007), .030 (LIWC2015); Results, Categorical Moderators, p. 854; Discussion, p. 857)
- Continuous moderators: effects were typically stronger for longer texts and weaker for larger samples. Negative-valence associations for Conscientiousness and Agreeableness (swear, anger, negative emotions) and for Emotional Stability (negative emotions) weakened with publication year. (Year-of-publication slopes significant for Agreeableness swear and anger and Emotional Stability negative emotions (cue validity, Table 6); sample size b < .001 for many cue-utilization effects; Results, Continuous Moderators, p. 854; Table 6, p. 856; Discussion, p. 858)
- Publication bias: Egger tests flagged 99 of 260 cue-validity and 11 of 260 cue-utilization effects; excluding Schwartz et al. (2013) left 6. The authors argue small-study effects do not indicate publication bias because effects did not shrink with sample size and were smaller without Schwartz. (99 (validity), 11 (utilization), 6 after excluding Schwartz et al.; Results, Publication Bias, p. 854)
- Benchmark against other methods. The strongest LIWC category per trait beat human judges for most traits but lagged computer methods (Moreno et al., 2021; IBM-WPI; HTTP). Average LIWC correlation in the table was .10 (self) and .25 (observer), versus .12 for human judges (Tskhay and Rule, 2014) and .27-.31 for other computer methods. (Average r: LIWC .10 (self) / .25 (observer); human judges .12; Moreno et al. (2021) .27; IBM-WPI .13; HTTP .31 (uncorrected); Discussion, LIWC Versus Other Methods, pp. 858-860; Table 7, p. 859; Future Research, p. 861)

## Limitations

- Corrected effects are probably underestimates: LIWC categories were not disattenuated for their reliability (authors argue they are formative measures; Limitations, p. 860).
- Cue utilization rests on only k = 5 samples (vs 26 for cue validity), so categorical moderators could not be tested for it and the kernel of truth is probably underestimated (p. 861).
- The cue-validity pool is dominated by Schwartz et al. (2013), n = 72,809 (the authors ran analyses with and without it).
- 12% of effect-size and moderator values were missing; missingness was non-random (non-significant effects were often unreported). The authors used a self-devised 'mean minus 1' imputation, which they admit may distort true effects (pp. 847-849).
- Observer reports were mostly zero-acquaintance, and only one study used acquaintances; the acquaintance effect could not be tested (p. 861).
- Several studies were small, and the authors note only large effects reach significance in small samples (p. 862).
- Test-retest reliability was not used, because repeating verbal tasks can change responses (p. 860).
- Results are English-specific, and LIWC has not been designed for personality assessment; Table 7 comparisons use each method's best category and are post hoc (footnote 9, p. 859).
- Language formality and synchronicity are broad classifications, which may have attenuated situational moderation (p. 858).
- Honesty-Humility is not covered (single study, Holtrop et al., 2019).
- Egger's test flagged many cue-validity effects; the authors argue this reflects small-study effects rather than publication bias, which is a judgment call (p. 854).

## Gaps and flags

- The lexical reader has no licensed LIWC dictionary: categories are short exemplar lists (swear = 3 words) plus the Empath overlay. The paper's effect sizes are for LIWC dictionaries, and the system has no check that its categories are equivalent.
- There is no formality or genre moderator in the lexical model. The paper shows formality and spoken vs written mode change effect sizes (Agreeableness-swear -.14 vs -.07; Extraversion-word count spoken .23 vs written .03). The system pools floor, interview and social text and has already seen genre transfer fail for N.
- No sign audit of the fitted SMOreg weights against literature kernel directions. ES on first-person singular and E on positive emotion differ from the paper's bivariate signs (the ES first-person comparison sets an observer-based effect against a model fit to other data), and ES on negative emotion is near zero.
- No open-vocabulary or embedding benchmark: the paper's Table 7 puts Moreno et al. (.27) and HTTP (.31) above LIWC's post hoc best-category .10 for self-reports. The system's alternative LLM reader is display-only and failed its behavioral diagonal (+0.064).
- No cue-validity vs cue-utilization distinction in the validation framing: observer-panel convergence (E, A) is not separated from shared-stereotype cue use.
- Flag: Metadata: the vault slug and hint say 2024. The paper is Psychological Bulletin 2022, 148(11-12), 843-868, doi 10.1037/bul0000381.
- Flag: Documentation drift: the component brief says affective_n = z(LIWC) + z(LLM). The live code, emit_affective_n.py:39-43, says LIWC is only the convergent-validity anchor since round-7b, and the measure is the E-residualized, party-controlled LLM affective-N.
- Flag: The active SMOreg weights (fec/liwc/mairesse_smoreg.json:20, :43, :51) give E a negative weight on positive emotion, ES a positive weight on first-person singular, and ES a near-zero weight on negative emotion, against the paper's bivariate directions. This is a multivariate-suppression caveat, not proof of error.
- Flag: Do not read observer-panel passes as trait accuracy. The paper says valid or utilized cues do not mean 'true' personality, and its observer evidence rests on k = 5 mostly zero-acquaintance samples.
- Flag: No brain wording rule is triggered: the paper does not argue for dropping regular cosponsorship edges or for per-edge volume normalization, and makes no LDA, reciprocity or influence claims.

Paul calls Ramey's closed-vocabulary approach "dated but foundational" and asks for frameworks specific to each venue. Birkenmaier's highlights call Ramey's SVM outdated and unvalidated. This meta-analysis backs both points with numbers. Closed-vocabulary categories explain only about 5% of self-reported trait variance, and venue matters: formality, spoken vs written, and heterogeneity in 96% of effects. So the evidence agrees with his skepticism and with his call for venue-specific frameworks.

His notes are stale relative to the system. They do not record two things:
- The pre-registered nomological test already indicted the lexical Big5 for behavioral prediction, and the receptivity layer now defaults to a behavioral basis (receptivity_layer.py:59-64).
- N now comes from an E-residualized LLM reader, with LIWC only as an anchor.

The paper does not support his endorsement of LLM trait reading directly, because it does not test LLMs. It only shows that non-LIWC computer methods reach r of about .27-.31. The system's own LLM reader has not cleared its behavioral bar.
