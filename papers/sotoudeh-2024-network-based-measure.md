# A Network-Based Measure of Cosponsorship Influence on Bill Passage in the United States House of Representatives

**Sarah Sotoudeh, Mason A. Porter, Sanjukta Krishnagopal (2024 (arXiv v1, June 2024; this file is v2, revised 23 Nov 2025)).** *arXiv preprint arXiv:2406.19554v2 [cs.SI]. Metadata hint confirmed: title, authors, arXiv ID all match the PDF header..* [DOI: arXiv:2406.19554](https://doi.org/arXiv:2406.19554). Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Sotoudeh, Porter and Krishnagopal (arXiv 2406.19554v2) build a pairwise influence score from House cosponsorship covering the 111th to 115th Congresses (2009 to 2019). Each pair's share of jointly cosponsored bills that passed the House is decayed exponentially (half-lives of 6, 12 or 24 months), summed over co-party members and normalized by party size. A bill's influence is then the mean or the max of its cosponsors' scores at introduction. In every non-overlapping 4-month window, passed bills score higher than failed ones. The max-cosponsor score separates them better than the mean (relative difference 0.248 vs 0.139 at a 6-month half-life). Half-life barely matters (0.248 / 0.223 / 0.203). Eigenvector, closeness and strength centralities computed on the same pass/total matrix barely separate passed from failed bills (0.002 to 0.004). The analysis is descriptive only: it reports standard errors (0.0139, 0.0119, 0.0107) but no formal tests, controls or strata. A bill counts toward its own score, and as the formulas are written, edges can include passage that happens after the scoring month.
> 
> For Paul's system there are three points. First, the paper is cited as the source of the CU cosponsor network's edge weights, but the built weights depart from it on nearly every parameter: a ×3 success multiplier applied to each passed-bill tie instead of a pass/total ratio, a per-Congress 0.8 decay (half-life of about 6 years), a 1/sqrt(C) size discount, unequal role weights and no party normalization. Second, the centrality result is weak evidence for direction-blind centrality as a passage indicator. It also shows that any check of InfluenceScore against bill passage would be circular, because the edges are already weighted by passage. Third, code comments and brain notes cite the paper for claims it never tests, namely recruitment chains and influence versus cosponsor count.

## Study

**Question.** Does a legislator-influence measure built from House bill cosponsorship, where two members accrue influence when a bill they both cosponsor passes and that influence decays over time, help explain which House bills pass? Does it beat conventional centralities (eigenvector, closeness, strength) as a signal of bill passage? (Sec. 1, pp. 1-2; Sec. 4-5, pp. 5-18)

**Theory.** Influence accrues between legislators through repeated mutual cosponsorship of bills that pass (quid pro quo / logrolling, social capital, homophily, alliance formation; pp. 3-4). Influence capital accumulates with tenure and decays when a member is inactive (p. 7). Bill-level influence is meant to capture the 'influentials' idea that a few very influential people drive opinion and decisions (p. 13, citing Weimann [49] and Watts and Dodds [51]).

**Data.** US House of Representatives bills, 111th-115th Congresses (Jan 2009 to Jan 2019), from ProPublica bulk bill data (ref [39]). Member IDs completed from Congress Legislators, Stewart and Woon, and McCrain data (refs [40-42]). Only bills are kept; simple, concurrent and joint resolutions are excluded (p. 4). Passage means success through introduction, committee and debate stages in the House, irrespective of Senate or enactment (p. 5). Units: bill (bill-level influence scores), legislator-month (influence I[t,i]), and legislator-pair-month (C tensors). N is not reported as a total bill count or member count anywhere in the text; only percentages appear. Table 1 (p. 7): share of House-originated bills passing in the House, Congresses 111-115: 10.98%, 7.3%, 9.8%, 11.84%, 13.49%. Share enacted into law: 3.87%, 2.96%, 3.55%, 3.31%, 3.84%. Mean cosponsors per bill: 21.50, 21.44, 21.96, 20.93, 19.01 (max 419, 425, 379, 332, 381). Mean bills per cosponsor: 235.72, 205.63, 227.45, 243.53, 250.87 (max 797, 632, 666, 695, 791). Text also states at least 87.85% of House bills had cosponsors in each term (p. 4). Median House passage time 98 days, mean 148 days (p. 11). Roughly 3% of all bills in the ProPublica data were enacted (p. 5). Bills are about 70% of proposed legislation (p. 5).

**Method.** 1) Build monthly undirected weighted cosponsorship tensors: A[t,i,j]pass counts bills introduced in month t that include i and j as sponsor/cosponsors and eventually pass the House; A[t,i,j]tot counts all such bills (p. 6). Edges are symmetric; sponsorship and cosponsorship are treated equally (pp. 5-6). 2) Exponential decay with half-life 6, 12 or 24 months (k = ln(0.5)/half-life), cumulated monthly: C[t,i,j] = sum over n<=t of e^{k(t-n)} A[n,i,j] (Eq. 1-2, pp. 7-8). 3) Party-normalized influence: P[t,i]Dems and P[t,i]Reps are the decayed pass/total ratio summed over co-party members divided by party size (Eq. 3, p. 8). I[t,i] = P_Dems + P_Reps (Eq. 4, p. 8). 4) Bill influence at introduction month t_k: mean or max of I[t_k,i] over cosponsors C_k (Eq. 5, p. 10). 5) Compare passed vs failed bills in non-overlapping 4-month windows via relative difference = (mean passed minus mean failed) / mean failed (pp. 11, 13). Comparators: eigenvector centrality of D = C_pass / C_tot on the largest connected component, eigenproblem D x = lambda x (Eq. 6-7, pp. 15-16); closeness and strength centrality on the same LCC, Appendix A (Eq. 8-9, p. 18). Analysis is descriptive: plots, means and standard errors. No regression, no controls, no formal significance test is reported.

**Identification.** None in the causal sense. The paper makes an associational claim: bill-level influence separates passed from failed bills. No control for bill topic, committee, sponsor or party beyond the party-normalized score construction (the authors list topic and committee as future controls, p. 17). Two features of the construction bear on how the separation should be read. (a) Authors' stated choice (p. 10, lines 417-422): a bill is included in its own influence calculation, so the outcome of the bill being scored can enter its own predictor; authors argue the effect is at most one bill in a large numerator. (b) As written in Sec. 4 (p. 6, 'eventually pass (including possibly in month t)'), A[n] counts a bill as passing whenever it eventually passes, so C[t] can include passage events that happen after month t. The authors do not discuss whether this is look-ahead. This is a reader's reading of the definitions, not a claim the paper makes.

**Scope.** Population: House-originated bills in the 111th-115th Congresses only. Institution: US House of Representatives; Senate, resolutions and enactment are excluded by design (pp. 4-5). Era: January 2009 to January 2019, which the authors tie to rising polarization after 2015 (p. 9, p. 13). Findings are aggregate; the authors explicitly decline to make claims about individual legislators (p. 17).

## Key findings

- Passed bills have larger bill-level influence than failed bills in every 4-month period from 2009 to 2019, for both mean and max aggregation. (Consistent separation in all 4-month windows; no per-window counts given.) — Sec. 5.1, p. 13 (Fig. 3, p. 12)
- Max-cosponsor influence separates passed and failed bills more than mean-cosponsor influence. (Mean relative difference at 6-month half-life: 0.248 (max) vs 0.139 (mean).) — Sec. 5.1, p. 13
- The max-vs-mean separation holds at longer half-lives. (12-month half-life: 0.223 (max) vs 0.122 (mean). 24-month half-life: 0.203 (max) vs 0.109 (mean).) — Sec. 5.1, p. 13
- Half-life choice between 6 and 24 months barely changes discrimination; shorter decay gives slightly larger separation. (Max-influence relative differences 0.248 / 0.223 / 0.203 for 6 / 12 / 24 months; standard errors 0.0139 / 0.0119 / 0.0107.) — Sec. 5.2, p. 14 (Fig. 4, p. 14)
- Eigenvector centrality is a much weaker indicator of passage than the influence measure. (Mean relative difference 0.004 (mean eigenvector) and 0.002 (max eigenvector), vs 0.139 and 0.248 for influence.) — Sec. 5.3, p. 17 (Fig. 5, p. 16)
- Closeness and strength centrality are also weak indicators, and the authors attribute the failure to dense networks (untested). (Closeness: 0.004 mean, 0.002 max. Strength: 0.004 mean, 0.003 max.) — Appendix A, pp. 18-19 (Figs. 6-7, pp. 20-21)
- Legislator influence scores are small and their distribution mean does not rise over time; the authors hypothesize less collaboration in Congresses 114-115. (Legislator scores range roughly 0 to 0.008. Distribution mean is smallest in Congresses 114 and 115.) — Sec. 4.2, p. 9 (Fig. 2, p. 9)
- Bill-level influence declines steeply from about 2015; the authors hypothesize this tracks rising polarization (not tested). (No numeric decline reported in text.) — Sec. 5.1, p. 13
- Authors' interpretive conclusion: a bill is more likely to pass when one very influential member cosponsors it than when several moderately influential members do. This rests on the max-vs-mean comparison. (Based on the 0.248 (max) vs 0.139 (mean) relative differences at 6-month half-life.) — Sec. 5.1, p. 13; Sec. 6, p. 17

## Limitations

- Authors state the influence notion is simplistic: all legislators are treated identically despite differing external influences and goals (p. 17, lines 650-654).
- Results are aggregate only; authors decline to comment on legislator-level rankings because they have more confidence in aggregates (p. 17).
- Bill topics, committee assignments and other factors that affect votes are not controlled; authors list this as future work (p. 17).
- Influence is symmetric; asymmetric sponsor-to-cosponsor influence is left to future work (p. 7).
- Aggregation uses 4-month non-overlapping windows; other windows and overlapping windows are not explored (p. 11).
- Only House passage is studied; passage in the originating chamber is the outcome, and enacted-into-law outcomes are not analyzed (pp. 4-5).
- Authors call the cosponsorship-derived influence measure 'only a proxy' for legislator influence (p. 17).
- Comparison with the legislative-effectiveness score (LES) is not performed (p. 18).
- Other network measures (e.g. network efficiency) and polyadic influence are not examined (p. 18).
- Explanation for centralities failing (dense networks) is stated as a suspicion, not tested (p. 19).
- Authors state no competing interests; funding from UC Presidential Postdoctoral Fellowship (Declarations, p. 22). Code is public at github.com/sarahsotoudeh/LegislativeInfluence (p. 1, p. 22).

## Gaps and flags

- The system has no as-of (time-sliced) network. Success flags come from the latest action at scrape time, so any validation of network measures against passage or advancement uses outcomes from the future. The paper shares a version of this flaw: a bill is included in its own score, and edges count eventual passage.
- No outcome-separation check has been run on the system's own success-weighted CU network, comparing mean or max cosponsor strength for advanced and non-advanced CU bills within the same window.
- No bill-level aggregation uses the strongest cosponsor (max) rather than an additive or mean signal. The paper's main contrast is max vs mean (0.248 vs 0.139), and none of the cascade, forecaster or path engines has a bill-level max-cosponsor feature.
- No party-size normalization on edge weights, and no sensitivity test of decay below one Congress. Both are central parameters in the paper.
- Neither the effectiveness shadow nor the network measures have been benchmarked against CEL LES. The paper leaves this open.
- DO NOT RECOMMEND: the paper's eigenvector input D = C_pass / C_tot and its pairwise influence ratio are per-edge volume normalizations. Per-edge volume normalization was measured and REJECTED in this project (association sqrt(Out*In) left Katz ~ volume at +0.552; row-stochastic at +0.477). Adopting the paper's ratio construction would re-propose a rejected design without new evidence.
- D-025 conflict: make_dashboard_html_00.tpl:735-738 cites this paper next to wording that calls chain reliability 'the exact, maximal probability' and 'mathematical certainty'. The paper gives no probability, and D-025 bars presenting graph ranks or paths as access or introduction probabilities.
- Overclaimed citations: the template comment (:738) and brain note actors/melange-dashboard-june-capstone.md:70 attribute 'influence, not count, drives passage' to the paper. The paper never compares against cosponsor count. The cosponsor-network topic note lists the paper as the source of the edge weights, yet the built formula's ×3 multiplier, 1/sqrt(C), role ratios and per-Congress decay are not in the paper.
- The paper's relative differences are pooled across parties and across 2009-2019, with a post-2015 decline the authors hypothesize is correlated with polarization. Any figure quoted from it should carry an UNSTRATIFIED label, along with its window (4-month bins, House 111th-115th) and denominator (mean of failed bills).
- Circularity hazard: testing InfluenceScore or weighted out-degree against bill passage on the current graph is contaminated, because edges on passed sponsored bills are multiplied by 3 (congress_member_scraper.R:1925; build_holistic_base.py:73).
