# Image as Data: Automated Content Analysis for Visual Presentations of Political Actors and Events

**Jungseock Joo, Zachary C. Steinert-Threlkeld (2022).** *Computational Communication Research 4.1 (2022) 11-67 (CCR 2022.1.001).* [10.5117/CCR2022.1.001.JOO](https://doi.org/10.5117/CCR2022.1.001.JOO) Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Joo and Steinert-Threlkeld (CCR 2022) is a methods tutorial on treating images as data, with two applications. Study 1 sends 15,647 Facebook photos from 677 candidates in 2018 through Google's label API. It runs chi-squared tests of label against party separately by gender, because the parties' gender ratios differ. Republican men over-index labels like Official and Military officer. Republican women over-index Blond and Red. There is no correction for testing 1,730 unique labels. Study 2 applies a fine-tuned ResNet-50 to protest images from five protest episodes (its 91.79% best fine-tune accuracy, in Appendix Table D1, comes from a separate protest training set). It defines framing as the share of near-duplicate images per event and frame label, where a duplicate is a normalized feature distance below 0.2, a threshold picked from one histogram. The authors expect protesters to share state violence more than protester violence and crowds more than individuals. The state-violence expectation holds in 3 of 5 events; for crowds the authors report that images of groups are shared at higher rates in three events (their own reading, not a reproduced count). The authors call the results provisional. There is no causal identification.
>
> For Paul's systems, the paper matters in one place. fec/network/frame_alignment.py ports both instruments to text: near-duplicate press titles across members (3-word-shingle Jaccard >= 0.5), and chi-squared category and term association with party. Checking the code showed that the transfer breaks on the unit of analysis. Joo's sharer chooses the image. The text port reads cu_press_stance_tone.csv with no source filter, and 816 of its 2,388 rows are americascreditunions.org articles with 816 distinct URLs but only 349 distinct titles (up to 19 members on one title). One ACU headline credited to many members therefore counts as members echoing one script. In a recompute reproduced on a second path, General CU duplication falls from 60.2% (n=442) to 5.2% (n=116), and CU Tax Status from 64.7% (n=190) to 38.1% (n=42), once only *.senate.gov and *.house.gov rows are kept. ## Study

**Question.** Can large-scale image data be analyzed with deep learning / convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to measure political communication that text methods and small manual codings cannot reach? Two applied questions: (1) how U.S. politicians' Facebook photographs differ by party and gender, measured with an off-the-shelf label API; (2) how protest images shared on Twitter frame events, with testable expectations that protest images emphasize state violence over protester violence and crowds over individuals.

**Theory.** Visual information is processed more easily and emotionally than text (Graber 1996; Grabe and Bucy 2009); the visual cue may dominate when visual and verbal cues conflict (Gibson and Zillmann 2000); images are information shortcuts for complex issues (Popkin 1994). Ideology predicts object emphasis (conservative: nationalism, capitalism; liberal: inequality reduction); gender predicts self-presentation of physical features (Carpinella et al. 2016; Bauer and Carpinella 2018). Protest framing: frames affect recruitment (Benford and Snow 2000); protester violence reduces support while state violence increases it, so protesters should emphasize state violence; crowds lower individual risk and create mobilization feedback, so protesters should emphasize crowds (Moore 1995; Biggs 2016).

**Data.** Study 1: 15,647 public Facebook photographs (timeline, mobile and profile albums, via the Graph API) posted by 677 candidates in the 2018 U.S. general election, Aug 5 to Nov 6, 2018. Unit = photograph (candidate-level sample; candidate list from Wikipedia, accounts identified manually). Each photo was sent to Google's Label Detection API; 1,730 unique labels were returned. Per-party and per-gender Ns are not stated in the main text. Study 2: geolocated tweets and their images from five protest episodes: Catalonia (Spain, secessionist, 2017), Hong Kong (2014, electoral reform), Russia (anti-corruption, 26 Mar 2017), South Korea (2016-17, Park Geun-hye), and Venezuela (2014-15), printed p.31-33 and footnote 5. Tweets were not keyword-filtered. The analysis sample N is not stated in the main text. Protest-image classifier training set: 32,611 images (Appendix D, printed p.48-50 and Fig. D1/D2 captions). Training images were a mixture of Google Image results and geolocated tweet images.

**Method.** Study 1: off-the-shelf Google Vision Label Detection (no training); label prevalence by party computed as the share of images with each label (pD, pR); chi-squared test per label, separate for male and female candidates; labels sorted by chi-square (Tables 1-2, printed p.28-29). Unsupervised check: ImageNet-pretrained CNN features (2,048-dim penultimate layer), k-means with k=200 (Appendix F, printed p.54-55). Study 2: fine-tuned ResNet-50 (He et al. 2016) for protest image classification and for violence/police/fire/sign labels; FairFace classifier (Karkkainen and Joo 2019) for face sex, race and age; six frame labels (contains child, faces, large group, small group, protester violence, state violence); duplicate detection via cosine-style pairwise distance on the 1,000-dim last fully connected layer, duplicate if normalized distance below 0.2 (threshold chosen by inspecting the histogram); framing rate = share of duplicate images within each event-label (Table 3, printed p.33). Background sections describe CNN architecture, transfer learning, training, validation (Grad-CAM visualization, Appendix D) and ethics. Table D1 compares architectures (AlexNet, VGG-16/19, ResNet-18/34/50) for protest fine-tuning.

**Identification.** None causal. Study 1: descriptive chi-squared association between party (or gender-by-party) and label presence, no controls, no multiple-comparison correction reported. Study 2: descriptive comparison of duplication rates across events and labels, no inferential statistics; hypothesis check is by comparing rates within events (state vs protester violence; group vs child/faces).

**Scope.** Population: candidates in the 2018 U.S. general election (Facebook photos) and protest-related Twitter images from five protest episodes in Catalonia, Hong Kong, Russia, South Korea and Venezuela (2014-2017). Institutions: U.S. elections and social-media platforms. Era: 2014 to 2018 data, published 2022. The paper is a methods tutorial with two applications, so the external validity of the substantive findings is explicitly limited.

## Key findings

- Among male candidates, Republicans' photographs over-index official and military/uniform labels; Democrats' over-index adaptation, community, youth and conversation labels. Top Republican-male labels: Official (chi2 53.0, pD 9.69%, pR 14.64%), Military officer (16.6; 1.02% vs 2.09%), Uniform (15.0; 2.50% vs 3.94%). Top Democratic-male labels: Adaptation (60.6; 6.87% vs 3.39%), Community (43.9; 28.81% vs 22.87%), Youth (30.8; 6.59% vs 4.05%). Numbers: chi2 53.0-60.6 for top labels; pD/pR shares given in Table 1 (Section 6.1 and Table 1, printed p.28 (PDF p.18))
- Female candidates show a weaker cross-party gap than male candidates (no military concepts among Republican women), but Republican women's photos carry traditional feminine labels. Top Republican-female labels: Red (chi2 59.8; pD 2.23%, pR 6.30%), Blond (51.1; 3.68% vs 8.21%), Jeans (42.7; 1.30% vs 4.01%), Long hair (8.0; 1.80% vs 3.03%), Pink (6.4; 2.46% vs 3.71%), Beauty (6.0; 1.64% vs 2.66%). Top Democratic-female labels: Glasses (41.6; 9.73% vs 4.51%), Adaptation (34.3; 7.12% vs 3.03%), Youth (27.2; 11.87% vs 7.16%). Numbers: chi2 41.6-59.8 for top labels; the authors say female candidates emphasize facial features more than males (Table 2 (printed p.29, PDF p.19); discussion printed p.30)
- The authors conclude that politicians combine partisan values with gender stereotypes in their self-presentation (Bauer and Carpinella 2018). Numbers:  (Printed p.30, PDF p.20)
- Unsupervised k-means (k=200) on CNN features yields clusters of very similar or identical images, e.g. a cluster of John McCain photographs posted after his death on Aug 25, 2018. Numbers: k=200; 2,048-dim features (Printed p.30 and Appendix F p.54-55 (Fig. F1))
- State-violence expectation partly supported: images containing state violence are shared more than protester violence in three of five events (Catalonia 0.304 vs 0.212; South Korea 0.042 vs 0.023; Venezuela 0.308 vs 0.280). Numbers: 0.308 vs 0.003 (about 103x); Venezuela all labels 0.163-0.308 (Table 3 and text, printed p.34-35 (PDF p.24-25))
- Crowd expectation partly supported: group labels (large or small group) are duplicated more than the child/faces labels within events. Images of groups are shared at higher rates in three events, though not the same three that frame state violence. Protests framed as state violence (Catalonia and Venezuela) also emphasize group nature. Numbers: Venezuela large group 0.249, small group 0.267; Catalonia large group 0.122, small group 0.144 (Printed p.32-34 and Table 3 (PDF p.22-24))

Accuracy per frame label is not reported in the main text. Numbers: ResNet-50 91.79%; AlexNet 89.29%; training n=32,611 (Table D1 and Appendix D, printed p.48-50 (PDF p.38-40); Fig. D1 caption)
- Methods finding: pretrained CNNs and commercial APIs can be used without training data, but their labels inherit dataset bias and face-classification accuracy gaps; the authors cite darker-skinned females at 65% vs lighter-skinned males at 99% accuracy for commercial gender-classification APIs. Numbers: 65% vs 99% accuracy (Buolamwini and Gebru 2018, as cited) (Section 5 Ethics (Bias), printed p.23 (PDF p.13))

## Limitations

- Authors call the Study 2 results provisional and say they are not a definitive answer (printed p.33, PDF p.23).
- Study 2 discards temporal variation and does not consider city or country correlates of frame emphasis (printed p.34, PDF p.24).
- Framing is operationalized only as percentage duplicated images; the authors suggest counts of tweets and more frames as alternatives (printed p.34).
- Study 2 uses a duplicate threshold of 0.2 chosen by inspecting one histogram; no sensitivity analysis is reported (printed p.32).
- Google's Vision API architecture and training data are not published; the authors recommend validating API labels against manual annotations (footnote 2, printed p.57).
- Labels inherit model and data bias; Google's gender label for photographs in image search underrepresents women relative to occupational share, as cited (Lam et al. 2018) (printed p.23-24).
- Pretrained classifiers and APIs have inferior customizability; custom concepts require new annotated training data (printed p.30).
- Interpretability: deep models have millions of parameters; validation relies on accuracy metrics plus visualization (Grad-CAM), which the authors say does not explain how the model reaches its output (printed p.51-53).
- Privacy and consent: face detection can identify protesters, so researchers should not release identifiable photographs and should seek IRB approval for social-media image studies (printed p.24).
- The paper does not report accuracy of the six frame labels in the main text; the verification it cites (Appendix G) is Grad-CAM visualization only (printed p.56).
- Study 1 reports no multiple-comparison correction across the 1,730 unique labels; significance stars are per-label chi-squared tests (Tables 1-2).
- Internal inconsistencies in the text: the conclusion says the protest example 'analyzes six protests' (printed p.36) while the study covers five countries; the text reports 'a tie in Russia' for protester vs state violence (printed p.35), but Table 3 shows 0.121 (protester) vs 0.100 (state).
- Study 1 covers one election cycle (2018) and one platform (Facebook); photo sets are candidate-level and per-party or per-gender N is not stated.

## Gaps and flags

- Frame alignment has no source-author filter. The press pillar's own-domain rule (*.senate.gov / *.house.gov) is not applied in frame_alignment.py, so ACU syndication is counted as member coordination.
- There is no sensitivity sweep on the near-duplicate threshold (Jaccard 0.5) and no human check that flagged title pairs are actually the same message. Joo leaves the same gap with the 0.2 threshold.
- frame_alignment.json was last written 2026-07-01 and frame_alignment.py is not in run_pipeline.py, so the artifact is stale relative to later press refreshes.
- The party-framing chi-square has no strata (chamber, author domain) and no multiple-comparison correction.
- There is no visual channel (member photos, floor video). Joo's pointer to Dietrich (2018), where physical aisle-crossing predicts vote polarization, is the kind of behavioral 'monitoring' measure the system lacks. Paul's 'Monitoring tool?' note sits beside that material. Nothing is built, and the paper gives no validity evidence for congressional targeting.
- Joo recommends validating off-the-shelf model labels against manual annotation before use. The frame-alignment duplicates and categories have never had a gold check.
- DATA ARTIFACT (recompute reproduced on a second path): the frame-alignment headline figures (CU Tax Status 65%, General CU 60%) are driven by 816 americascreditunions.org rows, 34% of the 2,388-row cu_press_stance_tone.csv. Own-domain recompute: General CU 5.2% (n=116), CU Tax Status 38.1% (n=42), Defense 13.9% (n=36).
- The brain note (2026-07-feature-influence-engine-internals-salience-sna-wave.md:38) quotes 65%/60%/12% and chi-square = 128 with no window, denominator or stratum. That breaks the rule that every figure names its window and denominator, and the pooled chi-square needs an UNSTRATIFIED label.
- The component description says frame alignment 'feeds salience intensity (0.5 + alignment)'. In the code this happens only under `--intensity auto`; the chain runs salience_shock --advise at the default 1.0 (run_pipeline.py:186), so production does not use it.
- The salience_shock.py:200 docstring cites 'our CU Tax Status at 65%' as evidence of a coordinated shop. That figure is the contaminated one.
- No conflict with the rejected SNA proposals, D-021 (LDA) or D-025: the paper argues for neither cosponsorship-edge dropping nor per-edge volume normalization, and the system draws no access or closeness language from it.

Paul's position names Joo's duplicate-rate frame alignment as the template, and the system did build exactly that port (fec/network/frame_alignment.py, docstring lines 3-17), so the note matches what is built. It is stale on two points. First, it assumes the port measures member coordination. In fact one-third of its input is ACU-authored syndication, and removing those rows collapses General CU alignment from 60% to 5%. Second, the measure does not reach production: it is not in the chain, and salience intensity uses it only on a non-default flag. Both of Paul's annotation documents list this paper as a citation only, with no takeaway. The 'Monitoring tool?' sticky beside Section 3 (printed p.17), next to the highlighted Dietrich (2018) passage on video aisle-crossing, points toward a visual or behavioral monitoring channel the system does not have. The paper gives no evidence that such a channel would carry over to CU targeting.
