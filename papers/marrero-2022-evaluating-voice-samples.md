# Evaluating voice samples as a potential source of information about personality

**Zachariah N.K. Marrero, Samuel D. Gosling, James W. Pennebaker, Gabriella M. Harari (2022).** *Acta Psychologica 230 (2022) 103740 (open access, CC BY; received 15 Mar 2022, accepted 4 Sep 2022, online 18 Sep 2022).* [10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103740](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103740). Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Marrero, Gosling, Pennebaker and Harari (2022) took one short smartphone voice sample (mean 92 words, about 51 s) from each of 614 UT Austin intro-psychology students. They used XGBoost with Tree-SHAP feature selection to predict self-reported BFI-2 traits from 94 LIWC-2015 dimensions and 272 pyAudioAnalysis vocal features. The fit metric is out-of-sample R-squared from 100-fold CV, and the paper gives no permutation null. Averaged across the Big Five, the joint feature set explained 10.67% of variance, vocal features alone 6.24% and LIWC alone 3.13%. Extraversion, Conscientiousness and Neuroticism reached 10-20% with the joint set, Openness stayed under 10%, and gender was predicted at over 90%. The authors say the joint result is more than the sum of the two parts, but they give no causal identification and the criterion is self-report.
> 
> This paper is not new to the system. It was the stated method source for `big5_audio_faithful_prereg.md` and was cited in Addendum 1 of `big5_audio_prereg.md`. Melange ran three one-look audio tests at N=90 legislators. Two findings partly carried over: a full eGeMAPS-88 ridge predicted Pink-E (held-out r +0.284, perm-p .030) and Pink-ES (r +0.298, perm-p .017). The paper's headline did not carry over. Super-additivity missed its registered bars: vocal added +0.001 over linguistic for E against Pink and -0.046 for C, and the pilot delta was -0.004 over text against behavioural visibility (a design the faithful prereg calls a strawman). An unregistered round-2 context result was +0.119 for E over linguistic against visibility. Voice did not measure C (r -0.081). The A-ES halo got worse (|r| 0.819 and 0.946).
> 
> What the paper means for the system as built: it is the benchmark the audio lane has already tested and largely failed to reproduce in legislators, and it does not support moving audio into the engine. Two leads remain open. Its top features (MFCC, chroma, spectral entropy, deltas) are mostly missing from eGeMAPS-88. It also shows reliability disattenuation.

## Study

**Question.** (1) How much information about personality (Big Five, self-report) is conveyed by the linguistic (LIWC 2015) and vocal (pyAudioAnalysis) features of short, semi-structured voice samples? (2) Do semi-structured voice samples reveal more about some personality traits than others? Secondary benchmarks: depression, age and gender (p.2, lines 148-156; p.4).

**Theory.** Six potential mechanisms linking voice samples to personality (p.1-2): (1) personality drives what people talk about; (2) personality drives how people talk (e.g., loudness, smoking affecting voice); (3) content of speech affects how others treat the speaker, which affects personality; (4) delivery style affects treatment, which affects personality; (5) and (6) a third variable (education, culture, biology) affects personality and either what is said or how it is said. Background: language-personality links across written and spoken text (Pennebaker et al., 2003; Yarkoni, 2010). Prior voice-personality work used either observer ratings or narrow vocal parameters (p.2). The paper states the mechanisms are not tested (p.7).

**Data.** Sample: UT Austin Introductory Psychology students in a Synchronous Massive Online Course, Fall 2018 (UT1000 Project). Of 1,342 students who completed a 3-week self-tracking assignment, 1,087 used the smartphone app; 213 (email) and 42 (journal) were excluded because they had no audio (p.2). 992 smartphone students gave 25,163 audio responses (mean 21.32 s; mean 40.28 words) (p.2). Preprocessing kept responses with at least 50 words per Google Speech-to-text, leaving 5,838 responses (p.3). One sample per person was kept (each person's first response with at least 50 words; 64% were their first response) = 614 analytic samples, mean 50.74 s and 92.41 words (SD 73.69) (p.3). Unit of analysis: one voice sample per participant. Table 1 Ns: Extraversion, Conscientiousness, Neuroticism, Openness, Agreeableness 545 each (means ~37-46, alpha 0.76-0.89); Depression 485 (CES-D-10, mean 11.26); Age 564 (mean 18.57, SD 1.34); Gender 564 (Female 374, Male 190) (p.3). The text does not explain why the Ns differ from the 614 analytic samples beyond noting different survey completion times (Table 1 note). Predictors: 94 LIWC 2015 dimensions (p.3) and 272 pyAudioAnalysis vocal features (136 per 1-s segment statistic, mean and SD over 1-s segments) (p.3). Outcomes: BFI-2 (60 items, 5-point scale) at end of week two; CES-D-10 about two weeks after the assignment (p.2). Recording prompt: describe your situation and current thoughts and feelings, plus about 10 s of environmental sound (p.2).

**Method.** Two-stage, automated machine learning pipeline in R 4.1.2 (mlr 2.19.0, mlrMBO 1.1.5) with XGBoost (1.5.2.1) as the learner (p.3-4). Stage 1 (global search): 150 Latin Hypercube hyperparameter configurations, each scored by 10-fold CV; a Gaussian Process surrogate and CMA-ES then propose new configurations for sequential model-based optimization, run for at least 50 cycles (extended while improvement continued) (p.4). Feature set fixed at 100%. Stage 2 (feature selection): nested 10-fold CV within each outer fold, Tree SHAP values for each test sample, a pseudo partial R-squared per feature (R-squared of model with the feature's SHAP removed vs. full model), repeated five times and weighted by error; top-30 features kept per outer fold and XGBoost refit with stage-1 hyperparameters held fixed; performance is the mean over the 10 outer folds (p.4). Evaluation: out-of-sample R-squared for regression defined as 1 minus CV test MSE divided by outcome variance (p.4). For classification, log loss is used rather than AUC, and scored as 1 minus model log loss over intercept-only log loss (p.5). Uncertainty: no standard test fits the repeated selection, so final models were re-estimated with 100-fold CV (about 9% more training data per fold), on the assumption that a generalizable model improves with more data (p.5). Post-hoc: reliability (Cronbach alpha, mean feature test-retest correlation) used to disattenuate performance (Spearman 1961) and to test whether scale reliability predicts model performance (p.7-8).

**Identification.** None causal. The design is cross-validated out-of-sample prediction only; the authors state that without an experimental design the causal direction of each mechanism cannot be established and most variables cannot be manipulated (p.7, lines 907-913). The generalizability standard is that a model fitted on some people predicts unseen people's self-reported traits from voice data (p.9).

**Scope.** Population: UT Austin Introductory Psychology students who used a smartphone app (mean age 18.6; 374 female, 190 male in the gender analysis), with self-report personality, depression, age and gender. Era: data collected Fall 2018, published 2022. Context: one standardized, open-ended voice prompt with mixed content and environments (not a lab reading task). The paper does not state the speech language or dialect. Results are described as a conservative estimate for brief (about 92 words), constrained, student-only samples (p.8).

## Key findings

- Voice samples (joint LIWC + vocal features) explained 10.67% of variance in personality traits on average; LIWC alone 3.13%; vocal alone 6.24%. (joint 10.67%, vocal 6.24%, LIWC 3.13% (average across Big Five traits, out-of-sample R-squared, 100-fold CV); Abstract p.1; Results p.5, lines 568-571)
- Up to about 16% of trait variance was explained, mainly for Extraversion, Conscientiousness, Neuroticism and Agreeableness. (up to 16%; joint-set models for E, C, N in the 10-20% range; single feature sets under 10%; Results p.5, lines 572-575; Summary p.8)
- Openness was the least well predicted trait (under 10% variance). Agreeableness and Openness were the two least well predicted traits in the discussion. (Openness below 10%; Results p.5, lines 575-577; Discussion p.7, lines 849-852)
- Depression was predicted at 11-18% of variance, and gender at over 90%. Vocal features predicted gender almost perfectly. (depression 11-18%; gender over 90%; depression mean 11.26 (CES-D-10, N=485); Results p.5, lines 577-579; Discussion p.8, lines 1009-1013)
- Joint features beat single feature sets for all traits except Agreeableness (best with vocal features only). Gender was the other task where joint features were not best. (exception: Agreeableness (vocal-only best) and gender; Results p.5-6, lines 580-582, 614-616; Conclusion p.9, lines 1168-1171)
- Neuroticism was predicted better by linguistic features than by vocal features. Extraversion, Conscientiousness and Neuroticism were the traits where the joint set reached 10-20%. (E, C, N joint 10-20%; single sets under 10%; Results p.5, lines 583-595; Discussion p.8-9, lines 1014-1019)
- Joint-feature performance was more than the sum of the separate feature sets, which the authors read as an interaction between language and voice. (joint performance exceeded sum of LIWC and vocal performance; Results p.6, lines 606-608; Discussion p.6-7, lines 662-670; Conclusion p.9, lines 1176-1181)
- Top LIWC predictors: negate (Extraversion), pronoun (Conscientiousness); the text names no top LIWC feature for Neuroticism. Top features are mostly vocal (MFCC, chroma, spectral entropy, delta features), not pitch or loudness. Feature rankings are reported as hard to interpret. (top-5 features per condition in Table 2; features ordered by pseudo partial R-squared; Discussion p.6-8, Table 2 (p.6-7), lines 671-876)
- Post-hoc: the reliability-prediction correlation was t(4)=2.65, r=0.80, p=.057, but leave-one-out CV gave r=0.309, so the true association is much smaller. Reliability did not change the ordering of traits. (t(4)=2.65; r=0.80 (p=.057, CI -0.03 to 0.98); LOO r=0.309; Post-hoc p.7, lines 930-935; Table 3 and text p.8, lines 944-954)
- Disattenuated (reliability-corrected) out-of-sample R-squared roughly tripled to quadrupled; the ordering of traits stayed the same. Original to disattenuated: E 0.16 to 0.57; C 0.11 to 0.47; N 0.15 to 0.56; O 0.04 to 0.17; A 0.07 to 0.26; depression 0.18 to 0.67. (E 0.16->0.57; C 0.11->0.47; N 0.15->0.56; O 0.04->0.17; A 0.07->0.26; Dep 0.18->0.67; Table 3, p.8, lines 1065-1102; text p.8-9, lines 945-948, 1182-1190)
- Sample reduction and sample size: 25,163 responses reduced to 5,838 with at least 50 words, then to 614 (one per person). Mean word count of final samples 92.41. (25,163 -> 5,838 -> 614; mean 92.41 words; Preprocessing p.3, lines 244-256)
- Model-based uncertainty: 100-fold CV improved out-of-sample R-squared by about 4% on average (gender by about 1.5%). (approximately 4 percentage points on average; gender about 1.5%; Results p.5-6, lines 609-613)

## Limitations

- Samples were brief (mean about 92 words), collected in a constrained context (a class-assignment prompt), and drawn from Introductory Psychology students; the authors call the results a conservative estimate (p.8, lines 1031-1041).
- The criterion was self-report Big Five scores, which the authors acknowledge have their own biases; they call for non-self-report validation (p.8, lines 1049-1055).
- The 50-word threshold is acknowledged as arbitrary; it was set from a Reddit study (25 words) and doubled (p.3, lines 237-243).
- Two-stage modeling: hyperparameters tuned on 100% of features were reused after selecting the top 30, which the authors say may be suboptimal. This likely explains why the joint set underperformed vocal-only for Agreeableness (p.9, lines 1108-1135).
- No pooling across tasks: predicted gender, age or depression were not used as features for personality prediction (p.9, lines 1136-1149).
- No standard statistical test fits the repeated model and feature selection; the uncertainty approach relies on an untested assumption about 100-fold CV (p.5, lines 528-557).
- Feature-level interpretation is heavily caveated: many features, non-monotonic effects, and variance explained is not the same as importance (p.6, lines 631-641). The explanations offered for feature-trait links are described as highly speculative (p.8, lines 1020-1022).
- Post-hoc reliability correlation (r=0.80, p=.057) is not robust under leave-one-out (r=0.309) (p.7).
- No causal identification and no experimental design; mechanisms in the Introduction were not tested (p.7, lines 907-913).
- Ns differ across outcomes (Table 1 note: 545 for Big Five, 485 for depression, 564 for age and gender); the text does not fully reconcile these with the 614 analytic samples (p.3).
- Raw voice data may contain personally identifiable information; the authors offer analyses or anonymized features on request (p.9, lines 1191-1194). Supplementary files: FeatureRankings.csv, SHAP_Data.RDS, SHAP_Indicators.RDS (p.6).

## Gaps and flags

- The paper's top-ranked vocal features (MFCCs, chroma, spectral entropy, delta features) are mostly missing from eGeMAPS v02-88. eGeMAPS has only MFCC1-4 and no chroma, and the round-2 prereg names an MFCC route as untried. Any future audio look could only use a richer feature set if it is pre-registered and kept to a single look.
- Disattenuation: the paper shows reliability-corrected out-of-sample R-squared roughly triples (E 0.16 to 0.57). The system reports validity coefficients against Pink (reliability about .67) and RKH and attenuation is mentioned only in prose.
- There is no self-report or observer personality criterion for legislators, so a Marrero-faithful self-report replication cannot be run. Pink is the stand-in criterion, and it shares method with the linguistic block, which biases the super-additivity bar against vocal features.
- Venue and editing confound: the paper used one unedited smartphone sample per person. The system pools edited broadcast and floor audio with venue effects (disfluency about 26 per 1k words live vs 0.22 on NPR), and that variance is not modelled in the trait tests.
- Nothing in the paper, or in the system's audio results, links voice-derived traits to legislative behaviour or lobbying responsiveness. Even if voice measured traits, that would not support feeding them into the receptivity layer.
- Do not cite this paper as support for voice adding to text in legislators. Super-additivity missed its registered bars in the Melange looks (+0.001 against Pink-E, -0.046 for C, and -0.004 in the pilot against visibility), and the system's own record says the paper's super-additivity 'did not transfer'. The one positive result is an unregistered context result, exploratory and not a registered test: vocal added +0.119 for E against behavioural visibility in round 2.
- Addendum 1 of big5_audio_prereg.md (line 87) reads the paper as showing 'A carried MORE by voice than text = halo-relevant'. The authors attribute Agreeableness doing best with vocal-only features to hyperparameters tuned on the full feature set being reused after selection, which they call likely suboptimal (p.9, lines 1108-1135). That reading over-states the paper and should not be repeated.
- Metric mismatch: the paper reports out-of-sample R-squared (1 - CV MSE / variance); Melange reports held-out correlation r. Converting the paper's 6-16% to r of about .25-.40 as an 'expected effect' is approximate, and any comparison should name both metrics and their denominators (N=545 self-report students vs N=90 legislators against Pink).
- No conflict with D-025, D-021, the rejected SNA proposals or the reciprocity rulings: the paper has no network, lobbying or edge content.
