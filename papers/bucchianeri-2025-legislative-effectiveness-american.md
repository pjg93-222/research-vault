# Legislative Effectiveness in the American States

**Peter Bucchianeri, Craig Volden, Alan E. Wiseman (2025).** *American Political Science Review, 119(1), 21-39 (first published online 15 Feb 2024).* [10.1017/S0003055424000042](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055424000042). Theme: [[themes/legislative-behavior-effectiveness|Legislative Behavior & Effectiveness]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Bucchianeri, Volden and Wiseman adapt the congressional Legislative Effectiveness Score to state legislatures. The result is the SLES: 80,344 legislator-term scores across 97 chambers, 1987-2018, built from about 1.8 million bills. Each score is a weighted average of 15 indicators: the share of a member's sponsored bills that reach each of five stages (introduced, action in committee, action beyond committee, passed chamber, law), at three significance levels weighted 1, 5 and 10. Scores are normalized to mean 1 within each chamber-term. The authors stress that absolute scores should not be compared across chambers or eras.
> 
> They check validity three ways. A hand audit found 95.2% of 49,037 sampled bills correctly coded. In North Carolina (2005-2012), SLES alone explains about half the variance in subjective effectiveness rankings and beats simple hit rates. Scores also persist from term to term (rho 0.38-0.66, highest when majority control does not change). In pooled OLS, majority status adds 0.370 (0.362 lower chamber, 0.387 upper) and a committee chair adds 0.507 (0.609 lower vs 0.307 upper). Moderates are more effective. Two chamber-level studies find that majority advantage grows with polarization, majority-party cohesion and professionalism (Study 1 tests named hypotheses; only Study 2 is exploratory). All results are associational.
> 
> What this means for Paul's system: the paper is the method source for the effectiveness shadow (fec/engine/legislative_effectiveness.py), which copies the five stages, the 1/5/10 weights and per-chamber-term normalization. The shadow departs from SLES in several ways: cosponsor credit with a 180-day timing decay, letters forced to weight 5, a 0.7/0.3 advocacy-results composite, and significance taken from an upstream impact record. The paper's validation evidence therefore does not carry over to those changes. The paper also bears on three features of the live rank:
> - Leg% and Influence% are pooled across the House and Senate.
> - The paper's hit-rate comparison is SLES against hit rates in North Carolina; whether it applies to the live efficiency term is the analyst's inference.
> - Nothing controls for majority status, which the paper finds is associated with advancement (0.370 pooled).
> 
> The brain records that CEL LES is roughly orthogonal to the live score (+0.10 Spearman); that value was not verified in this check.

## Study

**Question.** Can individual state legislators' lawmaking effectiveness be measured comparably across 97 state legislative chambers and over time, and does the measure validly capture effectiveness? Two illustrative questions follow: (1) how does the majority-party advantage in lawmaking vary with ideological polarization, majority-party cohesion, and seat-share imbalance? (2) how do institutional designs (pay, professionalism, session length, staff, agenda-control and gatekeeping rules, committee-appointment votes, chamber size, term limits) shape the relative lawmaking power of majority vs minority, chairs vs rank-and-file, and senior vs freshman legislators? (pp. 21-22, 30-34)

**Theory.** Effectiveness is defined as 'the proven ability to advance a member's agenda items through the legislative process and into law' (Volden and Wiseman 2014, quoted p. 22). It is measured across lawmaking stages, with later and rarer stages weighted more heavily (pp. 22-23). Study 1 draws on conditional party government (Aldrich and Battista 2002): polarization and majority cohesion should raise majority advantage. A competing electoral account says tenuous majorities intensify partisanship (Rosenthal 1998). Median-voter logic predicts moderates do better (Black 1958; Downs 1957; Hitt, Volden and Wiseman 2017). Study 2 is explicitly exploratory with no specific hypotheses; it draws on Anzia and Jackman (2013) for agenda and gatekeeping effects (pp. 30-34).

**Data.** Sample: 80,344 legislator-term SLES scores across 1,032 chamber-terms (legislative sessions) in 97 state legislative chambers, 1987-2018, built from about 1.8 million bills (p. 21). Kansas is excluded because sponsor names are rarely attached to bills (fn. 1, p. 22). Four states enter after 2003 (MA 2009; NE, OR, RI 2007). Unit for Table 1: legislator-term (72,888 observations with full covariates; 53,846 lower-chamber and 19,042 upper-chamber). Unit for Tables 2 and 3: chamber-term (868 and 874 chamber-terms in Table 2; 776-818 in Table 3). Sources: state government online legislative archives (bill histories and sponsors), LexisNexis and NewsBank newspaper coverage (substantive significance), Shor and McCarty (2011) ideal points (coverage 1993-2016), Anzia and Jackman (2013) procedural rules, and North Carolina NCCPPR rankings for 2005-2012 (Edwards 2018).

**Method.** Measure: a weighted average of 15 indicators. These are the shares of a legislator's bills that reach each of five stages (introduced, action in committee, action beyond committee, passed home chamber, became law) at each of three significance levels (commemorative weight 1, substantive weight 5, substantive and significant weight 10). Scores are normalized so each chamber-term has mean 1 (Eq. 1, pp. 23-24). Validity checks: hand-coding audit, North Carolina criterion validation, and construct validation via lagged correlations and OLS. Table 1 is OLS with fixed effects by term and chamber (or state), with standard errors clustered by legislator. Tables 2 and 3 are chamber-level OLS with standard errors clustered by state chamber. Outcomes are the median majority-minus-minority SLES gap, the share of majority members above the minority median, and median differences for chairs vs rank-and-file and non-freshmen vs freshmen. Tests are two-tailed (+p<0.1, *p<0.05, **p<0.01).</method>
<parameter name="identification">No causal identification strategy. Results are associational. Fixed effects absorb state, chamber and term differences. The authors state that endogeneity is unaddressed: rules may be chosen by strong parties, and term limits or professionalism may respond to entrenched power (p. 34). Study 2 is framed as illustrative and exploratory.

**Scope.** Population: state legislators in 97 chambers (49 states, derived from Kansas exclusion and Nebraska's unicameral chamber), 1987-2018. Institution: state legislatures only. The paper says SLES values should not be compared directly across chambers or eras, because absolute scores are relative to each chamber-term; comparisons of factor effects across settings are appropriate (pp. 23-24). It explicitly does not capture oversight, obstruction, constituency service, or behind-the-scenes lawmaking (fn. 8, p. 24).

## Key findings

- Hand-coding audit of the bill-coding pipeline: research assistants found 46,693 of 49,037 sampled bills (95.2%) accurately coded; after fixes, alignment exceeded 99%. Numbers: 46,693 / 49,037 = 95.2%; >99% after revision. (p. 24, Validity Explorations, first check)
- Criterion validity: SLES tracks North Carolina's subjective NCCPPR effectiveness rankings and outperforms hit-rate metrics. Numbers: SLES alone explains about half the variance in NCCPPR rankings (2005-2012). (p. 24; Figure 1; Table A4 in Supplementary Appendix)
- Construct validity over time: lagged SLES correlates positively with current SLES, strongest when majority control is unchanged. Numbers: rho = 0.38 (entered majority), 0.40 (entered minority), 0.66 (no change in control). (p. 25; Figure 2)
- Seniority, chairs, and majority status predict higher SLES in the pooled OLS (Model 1.1). Numbers: Seniority 0.032 per term (about 3% of mean 1); Committee chair 0.507; Majority party 0.370, which is 58% above the minority mean of 0.636 (p. 26). (p. 25-26; Table 1 (p. 27))
- Ideological moderates are more effective (distance from chamber median is negative), and women and African American and Hispanic legislators score lower on average. Numbers: Distance from Median -0.114 (p<0.01); Female -0.034 (p<0.05) in full sample; African American -0.101 (p<0.01); Hispanic -0.078 (p<0.01). (p. 26-27; Table 1; Figure 4 (p. 28))
- Institutional positions matter more in lower chambers; the Table 1 advantages of leadership, chairs and power committees are larger in the lower chamber. Numbers: Committee chair 0.609 (lower) vs 0.307 (upper); Majority party 0.362 vs 0.387 (similar). (p. 27 and Figure 6 (p. 30))
- Study 1: polarization and majority-party cohesion widen the majority advantage (conditional party government). Numbers: Polarization 0.158 (SLES gap, p<0.05) and 0.072 (share more effective, p<0.01); 1 SD (0.48) increase in polarization gives +0.075 gap and +3.5 points; majority heterogeneity -0.886 and -0.347 (p<0.01); 1 SD decline gives +0.10 gap and +4.0 points. (p. 32; Table 2 (p. 33))
- Study 1: insecure majorities raise partisanship. Smaller seat-share imbalance widens the majority SLES advantage; the authors read the negative coefficient as support for insecure majorities. Numbers: Partisan Seat Share Imbalance -0.529 (p<0.01) on SLES gap and -0.168 (p<0.01) on share; Arkansas (imbalance 0.49) vs Colorado (0.03) implies a 0.24-point larger SLES gap in Colorado (Model 2.1). (p. 32; Table 2)
- Study 1: professionalism, gatekeeping and calendar control correlate with a larger majority advantage. Other covariates were weaker. Numbers: Legislative professionalism 0.693 (gap, p<0.01) and 0.351 (share, p<0.01); Majority Party Controls Calendar 0.128 and 0.058 (p<0.05); Committee Gatekeeping 0.128 (gap, p<0.05). (p. 32-33; Table 2)
- Study 2: session length and majority-party calendar control favor majority and chairs over others. Session length raises the majority and chair gaps. Numbers: Log Session Length 0.179 (Partisan Difference, p<0.01) and 0.206 (Chair Difference, p<0.01); Majority Party Controls Calendar 0.116 (p<0.05) on partisan gap. (p. 34-35; Table 3 (p. 35))
- Study 2: staff per legislator narrows the chair gap; chamber votes on committee appointments narrow the chair gap; larger chambers widen the chair gap. Numbers: Staff per Legislator -0.020 (chair gap, p<0.05); Chamber Votes on Committee Appointments -0.180 (chair gap, p<0.01); Log Chamber Size 0.205 (chair gap, p<0.05). (p. 34-35; Table 3)
- Study 2: higher pay is associated with a smaller seniority gap among majority members, and longer sessions with a smaller seniority gap among minority members; term limits show negative coefficients in the first three columns. Numbers: Log Annual Salary -0.032 (majority seniority gap, p<0.01) and -0.026 (minority, p<0.05); Log Session Length -0.071 (minority seniority gap, p<0.01); Term Limits -0.094 (majority seniority gap, p<0.1); Polarization -0.084 (minority seniority gap, p<0.01). (p. 34-35; Table 3; p. 36 conclusion)
- Majority-party and chair advantages are sensitive to design choices. The authors interpret the institutional results as incumbents and chairs keeping control. Numbers: Quoted in short_quotes; no additional numbers. (p. 35-36 (Study 2 closing and Conclusions))

## Limitations

- Results are associational. The authors say the institutional-design relationships may face endogeneity (rules chosen by strong parties; term limits or professionalism adopted to weaken entrenched politicians) (p. 34).
- Study 2 is explicitly exploratory, with no specific hypotheses tested (p. 34).
- Effectiveness excludes oversight, voting for district interests, communication, and obstruction; it also misses behind-the-scenes lawmaking and bills that hitchhike on must-pass legislation (pp. 22, 24, fn. 8).
- Absolute SLES values are not comparable across chambers or eras; only relative comparisons of factor effects are recommended (pp. 23-24).
- Sponsor attribution for committee bills and multiple-sponsor bills required judgment (fn. 2, p. 22), and some states (Massachusetts by-request bills, Connecticut, Iowa, Idaho committee bills) required case-by-case decisions (p. 23-24).
- Substantive significance is coded from newspaper mentions and state-specific dictionaries (p. 23), with a coding error rate of 4.8% before revision (p. 24).
- SLES and NCCPPR rankings may tap different concepts; party leaders who act behind the scenes may be underrated by SLES (p. 24).
- Caution is urged about women and minority coefficients, which may reflect unmodeled factors (p. 26).
- About 8,000 Table 1 observations are lost to missing covariates, and Nebraska's 301 observations are dropped as nonpartisan (fn. 9, p. 25).
- Term limits are a binary indicator; binding status and length are not modeled in the main text (fn. 20, p. 34; Table A9 in appendix).
- Kansas is excluded for data quality (fn. 1, p. 22).

## Gaps and flags

- Live Leg% (legacy mode) and Influence% (committee raw/max) pool the House and Senate in one percentile or maximum. The paper says effectiveness scores are relative to each chamber-term and should not be compared across chambers. Only the unactivated shadow normalizes within each chamber.
- No component models majority versus minority status, even though the paper finds it is associated with advancement (0.370 pooled, 58% above the minority mean; the chair estimate of 0.507 is larger). The live efficiency term and the say-do advancement threshold both absorb it unadjusted.
- Score persistence depends on whether majority control changed (rho 0.38/0.40 vs 0.66). The 0.8-per-Congress decay in the shadow and in congress-activity scoring treats every prior Congress alike, whatever happened to party control.
- The shadow's substantive-significance tiering, which comes from an upstream impact record, has no coding audit comparable to the paper's 49,037-bill check (95.2% correct before revision).
- The live blended score has no external effectiveness validation. The brain records CEL LES as roughly orthogonal to it (+0.10, not verified here) and the shadow as not activated (activation is Paul's product decision).
- No ideological-distance-from-median term appears in any effectiveness or advancement measure, although moderates score higher (-0.114 per unit of distance).
- Flag: Pooled coefficients: the paper's Model 1.1 chair (0.507) and majority (0.370) estimates pool 97 chambers. They are quoted here with lower/upper strata (0.609/0.307; 0.362/0.387). Any downstream reuse must keep the strata or carry an UNSTRATIFIED label.
- Flag: The shadow is labeled Bucchianeri/Volden/Wiseman-style in the brain, but it departs from SLES in cosponsor credit, timing decay, letters forced to weight 5, the 0.7/0.3 composite, and significance from upstream impact data. Citing this paper's validity checks as validation of the shadow would over-claim.
- Flag: The paper presents associations only and calls Study 2 exploratory (Study 1 tests two named hypotheses), with endogenous institutions (p. 34). None of its institutional coefficients should be imported as multipliers.
- Flag: The paper cites network-effectiveness work (Battaglini et al. 2020; Kirkland 2011). It does not argue for dropping regular cosponsorship edges or for per-edge volume normalization, so it does not conflict with the two rejected SNA proposals.
- Flag: No LDA, revolving-door or graph-rank language is involved, so D-021 and D-025 are not implicated.

The paper agrees with Paul's takeaway that effectiveness should be measured by stage progression, with LES as the criterion; he highlighted LES via Aref & Neal and Sotoudeh. It also supports his preference for observable, costly action. His recorded citations for the effectiveness shadow (Aref & Neal, Sotoudeh, Ramey) omit this paper, even though the shadow lane was commissioned in September as a Bucchianeri/Volden/Wiseman-style build. On that point his notes are stale. They also do not reflect two facts recorded in the brain: the live score is roughly orthogonal to CEL LES (+0.10), and the shadow has been built and verified but not activated. The paper gives no support to any particular pillar weights, so his position that the blend weights are untested owner policy still stands.
