# Conflating Lobbying and PACs: The Surprisingly Low Overlap in Organizational Lobbying and Campaign Expenditures

**Alexander C. Furnas, Timothy M. LaPira, Clare Brock (2025).** *PS: Political Science & Politics (Cambridge University Press on behalf of the American Political Science Association), October 2025, pp. 597-605, "Politics" section. Metadata hint corrected: the journal is PS: Political Science & Politics, confirmed by the copyright line, running footer and data-availability statement..* [10.1017/S1049096525100929](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1049096525100929). Theme: [[themes/lobbying|Lobbying]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Furnas, LaPira and Brock (PS, Oct 2025) put Center for Responsive Politics LDA lobbying records and FEC records of organization and affiliated-PAC giving side by side for each organization, 1998-2018. Most politically active organizations do only one of the two. In 1998 (n=9,076 organizations), 64% only lobbied, 24% only gave through a PAC and 12% did both. By 2008 (n=15,729) the split was 70/20/10. Pooled over 1998-2018, 3,427 of 54,517 organizations (6.3%) did both. That denominator is the extractor's sum of the Figure 4 counts and includes 7,524 organizations Figure 4 labels 'Not Active'.
> 
> Within that minority sits most of the lobbying money. About 75% of lobbying spending in 1998-2018 came from organizations that also gave through a PAC. Their mean yearly lobbying spend was $403k, against $64k for organizations that only lobbied. Going the other way, the share of PAC dollars that came from lobbying organizations fell from over 65% in 1998 to about 30% in 2018. Employee giving, Super PACs and dark money are excluded. The paper is purely descriptive: no model, no identification strategy, and nothing about access or votes.
> 
> For Paul's systems the paper matters in three ways:
> 
> - **FEC as a stand-in for lobbying.** Across the population, campaign money is a poor proxy for lobbying. That supports the twice-locked rule that FEC exposure is never presented as a lobbying predictor. It also argues against reading the 42-month figure as a lead time: in the code it is only how much fresher the FEC data is than the LDA data.
> - **The flip model's competitor-money leg.** Its money leg counts only industry PAC money (the model also has a peer/network leg). That money leg can see the large trade associations, which almost all have PACs. It cannot see organizations that lobby without a PAC.
> - **Mentat's counts.** Mentat matches individual employee giving by employer string. The paper's definition of organizational giving excludes employees, so its overlap figures do not carry over to Mentat's totals without first aligning the two definitions.

## Study

**Question:** Are campaign contributions (PAC giving) and lobbying complements, substitutes, or unrelated forms of organizational political engagement? Empirically: how much do the interest groups that lobby overlap with the groups that contribute through PACs, and which types of organizations do each?

**Theory:** Access-purchase account: PAC contributions open doors that lobbyists then use, so PAC givers should also lobby and heavy lobbyists should have PACs. Election-targeting account: contributions mainly aim at electoral outcomes, so the two activities need not be linked; organizations can also lobby without donating. The authors treat the two as conceptually distinct: lobbying involves no direct transfer to members, while contributions do. They explicitly do not theorize why organizations choose one, both, or neither.

**Data:** Center for Responsive Politics (CRP) data, curated from LDA Registrations and Activity Reports (LaPira and Thomas 2020) and FEC contribution disclosures by organizations and their officially affiliated PACs, linked by an expert-curated CRP crosswalk. Unit: organization (interest group). Period: 1998-2018 (even-numbered cycles in the figures). Sample sizes stated in the text: 9,076 organizations reporting political involvement in 1998; 15,729 in the 2008 cycle (text: "a decade later"). Figure 4 pooled counts over 1998-2018: Only Lobbies n=34,038; Only Contributes n=9,528; Lobbies and Contributes n=3,427; Not Active n=7,524 (sum 54,517, computed by the extractor). Lobbying totals include in-house and contract-firm lobbyists. Figure 1 cycle-level bar labels did not parse cleanly from the text layer, so only the figures stated in the prose are used here.

**Method:** Descriptive only. Figure 1: yearly share of organizations that are lobby-only, PAC-only, or both. Figure 2: share of yearly lobbying expenditures and of PAC contributions by group type. Figure 3: distributions of average yearly PAC contributions (3a) and average yearly lobbying spending (3b) by group type, shown as dot plots with violin density, mean, IQR and 2.5/97.5 percentiles. Figure 4: log-log scatter of total lobbying spending against total PAC contributions per organization, with marginal histograms, coloured by the four activity categories. No regression, hypothesis test, or model is reported.

**Identification:** None. The paper makes no causal claim and uses no identification strategy; it is a descriptive accounting of overlap.

**Scope:** Population: US federal interest-group organizations that register to lobby under the LDA or are PAC or political-committee filers in CRP data, 1998-2018. Institution: Congress (lobbying) and federal candidate elections (contributions). Excluded by design: independent expenditures and Super PACs (note 1, p. 604); dark-money contributions (unobserved by definition, p. 599); contributions by employees and contract lobbyists, which are not counted as organizational giving (p. 599).

## Key findings

- Most politically active organizations engage in only one of the two activities; only a small minority do both. The abstract states the headline finding, and the results section gives the counts. (1998 (n=9,076 orgs): 24% PAC-only, 64% lobby-only, 12% both. Around 2008 (n=15,729 orgs): 20% PAC-only, 70% lobby-only, 10% both. Percentages in each year sum to 100, so the categories are treated as exclusive.; Abstract, p. 597; Results, p. 600)
- Pooled over 1998-2018, only a small share of organizations do both. (Lobby-only ~34,000 (n=34,038); PAC-only ~9,500 (n=9,528); registered but not active ~7,500 (n=7,524); both n=3,427 = 6.3% of the 54,517 total (6.3% is stated in the text; the 54,517 denominator is the extractor's sum).; Results, p. 601; Figure 4, p. 603)
- Lobbying money is concentrated among organizations that also make PAC contributions, but that share fell over time. (About 75% of lobbying expenditures (1998-2018) came from organizations that also contributed through PACs. The share of PAC contributions coming from organizations that also lobby fell from more than 65% in 1998 to about 30% in 2018.; Results, Figure 2 text, p. 601)
- Organizations that both lobby and contribute spend far more on lobbying than lobby-only organizations. (Both: mean $403,435 (IQR $118,825-$1,558,597). Lobby-only: mean $64,000 (IQR $30,000-$130,000).; Results, Figure 3b, p. 601)
- PAC contributions are higher on average among dual-activity organizations, and the distribution is very wide. (Contribute-only: mean $7,857 (IQR $2,000-$33,566). Both: mean $31,167 (IQR $8,227-$1,082,366). The largest average contributors exceed $100 million (examples named: Crossroads GPS, Restore Our Future, Priorities USA).; Results, Figure 3a, p. 601)
- The top lobbying groups spend the most and almost always have affiliated PACs, so most lobbying money comes from a few big spenders that also have PACs. (No separate numeric estimate beyond the Figure 2 and Figure 3 results above.; Abstract, p. 597; Implications, p. 602)
- Lobbying spending starts high and concentrates between $10,000 and $1 million, while PAC spending is more uneven, with many contributors giving under $10,000. (Few lobbying organizations report under $10,000. Many PAC givers are under $10,000.; Implications, Public Discourse, p. 602)
- The authors restate the overlap as a yearly figure and note that organizations doing both are likely better resourced and, on average, more conservative. The conservatism point is cited to Crosson, Furnas and Lorenz (2020), not tested here. (Only a 10% to 15% overlap between lobbying-registered and contributing organizations in any given year.; Implications, Rationales, third point, p. 604)

## Limitations

- Only contributions to candidates are counted. Independent expenditures and Super PACs are excluded (note 1, p. 604).
- Dark-money contributions are not observed, by definition, because they are unaffiliated with disclosing organizations (p. 599).
- Contributions by employees and contract lobbyists are not counted as organizational giving (p. 599). The paper says individual lobbyist donations need further study (p. 603-604).
- Possible undercounting of lobbying organizations with PACs because of lax LDA reporting and enforcement. The authors argue that shadow-lobbyist gaps sit at the individual level, not the organization level (note 3, p. 604).
- Purely descriptive. The paper does not test why organizations choose one, both, or neither, and says it did not speculate about motivations (p. 599). It calls for future work on that question (p. 599, p. 604).
- The authors note that individual corporate-executive giving, including to Super PACs, is not analyzed (p. 604).
- Figure 1 cycle-level counts were not reliably recoverable from the text layer; the prose figures are used instead.

## Gaps and flags

- There is no census, by organization, of who lobbies only, who gives through a PAC only, and who does both, for the CU, bank, fintech and crypto opponent sets. So the system cannot say how much of the opposition's lobbying force is invisible to the PAC-only money channel in the flip and money-response models.
- The flip model's competitor leg (flip_model.py:39) has no lobbying-intensity term. Organizations that lobby without a PAC (64-70% of all politically active organizations in the paper's data, about 84-88% of lobbying organizations) add nothing to b's money leg. Any fix would need a lobbying-spend source other than LDA member edges (D-021). The paper does not say how to weight such a source, and this is a gap, not a recommendation.
- The opposition prior and Mentat use employee giving, while the paper and congestion.json use organizational and affiliated-PAC giving. No reconciliation sits between the two definitions, and the paper shows the definition decides who counts as 'giving' at all.
- LD-203 lobbyist contribution reports (the HLOGA channel the paper says conflates lobbying with giving) are used only to resolve a registrant's identity (fec/surfaces/build_staffer_groups_section.py:907). That is consistent with D-021. The system does not measure contributions from individual lobbyists, which the paper flags as understudied.
- The '42-month lead' (FEC leads LDA) is computed in build_opposition_prior.py:564-572 as a data-freshness gap, not an estimated lead. The paper's 64-70% lobby-only share argues against any wording that implies an organization's FEC giving foreshadows its own lobbying. That is consistent with the twice-locked rule that FEC exposure is never a lobbying predictor.
- Paul's position list cites this paper under flip-model as support for 'money buys access'. The paper tests neither access nor votes: it is descriptive overlap only. It must not be cited as support for the WM 0.35 / WP 0.65 split or for any access claim.
- LDA stays an identity source (D-021). The paper's discussion of HLOGA LD-203 contribution reporting is not a licence to turn LD-203 contributions into member edges or weights.
- Overlap figures must keep their windows and denominators. Per year: 12% (1998, n=9,076) and 10% (2008, n=15,729). Pooled: 6.3% of 54,517 organizations, 1998-2018; that denominator is the extractor's sum of the Figure 4 counts, not printed in the paper, and includes 7,524 'Not Active' organizations. The 75% lobbying-dollar share is pooled over 1998-2018.

Paul's highlights (low overlap, 'do not conflate lobbying with electioneering', the top lobbying groups almost always have PACs) agree with where the system now stands. Bridge round 3 found a four-control partial of zero, and the standing rule bars FEC exposure as a lobbying predictor. His fec-lda-bridge position is right that his own notes expected donations to lead lobbying, and that expectation is stale. On 2026-07-31 he ratified FEC as a 'precognition' heuristic for lobbying attention, and the 42-month figure that came out of that is a freshness gap, not a lead. His flip-model entry cites the paper alongside 'money buys access', but the paper is silent on access and votes. The only relevant thing it shows is that PAC money identifies the large dual-activity spenders and misses organizations that lobby without a PAC. His election-year timing highlight comes from the paper's literature review, not from its own findings. So for mentat-giving-forecast.cycle-model it is context only, not evidence. ## Related papers

- [[kim-2020-strategic-sequential-links]] — complements — Kim, Stuckatz and Wolters study firm-politician dyads among publicly traded US manufacturing firms (2,703 that lobbied or donated, 2008-2018) and test whether donations precede lobbying.
