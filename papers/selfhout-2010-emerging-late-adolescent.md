# Emerging Late Adolescent Friendship Networks and Big Five Personality Traits: A Social Network Approach

**Maarten Selfhout, William Burk, Susan Branje, Jaap Denissen, Marcel van Aken, Wim Meeus (2010).** *Journal of Personality, 78(2), April 2010 (printed pages 510-538 visible; first-page header not in extracted text).* [DOI 10.1111/j.1467-6494.2010.00625.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6494.2010.00625.x). Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Selfhout and colleagues followed 205 Dutch psychology freshmen in 10 randomly assigned introduction groups. They collected five monthly waves of friendship nominations, covering about the first 4 months of university, and analysed them with SIENA actor-oriented models. Each group was modelled separately and the results were then pooled by meta-analysis. Over the first three months the networks grew denser and then levelled off: average degree went from 1.71 to 3.64, and transitivity from .33 to .45. The structural terms were large in every group. Reciprocity was 1.73, and the score tests for transitivity and distance-2 ran from 15.7 to 93.4. A model that looks only at pairs would therefore misfit. Traits had small effects on individuals. Extraverts nominated more friends (ego effect .10), agreeable students were nominated more often (alter effect .14), and openness, conscientiousness and neuroticism had no ego or alter effect. Similarity in openness, extraversion and agreeableness had the largest effects (1.45 to 1.55), but they varied across groups: they were significant in 7, 8 and 10 of the 10 groups respectively.
> 
> The paper does not touch lobbying, legislators or elections. Its value for Paul's systems is narrow and comes in two parts. First, it shows that personality matters mostly for pairs of people, through how similar they are. A trait level on one person matters much less. Second, it shows how to test similarity: inside a model that also controls for reciprocity and closure, because a raw correlation can mislead. That bears on the persona-homophily null and on the receptivity layer, where every input describes one member. It also bears on the cosponsor forecaster, which has no reciprocity or closure features, and on the stance co-movement test, which is only a pair-level test. Transfer is weak throughout. These are self-rated single-item traits among strangers forming friendships. Legislators meeting lobbyists, or building cosponsorship records over years, are a different setting.

## Study

**Question.** Which self-rated Big Five traits predict friendship selection among just-acquainted late adolescents, separating selecting friends (ego), being selected as a friend (alter) and selecting similar friends (similarity), while controlling for network structure and sex; and how do friendship networks develop during acquaintanceship (pp. 513-514)?

**Theory.** Main-effect accounts: extraversion as reward-seeking with more positive affect in social situations (Elphick et al. 1998; Fleeson et al. 2002; pp. 509-510); agreeableness as prosocial and altruistic behavior that makes people attractive friends (pp. 510, 528-529). Similarity-attraction (Byrne 1971) with reinforcement-affect (Clore and Byrne 1974) and uncertainty reduction (Berger and Calabrese 1975) mechanisms (pp. 511-512). Trait-specific similarity argued for extraversion (predictable interaction styles) and agreeableness (evolutionary game theory of altruism; Maynard Smith 1984; Gilchrist 2007) (pp. 512-513). Network closure: friendships form through existing ties, so transitivity and geodesic distance-2 are modeled (p. 514; definitions p. 519).

**Data.** Sample: 205 psychology freshmen (M age 18.9, SD 1.6; 168 women = 82%; 189 Dutch = 92%) at Utrecht University, the Netherlands, nested in 10 introduction groups (of 20 groups of 16-24 students; 489 students randomly assigned; 378 agreed from 18 groups; 10 groups with more than 80% registration retained; 221 of 238 agreed, 93%; 205 of 221 retained at Wave 5, 93%) (pp. 514-516). Unit: directed friendship nominations among group members (dichotomized, 1 if 4 or higher on a 6-point scale), 5 monthly waves starting in week 2 of the first semester, i.e., roughly the first 4 months of university (pp. 515-516). Abstract says first year; the text covers about 4 months. Data-collection year not stated in text. Personality from single-item TIPI-r self-ratings on 1-7 bipolar scales, monthly (p. 516).

**Method.** Stochastic actor-oriented models for network evolution estimated in SIENA (Snijders 2001; Snijders et al. 2006), run separately for each of 10 groups, then combined by multilevel meta-analysis of parameter estimates (Snijders and Baerveldt 2003) with group-size weighting (pp. 517-521). Network effects: outdegree, reciprocity, transitivity and geodesic distance-2 (GD-2). Covariates: sex and five Big Five traits, each with ego, alter and similarity parameters (18 parameters entered together; p. 520). Two-sided significance at p less than .01. Score tests (chi-square above 3.84, df 1) assessed whether transitivity and GD-2 improve fit (p. 520). Tested whether the variance of each mean effect differs from zero for between-group heterogeneity (p. 521).

**Identification.** The text does not claim causal identification. Identification rests on longitudinal actor-based modeling of tie changes conditional on network state, simultaneous ego, alter and similarity parameters, controls for network effects and sex, and random assignment of students to introduction groups, which the authors say makes group-level confounding less likely (pp. 518, 531-532). The authors also note ego and alter effects may be confounded, so they estimate them jointly (p. 527).

**Scope.** One Dutch university, psychology freshmen in one cohort, predominantly female and highly educated, observed over about the first 4 months of university (p. 531). Generalization to other demographics is explicitly not established (p. 531).

## Key findings

- Networks became more cohesive over the first 3 months, then stabilized: average degree rose from 1.71 (Wave 1) to 2.64, 3.56, 3.67 and 3.64 (Wave 5); reciprocity .13 to .32; transitivity .33 to .45. (Table 1, p. 522; text pp. 521-522)
- Mean network effects were all significant: outdegree negative (selectivity), reciprocity positive, transitivity positive, GD-2 negative (signs per text; table minus signs lost in extraction). (Table 2 p. 524; text p. 524)
- Adding transitivity and GD-2 significantly improved fit in all 10 groups, supporting the actor-based network approach over a dyadic model. (p. 524)
- Friendship ties formed at Wave 2 persisted: 92% at Wave 3, 88% at Wave 4, 82% at Wave 5. (p. 525)
- Extraversion ego effect was the only significant predictor of nominating more friends (more extraverted students nominated more friends). (p. 525; Table 2)
- Agreeableness alter effect: higher agreeableness predicted being nominated as a friend; no other alter effect was significant. (p. 525; Table 2)
- Similarity effects: individuals selected friends similar in Openness, Extraversion and Agreeableness, the strongest effects in the model. (p. 526; Table 2)
- Similarity effects varied across groups for Openness, Extraversion and Agreeableness, but were significant in most groups: 7/10 (Openness), 8/10 (Extraversion), 10/10 (Agreeableness). (p. 526; Table 2 variance column)
- Sex: women nominated more friends (ego), and students selected same-sex friends (similarity). (p. 525; Table 2)
- No significant ego or alter effects for Openness, Conscientiousness or Neuroticism, and no Conscientiousness or Neuroticism similarity effect. (Table 2; pp. 525-526, 530)
- Mean levels of the Big Five did not change over the 4 months. (p. 522)
- Trait-specific similarity effects were larger than main effects, so matching on specific traits mattered more than absolute trait levels. Overall profile similarity was not modeled in this paper. (p. 530)

## Limitations

- Sample is highly educated and mostly female (82%) and Dutch (92%); forced same-gender and same-education choices may limit choice; authors note social homogamy may make the setting realistic (p. 531).
- Only 10 introduction groups selected by participation rate (more than 80% registration); personality data unavailable for nonparticipants, so group selection bias cannot be adjusted (p. 531).
- Nonparticipating group members rated themselves less neurotic (3.21 vs 3.62) and substantially less conscientious (3.92 vs 4.75); conscientiousness predicted attrition but not selection (pp. 516-517, 531).
- With 10 groups, between-group variance estimates are imprecise (pp. 531-532).
- Ties were dichotomized (friend or better vs not) because SIENA handles binary ties; a different cutoff could change results, though an alternative cutoff gave similar patterns (p. 532).
- Only about 4 months of data; no network information after Month 4, so later development is unknown (pp. 527-528).
- SIENA assumes ties are enduring states; the authors could only partly test this assumption over a short period (p. 518).
- Measures are single-item TIPI-r self-ratings, not full Big Five scales (p. 516).
- The abstract says first year of university, but the analyzed waves cover about 4 months.

## Gaps and flags

- No component has a pairwise similarity or compatibility term between the source (a lobbyist, or a recruiting member) and the target. Receptivity, seed_prob and edge_mult are all single-member multipliers, although the paper finds similarity effects about 10 times larger than individual effects. The system's own persona-homophily test is null, so this is a recorded gap, not a recommendation.
- The cosponsor forecaster has no reciprocation feature (did the sponsor previously cosponsor this member?) and no closure feature (shared cosponsor partners). The paper shows such structural terms improved fit in every group it studied.
- The homophily and co-movement tests are cross-sectional or pair-level and are not conditioned on network structure. No model jointly estimates selection and influence, so the cosponsor-stance null cannot separate 'no influence' from 'selection hides it'.
- Flag: The paper's 'reciprocity' is the reciprocation of friendship nominations. It must not be read as support for the money reciprocity in the competitiveness multiplier, which remains NOT validated (D-104..D-108).
- Flag: The pooled similarity estimates come from 10 groups with significant between-group variance (openness .31, extraversion .35, agreeableness .82). Quote them with strata (significant in 7/10, 8/10 and 10/10 groups) or mark them UNSTRATIFIED.
- Flag: No conflict with the two rejected SNA proposals. The paper dichotomises ties at 'friend or better' because SIENA requires binary ties. That is a method constraint, not an argument for dropping regular cosponsorship edges or for per-edge volume normalisation, and it must not be cited as one.
- Flag: The paper gives no basis for re-proposing persona-homophily edge weighting. The system's gate (|r| <= 0.015) stands. Any SIENA-style rerun is new agent analysis and stays UNVERIFIED (DE-062).
- Flag: Window and denominator: 205 students in 10 groups (out of 20 introduction groups), 5 monthly waves over about 4 months, single-item TIPI-r self-ratings, 82% women. The abstract's 'first year' overstates the window.

It is nearest to his position on the receptivity layer, that psychological traits should enter as edge parameters (Oropeza), and to his agency/communion node design. The paper broadly agrees that personality acts on ties, and mainly through pairwise similarity, not through one person's trait level. That is closer to Paul's edge framing than to the system's single-member seed_prob. His notes are stale in one respect: they do not record that the system tested persona homophily against cosponsor ties and found nothing (all |r| <= 0.015), so the edge idea is unearned for now. This paper does not change that verdict, because friendship among strangers is a weak proxy for legislative ties.
