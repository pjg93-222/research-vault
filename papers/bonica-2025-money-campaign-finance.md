> [!abstract] Summary
> Bonica and Grumbach link DIME itemized FEC contributions to the L2 voter file and to candidate birth years. They show that US campaign money given to congressional candidates is much older than the electorate. In the 2020 cycle, the median contribution dollar comes from a 66-year-old, the median donor is 59, and only 9% of dollars come from donors aged 40 or younger. For comparison, the median general-election voter is 52, the median primary voter is 59 and the median non-voter is 39. Within districts, each year of candidate age adds about 0.21 years to average donor age for non-incumbents (the main effect; the incumbent slope is about 0.10 because of a Candidate age x Incumbent interaction of -0.11; N = 6,098 observations of average donor age by candidate, 2012-2020). Within donors, using two-way fixed effects on 2020 donor-candidate pairs, a larger age gap lowers the chance of giving. The authors put a 30-year gap at about a 50% lower donating rate. The coefficient is -0.00328 for Democrats and -0.00317 for Republicans on a x100 scale. Pre-2020 Republican estimates are unstable because of WinRed coverage. Seed money, defined as the first 50 donations, is older and larger for older non-incumbent House candidates (N = 931). A voucher reweighting, which is a counterfactual and not an estimate, would raise funds for candidates under 66. The paper is descriptive and states that it identifies no causal effect of wealth or of policy, although its abstract and Sec. 5 use stronger causal wording than the design supports.
> 
> For Paul's systems, its main use is as a measurement warning, not as an engine parameter. Terrier's Sphere and Participation standings run on vendor fields for political giving and turnout. The paper shows both are steeply age-graded. Terrier stratifies standings by registration but not by age, so its top deciles will lean older. Mentat's identity ladder and conduit handling line up with the paper's own data hazards: linkage built to avoid false positives, and small-donor itemization that is only complete from 2020. The paper does not bear on the Moneyball ranking, the cascade engine or the SNA layers.

## Study

**Question:** How unequal is money in US politics across generations, and does this inequality affect political representation? (a) Age distribution of donors, voters, candidates and officeholders; (b) whether donors give more often and more per gift to candidates closer to their age; (c) whether seed (early) fundraising depends on candidate age; (d) how a voucher policy would change candidate fundraising by age.

**Theory:** Resource-based account of participation (Brady, Verba and Schlozman 1995) applied to age: generational wealth gaps (life-cycle and cohort effects, Gale et al. 2020) produce contribution gaps, which shape the candidate pipeline through campaign money as a selectorate (early seed money from personal networks; Bonica 2017, 2020) and access (Kalla and Broockman 2016; Austen-Smith 1995). Alternative explanations (public demand/taste-based discrimination, population demographics, electoral institutions; Sec. 1, pp. 2-3) are argued to be less likely drivers. Campaign finance is positioned as one important driver, not the sole one (p. 3).

**Data:** (1) FEC itemized individual contributions from DIME v4.0: over 600 million itemized donations 1979-2020, most 2016-2020; 23 million unique donor IDs; record linkage links 75% of individual contributors who gave in the 2012-2020 cycles, about 17 million donors (Sec. 3.2, p. 5). (2) L2 national voter file snapshot (April 2021): about 208 million registered voters, with date of birth, primary and general turnout history; census age estimates adjust for unregistered and non-citizen populations (Sec. 3.1, p. 4; fn. 8). (3) Candidate birth years for 11,741 congressional candidates 2012-2020 from congressional bios, Project Vote Smart, and L2 linkage; linkage correlation with Vote Smart r = 0.95 (Sec. 3.3, p. 5; fn. 11). Main donor analyses focus on the 2020 cycle because small-donor coverage (ActBlue, WinRed) is near-complete then (Sec. 3, p. 4). Unit and N by table: Table 1 average donor age by candidate, N = 6,098 (2012-2020, at least 10 donors per cycle); Tables 2-3 donor-candidate pairs in 2020: Democrats 2,048,581,125 pairs (Donated outcome) and 6,945,203 (asinh amount); Republicans 829,871,280 and 3,964,488; Table 4 non-incumbent House candidates in 2020, N = 931 (challengers and open seats); seed funding uses each candidate first 50 donations. Panel robustness (appendix) about 200 million same-state dyads, 2012-2020.

**Method:** Descriptive: age distributions for 2020 (box plots, medians and dollar-weighted means), generational shares of wealth vs donations (Fig. 6), and birth-cohort representational weight = cohort share of contributions over population share, scaled to 18-year-olds = 1 (Fig. 4). Candidate-level OLS (Eq. 1): average donor age (or dollar-weighted average) on candidate age x incumbency, with cycle and district fixed effects (Table 1). Donor-level linear probability model (Donated x100) and asinh(amount) (Eq. 2): |Age Gap| between donor and candidate, same-state control, donor and candidate two-way fixed effects, separately for Democrats and Republicans, with generation interactions (Tables 2-3; text p. 8-9). Seed-funding OLS with district fixed effects (Table 4). Counterfactual voucher simulation (Sec. 6.1, pp. 12-13): post-stratification reweighting of contributions so that strata (birth year, precinct partisanship in 10 groups, population density in 5 bins) match electoral shares, via inflation factor eta_s = electoral share / contribution share; gamma_c = voucher-program contributions / actual contributions per candidate; log(gamma_c) plotted by candidate age with loess (Fig. 8).

**Identification:** No exogenous policy shock. Within-district variation (Table 1, district FE) and within-donor / within-candidate variation (Eq. 2, two-way FE; donor and candidate fixed effects absorb time-invariant donor and candidate traits, incl. wealth and ideology). Authors state they do not estimate the wealth elasticity or the causal effect of wealth or of policy (pp. 1-2) and call exogenous policy variation future work (Sec. 7). Eq. 2 relies on the age gap varying across donor-candidate pairs within a donor; the text does not test time-varying dyad confounds. Voucher results are a counterfactual reweighting under stated assumptions, not an estimate.

**Scope:** US federal elections (House and Senate candidates and their individual donors), mainly the 2020 cycle; panel robustness 2012-2020 in appendix (same-state dyads only). Population: near-universe of voters (L2), donors (DIME-linked), and candidates. Era: 2012-2020 data, with 2020 as the core cycle. Institution: post-Citizens United, post-McCutcheon US campaign finance (individual limit $2,900 per election cited on p. 11). Not covered: PAC, party and presidential money (excluded in Fig. 5); dark money (explicitly not accounted for, Sec. 6, p. 11).

## Key findings

- Median dollar of campaign contributions comes from a 66-year-old; mean dollar from a 64-year-old (63.9); median unweighted donor is 59. Only 9% of contribution dollars come from donors aged 40 or younger. (Abstract p. 1; Sec. 4.1 p. 5) Numbers: median dollar 66; mean dollar 63.9; median donor 59; 9% of dollars from donors <=40
- Age gaps in participation: median voter 52, median non-voter 39, median primary voter 59. Average American age 38; voting-age public age 47. Candidates slightly younger than donors; incumbents slightly older. Those gaps are smaller than the gap between donors and general-election voters or non-voters. (Sec. 4.1 p. 5 (Fig. 3)) Numbers: voters 52; non-voters 39; primary voters 59; donors 59; US average 38; voting-age public 47
- Generational shares of donations track wealth shares. Baby Boomers give 53.6% of donation dollars and receive 48.5% of candidate receipts; Millennials give 5% of donation dollars and receive 10.4% of candidate receipts. Millennial candidates get over 66% of their dollars from Silent and Boomer donors plus about 20% from Gen X; Millennial donors supply 4% of Boomer candidates' receipts. (Sec. 5.1 p. 7 (Fig. 6); Sec. 4.1 p. 6 and Appendix A7 for wealth shares) Numbers: Boomer donors 53.6%; Millennial donors 5%; Millennial candidate receipts 10.4%; Millennial candidates 66%+ from Silent/Boomers; Gen X ~20%; Millennial share of Boomer-candidate money 4%; wealth shares Millennials 4.5%, Boomers 53.1%; contributions Millennials 5.3%, Boomers 52.8%
- Representational weight rises sharply with age. 35-year-olds are over 25 times better represented than 18-year-olds; the most represented cohorts (ages 70-80) give about 200-300 times more per capita than the youngest cohort. (Sec. 4.1 p. 6 (Fig. 4)) Numbers: 35-year-olds >25x 18-year-olds; ages 70-80 200-300x youngest cohort per capita
- Average contribution size rises with donor birth cohort. Donors born after 1985 give about $75 on average; donors born 1940-1965 about $200; pre-1935 cohorts about $300. Ever-donors to the $2,900 limit average 63 years old. (Sec. 5.1 / Fig. 5 p. 6 and Sec. 6 p. 11) Numbers: post-1985 ~$75; 1940-1965 ~$200; pre-1935 ~$300; limit-donors average age 63
- Candidate age is strongly associated with donor age within districts. Each year of candidate age raises average donor age by about 0.21 years (unweighted; main effect, with an incumbent interaction of -0.11 giving a slope near 0.10 for incumbents) and dollar-weighted donor age by about 0.20 years. Donor-age SD is 5.8 vs 12.5 for candidate age. Incumbents have donors about 8 years older on average (unweighted). (Sec. 5.2 p. 8, Table 1) Numbers: 0.21 (SE 0.01); 0.20 (SE 0.01); N = 6,098 (average donor age by candidate); incumbent +8.16 years; SD donor age 5.8, candidate age 12.5
- Within-donor, two-way fixed-effects estimates: donors are less likely to give, and give less, the larger the age gap with a candidate. Paper reports that a 30-year age gap is associated with about a 50% reduction in the likelihood of donating (baseline 0.189 for Democrats, 0.184 for Republicans, x100 scale). Effects are similar across parties on the extensive margin. (Sec. 5.3 p. 9; Tables 2-3 p. 10-11) Numbers: Democrats extensive |Age Gap| coef -0.00328 (SE 0.00001); Republicans -0.00317 (SE 0.00003); intensive asinh(amount) Democrats -0.00178 (SE 0.00005), Republicans -0.00218 (SE 0.00008); baseline donating rate 0.189 (D) and 0.184 (R); 30-year gap ~50% lower donation likelihood (authors' calculation)
- Generation interactions: the age-gap penalty on donating is largest for the oldest and youngest donors (Silent generation -0.00502 for Democrats). For Gen Z, a larger age gap strongly lowers donating but conditional on donating, Gen Z donors give slightly more to more distant-age candidates (Democrats +0.00132; Republicans +0.00335 on the intensive margin, which the text describes as the same pattern). (Sec. 5.3 p. 9 and fn. 14 p. 9; Tables 2-3) Numbers: Silent x age gap -0.00502 (D); Gen Z x age gap -0.00332 extensive (D); Gen Z intensive +0.00132 (D), +0.00335 (R)
- Effect is more pronounced for Democrats, where donor age varies more and ideology is more age-polarized (abstract and Sec. 5.3). Republican coefficients are unexpectedly positive in cycles before 2020, which the authors attribute to lower Republican donor-age variance and missing small donors before WinRed. (Intro p. 2; Sec. 5.3 p. 9; fn. 3 p. 2; fn. 3 text pp. 2-3) Numbers: Pre-2020 Republican age-gap coefficients positive; Democratic coefficients similar 2014-2018 and smaller in 2012
- Seed funding (first 50 donations) is older-donor heavy for older candidates. Within district, a 65-year-old's seed donors are 8.4 years older than a 35-year-old's. Each year of candidate age adds about $6.91 to average seed-donor amount (a 65- vs 35-year-old raises about $207 more per early donor). Older candidates self-fund more, about 6% more per year of age. (Sec. 5.4 pp. 9-11, Table 4 p. 11) Numbers: seed donor age coef 0.28 (SE 0.02) per year; $6.91 (SE 1.67) per year; ln(self-funding) 0.06 (SE 0.01); N = 931; R2 0.58, 0.46, 0.43; 8.4-year and $207 differences for 35 vs 65
- Voucher simulation (4 x $25-type credits in Seattle comparison; simulated full participation matching voter turnout): the average candidate under age 30 would raise over three times more funds; candidates over 66 raise less on average, down to about one-third fewer for the average candidate over 80. Threshold age is 66. Intro states the average candidate under 50 would get about twice the share of contributions and candidates below 66 benefit on average. (Intro p. 2; Sec. 6.1 pp. 12-13 (Fig. 8)) Numbers: threshold age 66; under 30 >3x; over 80 about 1/3 fewer; under 50 ~2x share of contributions; example gamma AOC (b. 1989) 2.05; Barbara Lee (b. 1946) and Jamie Raskin (b. 1962) below 0.5
- Underrepresented young subgroup in the simulation: older Millennials (b. 1985-1989) in Democratic-leaning, high-density districts are 1.45% of the 2020 electorate but only 0.96% of contributions; the simulation raises their share to 1.45%. (Sec. 6.1 p. 12) Numbers: 1.45% electorate vs 0.96% of contributions
- Authors reject taste-based age discrimination by voters as the main driver: conjoint studies show public favors younger candidates, over 70% back maximum age limits, and constituent approval is negatively associated with age net of ideology. Party age distributions are close (House Democrats avg 59, Republicans 57). US legislators are the oldest in the OECD, while the correlation between population median age and legislator age across countries is -0.003. (Sec. 1 pp. 2-3; Fig. 1 p. 2 caption; intro p. 1) Numbers: correlation -0.003; over 70% support age limits; Congress avg age 58 House, 64 Senate (2022); Democrats 59, Republicans 57
- Donor-candidate linkage is near-representative: linked and unlinked donors show similar retired/student shares; unlinked donors have slightly more conservative CFscores and larger average donations, which the authors do not treat as material bias. (Sec. 3.2 p. 5; App. Table A4) Numbers: 75% linked; 17 million linked donors (2012-2020); the r = 0.95 validation is for the candidate birth-year linkage, not the donor linkage
- Abstract claims older donors are much more ideologically conservative than younger donors, and that donor-partisanship age gaps exceed those in voting and survey attitudes. The text body does not give the supporting numbers (they are in appendix figures not extracted). (Highlights p. 1; abstract/intro p. 1-2; Sec. 4 (Appendix A6)) Numbers: not in extracted main text

## Limitations

- Paper states it does not directly estimate wealth elasticities or causal effects of wealth or contributions; cited causal evaluation of policy left to future work with exogenous changes (pp. 1-2; Sec. 7).
- Small-donor coverage: data before 2020 exclude most small donors (ActBlue and WinRed itemization; Sec. 3 p. 4; fn. 3; Sec. 5.3 p. 9). Pre-2020 Republican coefficients are unexpectedly positive and the party comparison is only clean for 2020.
- Dark money (Citizens United-era independent groups) is not accounted for (Sec. 6 p. 11).
- Extensive-margin models use donor fixed effects, so non-donors drop out of that estimate (Sec. 5.3 p. 8). Panel robustness (2012-2020) restricted to same-state dyads (about 200 million observations).
- Record linkage links 75% of donors; misses occur for different addresses, unregistered donors and name changes. Linkage algorithm designed to limit false positives, which can bias the linked sample (Sec. 3.2 pp. 4-5).
- Voucher simulation assumes full participation with donor shares matched to voter turnout, all candidates adopt vouchers and cannot take private money, and simple assumptions about spending and candidate pipelines (intro p. 2; Sec. 6.1 pp. 11-13; fn. 16). Informational costs of vouchers may reduce young participation (Sec. 6 p. 11). Contribution-limit age effects are described as less certain (Sec. 6 p. 11).
- Seed-funding model uses only non-incumbent House candidates in 2020 (N = 931) with district fixed effects and no candidate fixed effects (Sec. 5.4 p. 10).
- Authors cannot rule out alternative theories of contribution behavior, e.g., political efficacy and inclusion (fn. 2 p. 2). Online small-dollar strategies by younger candidates are an alternative explanation for seed-funding gaps (fn. 15 p. 11).
- Cohort vs life-cycle effects are not separately identified in the main analysis; text says generational patterns are interpreted with both (Sec. 1, Sec. 7).
- Data available only on request (Data availability statement). Appendix figures and tables (A2-A7) not in the extracted text, so appendix-only claims were not verified.
- Reference-list mismatch: text cites (Li, 2022) for online scams, but the reference list has Zhao, L., 2022 (Lemons in the political marketplace). Treat that citation as unverified.

## Gaps and flags

- Terrier has no age-stratified or age-residualized standing for Sphere, Participation or Overall. Only a within-registration standing exists, at engine.py:564-571. The paper shows political giving and turnout are steeply age-graded, so top-decile lists will concentrate older residents for reasons unrelated to CU alignment. Adding an age-stratified display beside the registration one would expose this. Any such change is Paul's call.
- No Mentat surface reports a coverage break for employee or individual itemized giving before and after the start of WinRed (2019-2020). Any multi-cycle employee-giving or partisan-share series that crosses 2020 could show a reporting jump that reads as behaviour.
- Electoral plays (fec/engine/build_electoral_plays.py:30-33) score a contest-primary route only from same-party stance gaps. They have no term for challenger viability. The paper shows viability depends on early seed money, which depends on candidate age and personal networks (N = 931, 2020, non-incumbent House candidates only). This is a possible extension, not a defect, and the evidence is descriptive.
- Mentat's identity ladder records no recall estimate. The paper reports only slight, unimportant differences between linked and unlinked donors (unlinked donors give slightly larger gifts), so it gives no basis for a dollar bias. A hand-labelled recall sample would size Mentat's own recall.
- Citation mismatch: Paul's bibliography gives DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4892936, which is the SSRN preprint. The published article's DOI, printed in the PDF, is 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2025.105451. Cite the journal DOI.
- The component map lists Bonica & Grumbach 2025 under melange-influence-engine.electoral-plays. This paper says nothing about donors targeting competitive races; that finding is Bonica 2013. Do not cite it as support for electoral plays or for the competitiveness multiplier (D-104..D-108; reciprocity is NOT validated).
- Pooled-coefficient rule: quote the age-gap effect by party and for 2020 only. The coefficients are D -0.00328 and R -0.00317 on the extensive margin, x100 scale, in 2020 donor-candidate pairs. Pre-2020 Republican coefficients change sign, and the generation interactions differ by stratum (Silent -0.00502 D; Gen Z positive on the intensive margin). Any cross-cycle or cross-generation pooled figure must be labelled UNSTRATIFIED.
- The '30-year gap means about 50% lower donating' figure is the authors' own calculation from a two-way fixed-effects linear probability model. It is descriptive and within-donor, not causal (pp. 1-2, Sec. 7). Do not restate it as a causal or access-probability effect (D-025 spirit).
- Every figure from this paper should carry its window and denominator. Donor-age medians are 2020 federal itemized individual contributions only, excluding PAC, party and presidential money (Fig. 5). Representational weights are per capita relative to 18-year-olds.
- Appendix-only claims are unverified here. These include donor ideology by age (A6), wealth shares (A7) and panel robustness (A4-A6).
- No rejected SNA proposal is argued here, and LDA is not used. No D-021 or SNA-rejection conflict.

Neither annotated-bibliography file has Paul-authored takeaways for this paper. Both are bare citation lists, and none of his 63 highlights has a note. His highlights stress the older, wealthier donor base, the seed-money and network selectorate, and the claim that age proximity drives giving. They are consistent with the component map's summary that donors are old and wealthy, which is attached to Terrier's Sphere. On Sphere, the evidence agrees with Paul's reading. It adds a consequence his notes do not draw: a giving-heavy Sphere, combined with Participation, will rank older residents higher. His Sphere ruling (45/45/10, 'leaning') rests on volunteers engaging members, and this paper neither supports nor tests that. The map's link from this paper to electoral plays and competitive-race targeting is stale or misattributed. That finding is Bonica 2013. Nothing in this paper bears on the Moneyball engine as built.
