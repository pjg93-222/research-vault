# Influence maximization in unknown social networks: Learning Policies for Effective Graph Sampling

**Harshavardhan Kamarthi, Priyesh Vijayan, Bryan Wilder, Balaraman Ravindran, Milind Tambe (2020).** *AAMAS 2020 (19th International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems, Auckland); arXiv:1907.11625v5.* [arXiv:1907.11625](https://doi.org/arXiv:1907.11625). Theme: [[themes/adaptive-influence-maximization|Adaptive Influence Maximization]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Kamarthi, Vijayan, Wilder, Ravindran and Tambe (arXiv 2019, published AAMAS 2020) study influence maximization when the network itself is unknown and must be discovered by querying nodes. Starting from 5 random nodes, an agent gets 5 queries (each reveals a node's neighbours); greedy influence maximization then picks 10 seeds on the discovered subgraph, scored by independent-cascade spread (p = 0.1, 100 runs) on the full graph. Geometric-DQN, a deep Q-network built on a GCN plus DiffPool graph embedding and DeepWalk action embeddings, learns the query policy. Policies are trained and tested per family (Indian villages, voles, retweets, homeless youth): each sees only its own family's 2 or 3 training networks plus synthetic graphs, and is tested on that family's held-out networks (13 test networks in all). Against the CHANGE friendship-paradox baseline, the best variant per family improves by 7.91 to 26.6 percent of the gap between CHANGE and greedy-optimal (Table 4). The text quotes this range as 7-23 in some places and 7-26 in others. Reward scaling makes training on several graphs at once possible, and synthetic SBM graphs help. Inspecting the learned policy gives a simple heuristic, H2 (query the lowest-degree node among those just discovered), which beats CHANGE on voles3, voles4, damascus and israel. On the Homeless networks its gaps are small (b1 19.4 vs 19.1; spy2 16.4 vs 16.01), and it loses on rural3 (17.3 vs 17.4). For Paul's systems, the main point is a boundary condition. The system treats the cosponsor graph as fully observed (a modelling assumption, since the code treats all listed edges as observed). The recruitment and frontier code runs classic greedy on it, and the recruitment template comment says no ML is needed at this scale. The paper's learned policy only pays when edges are hidden. The premise would matter for the staffer graph only if that graph is incomplete, and then only as a way to decide which directories or people to research next. It does not justify any reading of access or influence. The reported numbers pick the best variant per test set and give no confidence intervals.

>
> The unknown-network case, formulated as an MDP and solved with a Geometric-DQN architecture (DiffPool for global state representation, DeepWalk for local action embeddings) trained by deep Q-learning. Directly analogous to the Melange situation where the true relationship/persuasion costs are not known a priori and must be revealed by probing — the agent learns *which nodes to query* to discover the network efficiently before seeding. Reports a 7–13% improvement over the best hand-crafted sampling heuristic.

## Study

**Question:** Can deep reinforcement learning learn a network-discovery (node-querying) policy, under a budget of 5 queries from 5 random seeds, such that the 10 seeds chosen by greedy influence maximization on the discovered subgraph spread further in the full unknown network than the CHANGE sampling heuristic?

**Theory:** Independent cascade diffusion (Kempe et al. 2003, 2005) and greedy influence maximization; friendship paradox (Feld 1991) motivates CHANGE; community structure motivates stochastic block models; MDP and POMDP framing for multi-graph training; deep Q-learning with graph representation learning.

**Data:** Four network families, each split into train and test networks (Table 1). Rural (microfinance diffusion in Indian villages, Banerjee et al. 2013): train rural1, rural2; test rural3, rural4. Animal (vole wildlife contact networks, Davis et al. 2015): train voles1, voles2; test voles3, voles4. Retweet (Twitter retweet networks, Network Data Repository, Rossi and Ahmed 2015): train copen, occupy; test israel (spelled isreal in Table 1), damascus. Homeless (HIV intervention campaigns for homeless youth in Los Angeles, Wilder et al. 2018): train a1, spy, mfp; test b1, cg1, node4, mfp2, mfp3, spy2, spy3. Totals: 9 training networks, 13 test networks. Unit is the network (graph); node counts, edge counts and collection periods are not reported in the text. Synthetic graphs: 5 SBM (or SSM for retweets) graphs per training graph, with Louvain communities. No individual-level sample size is given.

**Method:** Markov decision process over the discovered subgraph: state is the discovered graph, action is querying an unqueried node, reward is the influence of the final seed set in the full graph at episode end (scaled by Eq. 1 using CHANGE and OPT) plus small per-step rewards for new nodes (Eq. 2). The Geometric-DQN network takes a global graph embedding (GCN layers pooled with DiffPool) plus per-node action embeddings (DeepWalk, found vital for stable training; GCN action features were unstable) and outputs a Q-value per candidate node. Trained with epsilon-greedy DQN, prioritized experience replay and a target network, on a random graph per episode from the training set, with synthetic graphs used on half the episodes. Deployed with frozen weights. Influence oracle O: greedy influence maximization on the discovered graph; OPT is greedy influence maximization on the full graph (the ceiling). Diffusion: independent cascade model, p = 0.1, budget 10 activated nodes, T = 5 queries. Baselines: CHANGE (query a random neighbour of each random seed; chosen over random greedy, snowball and recommend, which were 3.9, 42.1 and 41.7 percent worse). Two hand-designed heuristics H1 (query minimum-degree node) and H2 (minimum-degree node among those discovered in the last step) were used for insight analysis.

**Identification:** Not a causal design. The comparison is algorithmic: policies trained on train networks are evaluated on held-out test networks of the same family, with the same budget, diffusion model and oracle as the baseline. Scores are averages over 100 diffusion simulations. No statistical tests, confidence intervals or seeds-variance reporting appear in the text.

**Scope:** Social and interaction networks from four domains (rural households, wild voles, Twitter retweets, homeless youth contact networks). Transfer is tested within family only (train on one family's networks, test on that family's held-out networks). Dates of the datasets are not stated. Authors from IIT Madras, McGill University and Mila, and Harvard CRCS. No live field deployment is reported; the paper argues the setting resembles real deployment.

## Key findings

- Best Geometric-DQN variant beats CHANGE on all 13 reported test networks (e.g. voles4 80.2 vs 58.9; damascus 119.3 vs 95.24; israel 43.6 vs 30.6; voles3 45.8 vs 33.7; rural4 35.7 vs 31.5). Note the variant is the best one per test network as reported. (Section 5 Results, Table 2) — Table 2 raw influence scores; OPT for voles4 115.7 and damascus 195.4
- Average improve percent for the best variant per family is Rural 23.76, Animals 26.6, Retweet 19.7, Homeless 7.91. The Results text says 7-26 percent improvement, while the abstract, contributions list and conclusion say 7-23 percent: an internal inconsistency. (Section 5 Results, Table 4; abstract; Section 1 contribution (4); Section 7 conclusion) — Table 4: 23.76, 26.6, 19.7, 7.91
- Text claims training on multiple networks beats the average of single-network models on all but one family. Reading Table 3 as extracted, multiple-network training is lower on rural3 (10.26 vs 13.14 individual) and spy2 (20.76 vs 21.40), so the exceptions appear to be two. The column alignment comes from text extraction and should be checked against the PDF. (Section 5, Table 3) — Individual averages 13.14 and 21.40 vs multiple 10.26 and 20.76 on rural3 and spy2; e.g. b1 4.76 vs 14.29; mfp3 7.18 vs 18.10
- Models trained only on synthetic SBM or SSM graphs score higher on average than the average of models trained on individual real networks. For Homeless networks, synthetic-only gives the highest average score. Adding synthetic graphs to real training data improved Homeless, Retweet and Rural results versus real-only training. (Section 5, Figure 4) — No numeric values in the text; results are shown only in Figure 4
- Heuristic H2 (minimum-degree node among those discovered in the last step) beats CHANGE on Animals, Retweet and some Homeless networks: voles4 72.8 vs 58.9; damascus 104.9 vs 95.2; israel 34.2 vs 30.6; b1 19.4 vs 19.1; spy2 16.4 vs 16.01. H1 is comparable to CHANGE. The text says H2 wins by huge margins on Homeless, but the Homeless gaps in Table 5 are small. (Section 6 Insights, Table 5) — Table 5 values quoted in finding; CHANGE, H1, H2, Geometric-DQN per graph
- Geometric-DQN policies almost always discover larger graphs than CHANGE, but across Geometric-DQN policies there is no correlation between influence score and the size of the discovered graph. (Section 6 Insights, Size of discovered graph) — None reported
- Two recurring behaviours in deployment: O1, the next node selected has minimum degree; O2, the node selected is among the most recently discovered. O2 almost always co-occurs with O1. (Section 6 Insights, Observations on node selection; Figure 5 toy example) — None reported
- Geometric-DQN queries nodes with higher true degree and true betweenness centrality than CHANGE on b1 and israel, especially in the last query. H2 raises betweenness over CHANGE but not degree. (Section 6, Figures 6 and 7) — Values appear only in figures; not extracted
- Among four sampling baselines, Random greedy, Snowball and Recommend are 3.9, 42.1 and 41.7 percent worse than CHANGE, which is why CHANGE was the only baseline kept. The metric behind these percentages is not defined in the text. (Section 4, Sampling baseline) — 3.9, 42.1, 41.7 percent
- Step rewards are about two orders of magnitude smaller than the final influence reward. The authors say they are essential for initial stable learning and suspect their role fades after early training. (Section 3.1, Step-rewards) — About 100x smaller (approximate, as stated)
- Using DeepWalk embeddings as action inputs was vital for stable training; using GCN node features for actions gave unstable training because the action space is non-stationary across graphs. Neighbourhood DeepWalk was also cheaper than structural encodings. (Section 3.2, Local action representation) — None reported
- Scaling the reward relative to CHANGE and OPT lets training pool graphs of different sizes and densities into one replay buffer; the paper treats this as the key enabler of multi-network training. (Section 3.1, Rewards when using multiple graphs (Eq. 1)) — None reported

## Limitations

- OPT is approximated by greedy influence maximization because exact optimization is NP-hard, and the reward depends on CHANGE as the normalizing baseline (Eq. 1).
- Table 2 and Table 4 report the best-performing Geometric-DQN variant per test network or test set. The text does not say how the variant was chosen, so these figures may be optimistic.
- Environment parameters are fixed (ICM p = 0.1, budget 10, 5 seeds, T = 5 queries); sensitivity to these settings is not reported.
- Multi-graph training turns the problem into a POMDP rather than an MDP, which the authors handle by reward scaling without a formal guarantee.
- The authors say there is no correlation between performance and discovered graph size, and state that a detailed investigation of the learned patterns is left for future work.
- Synthetic-graph results are reported only as a figure with no numbers in the text.
- Homeless family is small (3 training and 7 test networks) and the paper gives no node counts or collection periods for any network.
- Only the independent cascade model is evaluated; no other diffusion models and no live field deployment are tested.
- Text inconsistency: 7-26 percent (Results) vs 7-23 percent (abstract, contributions, conclusion). The paper has no explicit limitations section.

> [!failure]- Claims that did not survive verification
> - Summary issue: Training scope is wrong. The summary says each policy is trained on 9 networks pooled across four families. The paper trains and tests per family (Section 4: train and deploy for each network family; Table 1). Each policy sees only its family's 2 or 3 training networks plus synthetic graphs, and is tested on that family's held-out networks.

> - Summary issue: The summary says H2 beats CHANGE on several test networks. That holds for voles3, voles4, damascus and israel. On the Homeless networks the gaps are small (b1 19.4 vs 19.1; spy2 16.4 vs 16.01), and H2 loses on rural3 (17.3 vs 17.4). The summary should not imply broad wins.
> - Summary issue: The 7.91 to 26.6 range and the 7-23 vs 7-26 inconsistency are reported correctly in the summary and need no change.

## Gaps and flags

- The cosponsor graph is treated as complete. No component models unobserved ties, or asks how far greedy recruitment and frontier results would move if part of the true contact network is missing from cosponsorship.
- The staffer router treats an incomplete route search as no evidence of no route, and it has no budgeted policy for which directories or people to resolve next, and no metric for graph completeness after each acquisition round.
- The system has not tested whether a cheap structural heuristic (for example H2-style low-degree frontier choice, or degree-ranked seeds) matches lazy greedy on its own kernel P. Such a comparison would show how much the MC-20 greedy machinery buys.
- Reported gains are optimistic. Tables 2 and 4 pick the best Geometric-DQN variant per test network or family, without saying how; no confidence intervals or seed-variance figures appear; and the range is given as 7-23 percent in the abstract and conclusion but 7-26 percent in Results. Quote it only as 7.91 to 26.6 percent of the CHANGE-to-OPT gap, best variant per family, 13 test networks in 4 families, IC p = 0.1, budget 10, T = 5 queries.
- Pooled per-family averages (Table 4) span different network sizes. Quote them with the family label, never as one pooled figure.
- Influence in this paper means simulated cascade spread. Carried over to the staffer graph, it must not become closeness, access or introduction probability (D-025), and influence language is barred on revolving-door edges.
- The paper does not argue for either rejected SNA proposal (dropping regular cosponsorship edges; per-edge volume normalization). It makes no use of LDA, and it makes no reciprocity claim.
- Metadata: the item file says 2019 (arXiv posting) while Paul's entry says AAMAS 2020. Both refer to the same work (arXiv:1907.11625v5).

Paul's entry is a bare citation (Kamarthi et al. 2020, AAMAS Geometric-DQN, unknown networks) with no takeaways or system links, so there is nothing for the evidence to contradict. The note is not stale. The system as built has not adopted learned discovery policies: recruitment and the frontier run greedy on a cosponsor graph treated as fully observed (an assumption), and the recruitment template comment says no ML is needed. That fits the paper's own logic, where learning only pays when the graph is hidden. The open use is staffer-graph data acquisition, if that graph is incomplete, framed as data acquisition rather than influence.
