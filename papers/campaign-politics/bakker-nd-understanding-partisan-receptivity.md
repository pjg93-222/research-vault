# Partisan Cue Receptivity: Bounded Rationality vs Expressive Utility

**Bert N. Bakker (University of Amsterdam), Yphtach Lelkes (University of Pennsylvania), Ariel Malka (Yeshiva University) (2020).** *The Journal of Politics, vol. 82, no. 3 (July 2020), pp. 1061-1077. Published online May 11, 2020.*. [https://doi.org/10.1086/707616](https://doi.org/10.1086/707616). Theme: [[themes/campaign-politics|Campaign Politics]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Bakker, Lelkes and Malka ran four US survey experiments, plus two ancillary ones and a pooled individual-data meta-analysis, on partisans in online panels from 2016 to 2018. The issues (food irradiation and farm subsidies) were chosen because they were not yet partisan. The paper tests two accounts of why people follow party cues. Bounded rationality says low-resource citizens use cues to save effort. Expressive utility says strong identifiers with high cognitive resources use cues to protect their party identity.
> 
> Bounded rationality gets no support. Cue effects are no larger among low-CRT, low-NfC or low-knowledge respondents, and most interaction signs run the other way. Expressive utility gets partial support. In the meta-analysis, among high-resource respondents the in-party cue effect rises with partisan identity strength (slope .15 for NfC and latent resources), and for latent resources only the rise is about 15 points from the weakest to the strongest identifiers. Only two of six three-way terms are significant, and the MTurk replication shows nothing.
> 
> The finding that matters most for Melange is the main-effect asymmetry. The out-party cue reduces support by .06 to .07 on a 0-1 scale, a decrease relative to the no-cue control (a p-value is reported only for study 1, p < .01). The in-party cue adds only about +.03 and is non-significant in studies 1 and 2. The engine cites this paper (paired with Furnas) for `cpf_pressure`, which scores co-partisan pressure at x1.7 and out-partisan pressure at a positive x0.5. The paper shows something different: out-party cues lower support, and in-party cues have a weak effect.
> 
> The subjects are members of the public reading one-shot survey vignettes, not legislators pressing other legislators. The evidence is therefore a direction-of-sign check for the engine, not a source for its constants.

## Study

**Question.** Why do citizens follow partisan cues when forming policy judgments? Is cue receptivity strongest among people with low cognitive resources (bounded rationality, cue-taking as effort-saving), or among people with both strong partisan social identity and high cognitive resources (expressive utility, effortful identity-protective reasoning)?

**Theory.** Bounded rationality (Simon 1972; Kam 2005; Lupia and McCubbins 1998): cues are heuristics that let low-resource citizens adopt value-consistent positions without costly effort, so cue receptivity should be highest among those low in cognitive resources. Expressive utility (Kahan 2013; Petersen et al. 2013; Lodge and Taber 2013; Groenendyk 2013): cue-following is identity-protective motivated reasoning, so the strongest cue receptivity should appear among people with strong partisan social identity who also have the resources to rationalize the party position. The authors treat the strong-identity-by-high-resource interaction as the key distinctive prediction of expressive utility (p. 1063).

**Data.** Study 1 (food irradiation): Survey Sampling International panel, July 2016; 883 respondents, 747 analyzed (136 excluded for no party ID or leaning). Study 2 (farm subsidy): Qualtrics panel, June 2017; 1,302 partisans in text (647 Democrats, 655 Republicans), but Table 3 reports N = 1,258; discrepancy not explained in main text. Study 3 (replication of studies 1 and 2): Bovitz Forthright sample, October 2018, N = 1,911 (appendix C). Study 4 (replication of food irradiation): Amazon MTurk, October 2018, N = 2,509 (appendix D). Individual-data meta-analysis of studies 1-4 (Table 4): 8,116 observations in CRT models, 5,607 in NfC and latent-resource models. Ancillary: US trade policy experiment (July 2016) and Danish ideology experiment (August 2017), both without a no-cues control (appendices H and I). Unit: individual respondent who identifies with or leans toward a party (the Danish study uses ideological identity). Outcome: policy support on a 0-1 scale (study 2 uses a seven-point item rescaled 0-1). Fieldwork 2016-2018.

**Method.** OLS regression of policy support on in-party and out-party cue dummies (control is omitted category), partisan social identity strength (PSID), one cognitive resource measure, and covariates (gender, age, education, race, party). The bounded rationality test uses cue-by-resource and cue-by-PSID two-way interactions. The expressive utility test uses cue x PSID x resource three-way interactions, plotted as marginal effects at -1 SD, mean, and +1 SD of PSID and resources. Individual-data meta-analysis (studies 1-4, and separately seven experiments for the in-party vs out-party contrast) uses mixed-effects regression with dummies for studies and random intercepts for study and individual. Studies 3 and 4 also measured depth of processing (reading time, thought-listing word count, factual quiz) to test the proposed mechanism.

**Identification.** Random assignment to cue condition (in-party cue, out-party cue, or control; ancillary studies compare in-party vs out-party only) identifies the cue main effects. Cognitive resources and PSID are pretreatment measures, not manipulated, so the moderation tests are observational interactions. The authors state they cannot identify causal effects of cognitive resources on cue-following (p. 1075). The key tests are three-way interactions, which are individually often nonsignificant. Analyses are OLS with covariates; replication files also run models without covariates (fn. 9).

**Scope.** US partisans (identifying with or leaning toward a major party) in four online panels (SSI, Qualtrics, Bovitz Forthright, MTurk) fielded 2016-2018. Issues were chosen to be not yet strongly partisan (food irradiation ban; farm subsidies), so findings are about cue-following on newly politicized issues (pp. 1073-1074). Panels are non-probability and the paper does not report their representativeness in the main text. Ancillary Denmark study covers ideology rather than party. Institutional scope: one-shot embedded survey experiments, not repeated elite messaging or real information environments (p. 1076).

## Key findings

- No support for the bounded rationality prediction. Cue effects are not stronger among low cognitive resource respondents, and in several cases the interaction points the opposite way. Numbers: Study 1: none of six cue x resource interactions significant; in 5 of 6 the sign is opposite to the BR prediction. Study 2: all eight cue x resource interactions opposite to BR; seven nonsignificant, one significant (political knowledge x out-party cue).. (Abstract p. 1061; study 1 p. 1065; study 2 p. 1066; meta-analysis p. 1070)
- Study 1 main effects: out-party cue reduces support; in-party cue effect not significant. Numbers: Out-party cue -0.06 points on 0-1 scale (p < .01); in-party cue +0.03 (not significant). N = 747.. (Study 1, p. 1065)
- Study 1, expressive utility (NfC): among high need-for-cognition respondents, the in-party cue effect grows with partisan identity strength; the three-way term is significant for in-party cue only. CRT and latent-resource three-way terms are not significant. Numbers: In-party x NfC x PSID b = 1.08 (SE .49, p = .027). Simple slope of in-party cue on PSID among high NfC b = .26 (SE .11, p = .019). Out-party analogue b = -.14 (SE .11), not significant. Out-party three-way b = -.53 (SE .51).. (Study 1, p. 1065-1066; Table 2 model 4; Figure 1 middle row)
- Study 2, expressive utility: out-party cue effect is larger for strongly identified partisans among high-CRT respondents; political knowledge gives a similar out-party pattern; NfC and latent-resource terms point the expected way but are not significant. Numbers: CRT: out-party x PSID x CRT b = -.78 (SE .34, p = .02); in-party three-way b = .29 (SE .37), not significant. Among high-CRT respondents, strong identifiers were more influenced by out-party cues than weak identifiers, b = -.27 (SE .12, p = .02); no parallel for in-party cue. Political knowledge: out-party three-way b = -.68 (SE .28, p = .02); in-party b = .54 (SE .28, p = .05, nearly significant).. (Study 2, pp. 1066-1068; Table 3 models 2, 6, 4, 8; Figure 2)
- Study 2 main effects: out-party cue reduces support; in-party cue has no effect. Numbers: Out-party cue -0.07 on 0-1 scale; in-party cue no significant influence. N = 1,302 in text (Table 3 note reports N = 1,258).. (Study 2, p. 1066)
- Replications (study 3, Bovitz Forthright, and study 4, MTurk) do not support the expressive utility or bounded rationality model in the same way as studies 1 and 2. Study 3 shows again no BR support and mixed EU support; study 4 shows no support for either model. Numbers: Study 3 N = 1,911; study 4 N = 2,509 (Table 1). Study 4 showed no trace of the patterns in other studies; its CRT means were significantly higher than study 3 (app. D.5).. (Pp. 1069; appendices C.1-C.2 (study 3) and D.1-D.5 (study 4); discussion pp. 1073-1074; fn. 18 p. 1076)
- Proposed mechanism not supported: strongly identified, high-resource partisans did not process policy information more deeply on reading time, thought listing, or factual quiz measures. Numbers: Null results across all depth-of-processing measures in studies 3 and 4 (the paper reports no coefficients in the main text). Exploratory reading-time ranking (after Petersen et al. 2013) also found no evidence (fn. 11).. (Pp. 1069-1070 (preregistered prediction); appendices C.3-C.5 (study 3) and D.3 (study 4); discussion pp. 1073-1074)
- Meta-analysis of studies 1-4: out-party cue lowers support, in-party cue raises it, and cue effects are not larger among low-resource respondents. Three-way terms are in the expressive utility direction, but only two reach significance. Numbers: Out-party cue -0.07 points; in-party cue +0.03 points (app. E.2). In-party x PSID x NfC b = .45 (SE .17); in-party x PSID x latent resources b = .50 (SE .20); in-party x PSID x CRT b = .09 (SE .10), not significant. Out-party three-way terms negative but not significant (CRT -.11, NfC -.10, latent -.17).. (Meta-analysis pp. 1070-1071; Table 4 (p. 1072))
- Meta-analysis slopes among high-resource respondents: the in-party cue effect increases with partisan identity strength. Substantively, for latent resources the effect rises about 15 percentage points from lowest to highest PSID among high-resource respondents. Numbers: Among high resources, in-party cue x PSID slope: CRT b = .09 (SE .04, p < .05); NfC b = .15 (SE .05, p < .01); latent b = .15 (SE .05, p < .01). Out-party x PSID slope among high CRT b = -.09 (SE .05, p < .05).. (Meta-analysis p. 1070-1071 and Figure 3)
- Ancillary in-versus-out-party meta-analysis (seven experiments, including trade policy and Danish ideology): no evidence cue receptivity is stronger among low-resource people; the in-party vs out-party x resource x PSID interactions are all positive; NfC and latent-resource terms are significant, CRT is not. Numbers: NfC three-way b = .55 (SE .16); latent resources b = .56 (SE .17); CRT b = .11 (SE .08), not significant. Study 3 food and study 4 MTurk coefficients listed in Table 4 are the study-level dummies.. (Pp. 1071 and 1072-1073 (Table 4 continued); appendix E.3 for full analysis; apps. H and I for ancillary studies)
- Issue dependence: in-party cue patterns are stronger in the food irradiation (change) studies, out-party cue patterns are stronger in the farm subsidy (status quo) studies. The authors offer this as a speculative explanation. Numbers: Qualitative; no coefficients given in the text for this contrast beyond the reported study results.. (Pp. 1073 (discussion of in-party vs out-party))
- Overall conclusion: the evidence is inconsistent with bounded rationality and only partially consistent with expressive utility, so identity-expressive motives more often account for cue-following than compensation for low cognitive resources. Numbers: Authors state the three-way interactions usually did not reach significance individually; in the second meta-analysis, three-way interactions were significant in line with EU in two of three cases (p. 1075).. (Discussion pp. 1073-1075; closing paragraph p. 1076)

## Limitations

- Experimental stimuli cover only a small fraction of political messages; variation in issue type, framing and wording may change both the extent and motives of cue-following (p. 1074). Salient, politicized issues may mute effects (p. 1074).
- Results depend on the cognitive resource measure: NfC and latent resources support EU more than the CRT, and CRT was consistent with the BR-null finding as well (p. 1075; fn. 8 on 3-item vs 7-item CRT in study 2).
- Cognitive resources were not manipulated, so no causal claim about their effect on cue-following. Resources may proxy careful reading or another motivation to toe the party line (p. 1075).
- Depth-of-processing mechanism was not supported; the measures (reading time, thought listing, quiz) may not capture rationalization effort (p. 1074). Authors say the mechanism remains untested.
- Individual three-way interactions usually did not reach conventional significance; only two of six meta-analytic three-way effects were significant (p. 1075).
- Study 4 (MTurk) produced no patterns; the authors suggest platform effects (higher CRT means, possible CRT overuse), and note MTurk may not be ideal for such studies (fn. 18, p. 1076).
- One-shot manipulations embedded in attitude surveys do not replicate the repeated, cacophonous political information environment; the paper speaks to short-term cue-following on newly politicized issues, not stable attitudes (p. 1076).
- Issues were chosen to be not yet strongly partisan, which limits generalization to already polarized issues (p. 1073).
- Two ancillary studies lack a no-cues condition and cannot speak to presence vs absence of cues; the Danish study uses ideological rather than partisan identity (fn. 15).
- The paper's explanation of issue-dependence (in-party vs out-party strength) is post hoc and speculative; sampling, method, and political environment differences across studies are not ruled out (p. 1074).
- Speculation that elite polarization could be self-reinforcing through identity-driven conformity among reflective partisans is not tested here (p. 1076).
- Panels are online, non-probability samples; representativeness is not reported in the main text. Study 2 N in text (1,302) differs from Table 3 (1,258).

## Gaps and flags

- cpf_pressure and lt_priming have no negative out-party cue term: cpf_pressure keeps out-party pressure positive (x0.5), and lt_priming clamps at 0. (salience_shock's retreat and backfire regimes do give out-partisans -1.) The paper's central main effect is an out-party decrease in support of about -0.07.
- The 1.7 and 0.5 constants are uncalibrated. The paper's in-party effect (+0.03, often non-significant) gives no basis for amplifying co-partisan pressure, yet the code cites Bakker for it.
- bpf, a cosponsorship bridging share, is the main identity-type variable in the engine (the receptivity layer also uses a communion and agency persona z, PERSONA_W 0.30, and a cooperation share; party is the categorical identity). None of these measures partisan identity strength, which is the moderator the paper actually finds.
- No elite-level cue calibration exists. The kernel's partisan constants rest on mass-public survey experiments; an elite cue-taking study such as Box-Steffensmeier et al. on Dear Colleague letters would transfer more directly.
- Citation over-claim: moneyball_core.py:11 and tpl:585 attribute 'co-partisan x1.7 / out x0.5' persuasion to Furnas/Bakker. Bakker reports out-party cues as lowering support, not attenuated-positive, so the Bakker half of the citation does not support a positive 0.5.

The pooled out-party effect (-0.07) is a reported decrease relative to the no-cue control; 'backfire' is an interpretive label, not the paper's word. The live clamp at 0, combined with the bridge exemption, does not reproduce a negative out-party term.
