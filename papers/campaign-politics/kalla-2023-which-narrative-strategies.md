# Which Narrative Strategies Durably Reduce Prejudice? Evidence from Field and Survey Experiments Supporting the Efficacy of Perspective-Getting

**Joshua L. Kalla, David E. Broockman (2023).** *American Journal of Political Science, Vol. 67, No. 1 (January 2023), pp. 185-204. Copyright notice reads 2021, Midwest Political Science Association..* [10.1111/ajps.12657](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12657). Theme: [[themes/campaign-politics|Campaign Politics]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Kalla and Broockman (AJPS 2023, vol. 67, pp. 185-204; DOI 10.1111/ajps.12657, so the vault slug's 2020 year and OSF DOI are wrong) take apart the deep-canvass conversation. They test which narrative step actually reduces exclusionary attitudes toward unauthorized immigrants and transgender people. Study 1 (MI, PA, NC, fall 2019; about 2,975 voters per arm) found that dropping analogic perspective-taking did not weaken effects, which lasted at least 4.5 months. Dichotomized health-policy items moved 5 to 9 points. In Study 2 (CA and TN, 2020; about 2,050 per arm, stopped early by COVID), a perspective-getting-only script took 7.3 minutes against 17.1 for the full script. It matched the full script: the 95% CI on the difference ran from -0.05 to 0.09 SD. Scholarship support rose 12.7 points and in-state-tuition support 7.4. A non-randomized phone follow-up gave about 0.12 SD. The within-subject analyses and a nine-arm meta-regression point the same way, but both are observational. Effects are intent-to-treat among people who came to the door, about 81 to 82% of whom engaged. All outcomes are explicit issue attitudes; votes and turnout were not measured.
> 
> For Paul's systems the paper bears only on Terrier's canvass layer, and only loosely. Untapped potential picks *whom* to knock on a prior that persuasion peaks at 50% candidate support. The paper says nothing about that prior. What it shows is that the effect depends on *what is said* at the door, and that a script half as long can work just as well. Nothing in Melange's legislator-facing engine is touched, because these are voter attitude experiments, not studies of elites, lobbying or networks. The paper also is not the 'Kalla & Broockman' that the flip model's money-buys-access comment cites.

## Study

**Question:** Which narrative strategies used in interpersonal conversation (analogic perspective-taking, vicarious perspective-giving, perspective-getting; and traditional perspective-taking in a survey) durably reduce exclusionary attitudes toward unauthorized immigrants and transgender people, and through which mechanisms?

**Theory:** Four narrative strategies are distinguished by source (self-generated, recalled, or received) and topic (own experience or outgroup member's experience) (Table 1, p.186). Traditional perspective-taking (imagining oneself as the outgroup member) promotes self-other merging but may be resisted for stigmatized groups (p.189). Analogic perspective-taking (recalling one's own similar experience) may work through self-outgroup overlap and reactive empathy (p.189). Vicarious perspective-giving (recounting an outgroup member's experience) may avoid self-similarity concerns but may fail if the receiver does not know an outgroup member well (p.190). Perspective-getting (hearing an outgroup member's narrative) may activate reactive empathy (identifiable victim effect), attributional thinking (situational rather than personal causes), entitativity (seeing the group as individuals), and self-outgroup merging (p.190), though it is a less active process and may be subtyped away (p.190). Conversation effects are expected through narrative transportation, effortful processing, and nonjudgmental exchange (p.188 to p.189). The authors predict that perspective-getting is the component that matters.

**Data:** Study 1 (door-to-door, Michigan, Pennsylvania, North Carolina; canvassing Sept-Nov 2019): 369,317 registered voters mailed a baseline survey; 8,925 baseline respondents randomized to Full Intervention (n=2,973), Intervention without Analogic Perspective-Taking (n=2,975), or Placebo (n=2,977); unit is the voter, with household-level blocked randomization. Follow-ups at 1 week, 7 weeks, and 4.5 months. Study 2 (door-to-door, California and Tennessee, winter-spring 2020, halted in March 2020 by COVID-19): 336,023 mailed; 9,340 completed the first baseline with valid email; 6,163 completed both baselines and were randomized to Full Intervention (n=2,053), Perspective-Getting Only (n=2,054), or Placebo (n=2,056). Follow-ups at 1 week, 11 weeks, and 4.5 months (4.5-month wave underpowered). Study 3 (phone follow-up of the Study 2 sample, 2020): difference-in-differences; sample size not stated in the main text (Appendix E). Study 4 (survey experiment on Lucid, 6 x 2 factorial crossing six prejudice-reduction arms with outgroup type): N and fielding date not stated in the text. Within-subject analyses use canvasser-kept records of which narratives were shared, Studies 1-3 (Study 1 records distinguish narrative type; Studies 2-3 record only who shared). Meta-regression uses n=9 treatment arms from Broockman and Kalla 2016, Kalla and Broockman 2020, and this article.

**Method:** Field RCTs with placebo arms, pre-registered outcome indices (first factor of a factor analysis of the pre-registered items, standardized). Study 1 and 2: OLS with pre-treatment covariates, standard errors clustered by household, intent-to-treat among those who identified themselves at the door. Within-subject (not pre-registered in Study 1): difference-in-differences with survey-wave and individual fixed effects, indicator for post-treatment wave interacted with whether the canvasser or voter shared each narrative type, SEs clustered by individual, treatment arms only. Study 3: difference-in-differences comparing change among reached and unreached people, without an uncontacted control group (Table 2 note, p.188), so the estimate is close to a complier-average effect. Pooled within-subject estimate is precision-weighted across Studies 1-3 (Fig 6). Meta-regression: random-effects, study-level clustering, dummies for presence of each narrative in each arm (p.197 to p.198). Survey experiment: linear regression with pre-registered pretreatment covariates, Identified Photo arm as baseline, separate scales for each outgroup, and a mediator battery (Fig 8).

**Identification:** Randomized assignment to arms at the household level in the field studies, with a placebo condition that includes the same contact step so that deliverable voters can be identified (p.191). Effects are reported as intent-to-treat among those who came to the door; roughly 81 to 82 percent of treated voters entered the conversation, and the authors suggest multiplying by about 1.23 (Study 1, p.192 fn 4) or 1.22 (Study 2, p.197 fn 5) to get effects among those who engaged. Survey experiment: individual random assignment to photo-and-strategy arms, with the Identified Photo arm added to address demand effects seen in two earlier survey experiments (p.199 fn 6). Within-subject and meta-regression analyses are observational: voters chose whether to share narratives (p.198), so identification depends on the fixed-effects difference-in-differences and the assumption that changes would otherwise have been parallel. The authors say these analyses cannot establish causality (p.194 and p.199).

**Scope:** Population: US registered voters reached by partner advocacy organizations in Michigan, Pennsylvania, North Carolina (Study 1), California and Tennessee (Study 2, Study 3 phone), and an online Lucid panel (Study 4). Outgroups: unauthorized immigrants (Studies 1-3 and Study 4 arm) and transgender people (Study 4 arm; prior study). Era: fall 2019 to spring 2020, when including unauthorized immigrants in Medicaid and universal health care was salient in Democratic primary politics (p.191). Institution: door-to-door canvassing by community organizations trained by the New Conversation Initiative. Outcomes: policy-support and prejudice indices (explicit attitudes only). Voting, behavior, and implicit attitudes were not measured (p.203). Not a study of elections, legislators, lobbying, or networks.

## Key findings

- Abstract summary: conversations employing only perspective-getting narratives durably reduce exclusionary attitudes; omitting analogic perspective-taking and vicarious perspective-giving does not diminish effects; perspective-getting consistently reduces exclusionary attitudes across all designs. (No numeric effect sizes in the abstract.; Abstract, p.185)
- Study 1: removing the analogic perspective-taking step did not reduce effects. The full intervention and the version without analogic perspective-taking were similarly effective, and the latter was slightly larger; effects persisted for at least 4.5 months. (Baseline randomized n = 2,973 (Full), 2,975 (without analogic), 2,977 (Placebo); 369,317 mailed; effect sizes only in Fig 1 (not in extracted text).; Results, p.192 to p.193; Fig 1)
- Study 1 dichotomized health-policy items: both treatments moved respondents into support, and the without-analogic version was always similar to or larger than the full version. (Increases of 5 to 9 percentage points on dichotomized health-care policy items.; p.193; Fig 2; Table OA20)
- Study 1 within-subject (observational, not pre-registered): voters who heard a story about an immigrant (perspective-getting) had significantly higher overall index changes; voters who told a story about someone they know (vicarious) showed a negative, insignificant change; analogic stories had no significant added change. (Point estimates in Fig 3 only; vicarious estimate negative and insignificant; perspective-getting significant (values not in text).; p.194; Fig 3)
- Study 2 (randomized): perspective-getting-only conversations had effects similar to the full intervention, and both were statistically significant on the overall index. The full-minus-perspective-getting-only comparison was nearly zero, and the authors judge the difference to be noise that warrants replication. (Overall-index 95% CI on Full minus PG-only from 0.09 to -0.05 SD; Full n = 2,053, PG-only n = 2,054, Placebo n = 2,056.; p.195 to p.196; Fig 4)
- Study 2 durability and duration: effects persisted at least 11 weeks; at 4.5 months the Full arm had p = .07 and the PG-only arm p = .24 (not significant). Perspective-getting-only conversations took about half the time. (Full conversation averaged 17.1 minutes; PG-only averaged 7.3 minutes.; p.196 to p.197 (footnote to 4.5-month p-values); p.195 (timing))
- Study 2 dichotomized items: first post-treatment survey showed increases in support for state-college scholarships and in-state tuition for unauthorized immigrants. Canvasser immigrant status made no difference. (+12.7 percentage points (college scholarships); +7.4 percentage points (in-state tuition).; p.197; Fig A1 and Table OA43; Table OA33)
- Study 3 (phone follow-up, difference-in-differences, non-randomized): reached respondents showed larger attitude change than unreached respondents on the overall index. (Approximately 0.12 SD on overall index of policy and prejudice items, p < .01.; p.198 (Study 3 paragraph); Table OA46 (appendix))
- Within-subject, Studies 1 to 3 (observational): attitude change is much larger when canvassers shared stories (perspective-getting), and there is little evidence the change is larger when voters shared stories (analogic or vicarious). (Pooled estimate: canvasser-story effect large and significant; voter-story effect small and not significant (values in Fig 6 only).; p.198 to p.199; Fig 6 (pooled precision-weighted row))
- Meta-regression of nine treatment arms across three studies (random-effects): arms including perspective-getting have a large, significant effect; adding vicarious perspective-giving or analogic perspective-taking gives negative, insignificant coefficients. (n = 9 arms; coefficients only in Fig 5.; p.197 to p.198; Fig 5)
- Study 4 survey experiment: traditional perspective-taking had no effect on prejudice; analogic perspective-taking and perspective-getting both had effects on both outgroups; perspective-getting with an essay task may have been larger than without, but not significantly. (Effect sizes only in Fig 7; N not stated in extracted text.; p.200 to p.201; Fig 7; Table 3 (p.200))
- Study 4 mechanisms: traditional perspective-taking increased self-outgroup merging and changed attributional thinking but did not reduce prejudice; analogic perspective-taking activated self-outgroup merging only; perspective-getting activated attributional thinking, reactive empathy and self-outgroup merging, but not entitativity. Perspective-getting without an essay did not activate reactive empathy yet had a borderline prejudice effect. (Effects in Fig 8 only.; p.201 to p.202; Fig 8; footnote 10 (p.201) gives item wording)
- Authors' overall conclusion: perspective-getting is the key ingredient; across field, within-subject, meta-analytic and survey evidence it is the strategy that reliably reduces attitudes, while the other strategies have less reliable effects. (No additional figures.; p.203 (Discussion); p.190 (Study 1 summary) and p.196 (Study 2 summary))

## Limitations

- Within-subject and meta-regression analyses are observational: voters chose whether to share narratives, and the within-subject Study 1 analysis was not pre-registered (p.194, p.198, p.199).
- Study 2 stopped early because of COVID-19, so standard errors are larger and the 4.5-month comparison is underpowered; the 11-week versus 4.5-month comparison is not sufficiently powered (p.196 to p.197).
- Study 3 was not randomized because the sample with phone numbers was too small for adequate power; the difference-in-differences has no uncontacted control group and estimates a complier-type effect (p.188 Table 2 note; p.198).
- Study 1 randomized only the analogic prompt; perspective-getting and vicarious giving were not separately manipulated, and the conversation effects of voter-shared stories are inferred from records (p.193).
- Study 2's perspective-getting-only arm still involved spontaneous two-way exchange, so it is not a pure perspective-getting treatment (p.195).
- Effects are intent-to-treat among voters who came to the door; about 18 percent of treated voters did not enter the conversation, and the authors' multipliers (about 1.22 to 1.23) are approximations (p.192 fn 4; p.197 fn 5).
- Outcomes are explicit self-reported attitudes; behavior, voting, and implicit attitudes were not measured (p.203).
- Survey experiment measured effects immediately after the task, and the authors note traditional perspective-taking results may not replicate in the field (p.200 to p.201). Two earlier survey experiments showed demand effects, which motivated the Identified Photo arm (p.199 fn 6); the Identified Photo arm itself had effects (p.200 fn 7).
- Mechanism measures are single items and the authors say mediation cannot be established; at least one unmeasured mediator must be operating (p.188; p.202).
- Samples were highly educated and politically engaged; weighting to the sampling frame increased estimates, which the authors read as conservative (p.193; p.197).
- Study 4 essay effect is not significant, and Study 4 sample size and fielding date are not stated in the extracted text.
- Scope conditions unknown: fictionalized versus documentary narratives, canvasser or actor quality, competing counternarratives, mass-media delivery, and outgroup stigma level are all left for future research (p.202 to p.203).

## Gaps and flags

- Untapped potential scores who to contact but has no notion of what treatment the contact delivers. The persuade term is the same whether the volunteer reads a reminder or holds a perspective-getting conversation, yet the paper shows the protocol determines whether any durable change happens.
- There is no validation design for the persuade term. The paper's template could test it: household-blocked randomization, a placebo arm with the same contact step, follow-up surveys at 1 week to 4.5 months, and ITT among door-openers. Without such a test, the s=0.5 peak in (1-|2s-1|) is an unvalidated prior.
- The knocked() exclusion treats any recorded result as worked. The paper's ITT logic separates door-openers who engaged (about 81-82%) from those who did not. Terrier's result codes are not used to distinguish a completed conversation from a brief contact, which matters if persuasion credit is ever estimated.
- The turf planner has no time-per-conversation input. The 2.3x difference in conversation length between scripts (17.1 vs 7.3 min) is not reflected in the fixed 60-door target.
- Citation mismatch: fec/engine/flip_model.py:21 cites 'Kalla&Broockman' for 'money buys access/effort > votes'. That is the campaign-contributions access experiment (vault slug kalla-nd-campaign-contributions-facilitate), NOT this prejudice-reduction paper. This paper must not be read as support for the flip model's money weights.
- Vault metadata error: slug year 2020 and DOI 10.31219/osf.io/z2awt (OSF preprint) are wrong for the published version. The correct values are AJPS 67(1):185-204, 2023, DOI 10.1111/ajps.12657. Paul's two annotation entries carry the preprint DOI.
- The within-subject (Fig 3, Fig 6), Study 3 DiD and meta-regression (n=9 arms) results are observational and partly not pre-registered. Any system note quoting them should carry that label. Only the Study 1 and Study 2 randomized arm contrasts are causal.
- Paul's annotation files for this work (c45e04ec65f6, 9488c4b3cd41) contain only bare citations with no takeaways. His substantive reading of this paper exists only as PDF highlights (129, all with empty notes).

Paul's highlights fall heavily on perspective-getting being the active ingredient, on the nonjudgmental exchange, on the shorter perspective-getting-only script, and on training possibly being unnecessary. These are the paper's own conclusions, so his reading agrees with the evidence. His component note for terrier-canvass.untapped-potential says the door protocol (nonjudgmental exchange, perspective-getting, 7-minute version) 'is not encoded in the persuasion term'. That is still accurate as of untapped.py:118-119: the persuade term is (1-|2s-1|) x t with no protocol input. The note is therefore not stale. One mismatch remains: the paper's outcomes are issue attitudes, not candidate support or turnout. So Paul's implied link from this protocol to Terrier's vote-choice persuade term is an extrapolation the paper does not test. His annotation citation also uses the 2020 OSF DOI rather than the 2023 AJPS version.
