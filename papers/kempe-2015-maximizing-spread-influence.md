# Maximizing the Spread of Influence through a Social Network

**David Kempe, Jon Kleinberg, Eva Tardos (2015).** *Theory of Computing 11(4), 2015, pp. 105-147 (extended version of KDD 2003 and ICALP 2005 papers).* [10.4086/toc.2015.v011a004](https://doi.org/10.4086/toc.2015.v011a004) Theme: [[themes/adaptive-influence-maximization|Adaptive Influence Maximization]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos give the founding result of influence maximization. Under the Independent Cascade and Linear Threshold models, expected spread sigma(A) is monotone and submodular. The proof goes through live-edge or triggering-set worlds (Lemma 4.4). Greedy hill-climbing therefore reaches (1-1/e-eps) of the optimal k-seed set, and eps shrinks as the Monte Carlo estimate of sigma improves. The guarantee has hard edges. LT needs thresholds drawn uniformly on [0,1] and in-weights summing to at most 1. General threshold models cannot be approximated within n^(1-eps) (Theorem 3.3). Competing cascades are left open. Experiments run on one 10,748-node hep-th co-authorship graph, with 10,000 simulations per evaluation and k up to 30. Under LT, greedy beats high-degree seeds by about 18% and distance centrality by over 40%. Under IC with p = 10%, the centrality heuristics plateau near 2,415 activations, and random seeds beat them beyond about 12 targets. Edge parameters are set by rule, not estimated. For Melange, the paper is the method source for the edge-keyed common-random-number draws, which are live-edge worlds, and for the lazy-greedy frontier. It also marks where the system sits outside the guarantee. The frontier greedy runs on a two-colour race, scales marginals by a leverage factor and averages only 20 worlds. LT priming uses a fixed threshold ladder. The coalition allocator ranks by singleton marginals, which is the overlap-blind pattern the paper's experiments penalize. All of this is model-based expected activation under assumed parameters. None of it is a closeness, access or persuasion probability (D-025).

## Study

**Question:** How should a k-node seed set be chosen to maximize the expected final number of active nodes when influence spreads through a social network under threshold or cascade diffusion models? The paper asks whether the NP-hard influence maximization problem admits efficient approximation guarantees, and which model generalizations keep such guarantees.

**Theory:** Progressive diffusion models from mathematical sociology (Granovetter and Schelling threshold models) and interacting particle systems (independent cascade). Influence is submodular under these models, so greedy hill-climbing gives a (1-1/e-eps) guarantee via Nemhauser-Wolsey-Fisher. Triggering-set equivalence (Lemma 4.4) is the main proof device. Generalizations: general threshold, general cascade, decreasing cascade, submodular threshold, non-progressive, and multi-action marketing with diminishing returns.

**Data:** No empirical influence data. Experiments use a co-authorship graph built from the high-energy physics theory section of arXiv as of winter 2002: 10748 nodes (researchers with at least one coauthored paper), about 53000 node pairs with edges, parallel edges kept as tie strength. Single-author papers ignored. Unit of analysis: researcher. Influence parameters are set by simple rules, not estimated (Section 8.2). Each targeted set is evaluated with 10000 simulations; target set size k runs from 1 to 30.

**Method:** Theory: NP-hardness reductions (vertex cover, maximum coverage, set cover), monotone and submodular proofs via triggering sets, the Decreasing Cascade coupling argument (Theorem 5.1), a layered-graph reduction for non-progressive models (Theorem 6.1), and a hill-climbing analysis for budgeted marketing actions (Theorem 7.1). Empirics: greedy Monte Carlo hill-climbing compared with high-degree, distance-centrality, and random seed selection under Linear Threshold, weighted Cascade, and Independent Cascade with p = 1% and p = 10%.

**Identification:** Not applicable. This is a worst-case algorithm analysis and a simulation comparison on a fixed graph. No causal identification strategy is claimed.

**Scope:** Population: abstract diffusion models on directed graphs, tested on one collaboration network. Institution: theoretical computer science. Era: conference versions 2003 and 2005, journal version 2015, with 2014 revisions. Claims are about model-based seeding, not about real-world marketing outcomes.

## Key findings

- Greedy hill-climbing gives a (1-1/e-eps) approximation for influence maximization in the Linear Threshold and Independent Cascade models (Theorem 1.1, p.106; proof Section 4, pp.118-122).
- Influence sigma(A) is monotone and submodular for every triggering-set model (Lemma 4.4, pp.119-120), including Independent Cascade (Theorem 4.5, p.121) and Linear Threshold with in-weights summing to at most 1 (Theorem 4.6, pp.121-122).
- Decreasing Cascade is submodular (Theorem 5.1, p.124) even though no triggering-set equivalent exists (example, pp.123-124); the proof uses a residual-process coupling.
- Exact influence maximization is NP-hard for Linear Threshold (Theorem 2.1, p.111), and for Independent Cascade it is hard to approximate better than 1-1/e (Theorem 2.2, p.113).
- For general threshold models, influence maximization cannot be approximated within n^(1-eps) for any eps > 0 under P not equal NP (Theorem 3.3, pp.116-117).
- Non-progressive influence maximization over horizon tau reduces exactly to progressive maximization on a layered graph with tau*|V| nodes (Theorem 6.1, p.127), so the guarantees carry over.
- For budget split across marketing actions with diminishing returns, hill-climbing gives 1 - exp(-gamma k/(k+delta m)) of optimum (Theorem 7.1, pp.128-130).
- Linear Threshold experiment on the hep-th co-authorship graph: greedy beats high-degree by about 18% and distance centrality by over 40% (Section 8.4, p.134; Figure 1).
- Weighted Cascade curves have the same shape as Linear Threshold, with values about 25% smaller (Section 8.4, p.135; Figure 2).
- Independent Cascade with p = 1%: each targeted node adds on average about three activations, so network effects are much weaker; random seeds do relatively better (Section 8.4, pp.135-136; Figure 3).
- Independent Cascade with p = 10%: beyond about 12 targets, random seeds beat both high-degree and distance-centrality heuristics; those heuristics reach about 2415 activations and then barely grow (Section 8.4, pp.136-137; Figure 4).
- Greedy keeps growing the active set in every experiment because it accounts for overlap of earlier seeds' reach (Section 8.4, pp.136-137); the paper says a local search step would likely improve results further (p.137).

## Limitations

- Influence probabilities and thresholds are not inferred from data; the experiments use simple uniform or weighted settings (Section 8.2, p.133; Conclusions, pp.138-139).
- The co-authorship graph is a proxy for social influence, with name-aliasing errors only partly corrected (Section 8.1, p.133).
- Models are progressive in the main analysis; the non-progressive extension is a reduction, not a full experimental study (Section 6, pp.126-128).
- No approximation hardness is known for Linear Threshold, so the optimal tight ratio there is open (Conclusions, p.138).
- Sigma(A) is only estimated by Monte Carlo; the paper notes exact evaluation is #P-complete (Section 4, p.118), and weighted sampling can be inaccurate for exponentially large weights (footnote 3, p.119).
- Experiments stop at k = 30 for computational reasons, and only one network is studied (Section 8.3, pp.134-135).
- The paper leaves competing cascades, negative opinions, and a consistent timing-free competition model open (Conclusions, pp.137-138).

## Gaps and flags

- The 20-world Monte Carlo average has no stated error bound. The paper used 10,000 simulations, and sample-complexity results (Sadeh et al. 2019, from the online scan) scale with n, seed count and eps^-2. The frontier knee and seed order may move between MC 20 and MC 1000. mc_stability_check.py exists, but whether it bounds seed-order stability was not checked here.
- No greedy-vs-degree-vs-random benchmark in the style of Section 8 has been run on the live 533-member kernel, so the gain from greedy over cheap centrality seeding is unmeasured for this system.
- The coalition-side (1-1/e) claim in scenario.py rests on Bharathi-Kempe-Salek. Whether the BFS first-to-reach race with fixed A-first queue order is submodular in pro seeds has not been shown or tested numerically (for example, by random diminishing-returns probes on the 20 CRN worlds).
- The fac-weighted, max(0, .)-clamped marginals in the frontier greedy optimize no explicitly defined set function, so no approximation statement attaches to the frontier path.
- Kernel edge probabilities are not estimated from data. Kempe flags parameter estimation as open, and the saturation constant 6 has no sensitivity sweep reported against frontier seed order.
- Weighted Cascade (Section 8.2) sets p(u,v) = c_uv / d_v, a per-edge normalization by receiver in-degree. Per-edge volume normalization was measured and REJECTED in this project, so do not adopt the weighted cascade kernel on that basis.
- The paper's 'influence' and sigma are expected activations under an assumed diffusion model. Any Melange spread, frontier or InfluenceScore output must not be described as a closeness, access, introduction or persuasion probability (D-025). The cosponsor_refinement report already words Kempe this way correctly.
- Kempe's experimental margins (18%, 40%, 2,415 activations) come from one hep-th graph, window winter 2002, denominator 10,748 researchers with k = 1..30. Quote them with that window and denominator, never as transferable effect sizes.
