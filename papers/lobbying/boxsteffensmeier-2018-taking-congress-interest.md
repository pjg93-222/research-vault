# Cue-Taking in Congress: Interest Group Signals from Dear Colleague Letters

**Janet M. Box-Steffensmeier, Dino P. Christenson, Alison W. Craig (2019).** *American Journal of Political Science, 63(1), pp. 163-180 (online 2018).* [10.1111/ajps.12399](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12399) Theme: [[themes/lobbying|Lobbying]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Box-Steffensmeier, Christenson and Craig (AJPS 63(1), 2019; online 2018) study 6,633 House bills that were the subject of supportive Dear Colleague letters, 106th to 111th Congresses (1999-2010; regression N = 6,597). They code the interest groups named as endorsers in each letter and measure two signals: Interest Group Social Power, the highest standardized eigenvector centrality of any endorser in the amicus curiae co-signing network, and Interest Group Count, the number of endorsers. In a negative binomial model of cosponsor counts with Congress fixed effects, both are positive (0.055 and 0.007, p<.001). Predicted cosponsors rise from 33 with no endorsement to 51 with a top-centrality endorser; 61 endorsements from groups with no more than average centrality are needed to match that. Logit models of committee reporting and House passage show no direct social-power effect. Count predicts passage (0.013) but not reporting. Cosponsor count predicts both later stages (0.002, 0.004). Hot-bill status (2.428) is the largest passage predictor; commemorative status (1.386) and majority-party sponsorship (1.353) follow. The paper has no causal design. Endorsements may be solicited by the sponsor's office, and estimates are pooled across six Congresses and both parties. For Paul's systems, the paper is mainly a source of features and a set of caveats. No built component uses endorsement or group-signal data. The cosponsor forecaster uses member-dyad features plus bill type, policy area, committee referrals and sponsor majority party, but no endorsement or outside-support feature, so bill-level outside-support signals are a possible addition. Any LDA-based substitute must respect D-021, however. The legislative pillar's efficiency term credits raw advancement rates with no majority-party adjustment; the paper reports majority status only as a control association and does not show that the efficiency rate is biased. The solicitation ambiguity is a caution about reading bill co-occurrence as influence; the system's LDA filings never name the members lobbied.

## Study

**Question.** Why do some bills move forward while others languish? Do interest group endorsements listed in Dear Colleague letters signal bill quality to members of Congress, and does their effect vary across the legislative process?

**Theory.** Cue-taking under limited information: members with limited time and staff rely on informative signals, and interest group endorsements are cheap, quick cues. H1 and H1a: endorsements from well-connected groups (high eigenvector centrality) are informative, most strongly early in the process. H2 and H2a: the number of endorsing groups matters more later, because it signals broad support and a larger lobbying ground game. Builds on Hall and Deardorff (2006) on lobbying as legislative subsidy and on the Kingdon cue literature.

**Data.** Electronic Dear Colleague letters sent between House members, 106th to 111th Congresses (1999-2010): 93,967 letters, of which 6,633 bills had letters urging support. Unit is the bill. 27 percent of bills with letters had letters naming interest groups. Endorsing organizations matched to 11,019 organizations in the Amicus Curiae Networks Project plus hand-added names. Outcomes (cosponsors, reported from House committee, passed House) and sponsor data from the Congressional Bills Project. Hot Bills list from the Senate Library. Regression N = 6,597.

**Method.** Negative binomial regression of the cosponsor count with Congress fixed effects (Table 3; AIC 60,835.04). Logit models for Reported House and Passed House (Table 4), each including cosponsor count as a regressor to capture an indirect path from social power to passage. Standard errors in parentheses; significance stars.

**Identification.** No causal identification design (no instrument, natural experiment, or bill-level panel). Associational estimates with Congress fixed effects, sponsor and bill controls, and the cosponsor count entered as a downstream regressor. Footnote 11 notes that solicited and group-initiated endorsements cannot be distinguished.

**Scope.** US House of Representatives only; bills that were the subject of Dear Colleague letters urging support, 1999-2010 (106th to 111th Congresses). Not the full population of introduced bills, and not the Senate, except that robustness on all bills in the 111th Congress is reported in supporting information (not in the text file).

## Key findings

- Early stage: bills endorsed by well-connected groups attract more cosponsors. Interest Group Social Power has a positive, significant effect on the cosponsor count. (coef 0.055 (SE 0.011), p<.001; predicted cosponsors 33 with unconnected endorsers, 38 with an endorser at centrality 2.51 (Anti-Defamation League), 51 with one at 7.66 (Planned Parenthood)) [Table 3 and Figure 3, pp. 175-176]
- Breadth also raises early cosponsorship: each additional endorsing group adds cosponsors. Matching one moderately connected endorser takes 19 low-centrality groups. (Interest Group Count coef 0.007 (SE 0.001), p<.001; 61 endorsements from groups with no more than average centrality match the 51 predicted for a Planned Parenthood-level endorsement) [Table 3 and Figure 4, p. 176]
- Social power has no significant direct effect on committee reporting or floor passage. The authors describe a single well-connected endorsement as no longer enough late in the process. (Reported House 0.020 (SE 0.031); Passed House -0.035 (SE 0.032); both not significant) [Table 4, p. 177]
- Breadth matters at the floor stage: the number of endorsing groups significantly predicts House passage but not committee reporting. (Passed House 0.013 (SE 0.004), p<.001; Reported House 0.004 (SE 0.003), not significant) [Table 4, p. 177]
- Predicted passage probability rises with the number of endorsing groups, though slowly. (predicted probability 0.09 with no endorsements, 0.10 with 10 groups, 0.20 with 75 groups) [Figure 5 and text, pp. 177-178]
- Cosponsorship is an indirect channel from social power to success: cosponsor count predicts both committee reporting and passage. (cosponsors coef 0.002 (SE 0.001), p<.01 for Reported House; 0.004 (SE 0.001), p<.001 for Passed House) [Table 4 and text, pp. 177-178]
- Hot bills and commemorative bills attract more cosponsors; hot bills and majority-party sponsors are more likely to pass. (hot bill cosponsors coef 0.365 (p<.001), about 15 additional cosponsors; commemorative bills average 78 cosponsors vs 33; Passed House: hot bill 2.428, majority 1.353, both p<.001) [Table 3 and Table 4, pp. 176-178]
- Network measure example: standardized eigenvector centrality in the amicus curiae network separates well-connected groups from peripheral ones. (NOW 8.2962; NEA 2.6653; AFL-CIO 0.8824; AHA -0.1095; ASA -0.2053 (set as the minimum for groups absent from the network)) [Pp. 172-173 and Table 2, p. 174]
- Sample scale: Dear Colleague letter volume rose sharply over the period. (31,994 electronic letters in the 111th Congress vs 15,477 in the 109th (up 106.7 percent)) [p. 168]
- Hypothesis pattern: H1a (social power stronger early) and H2a (breadth stronger late) are both supported as stated by the authors. (early social power significant, late social power not; early count significant, late count significant for passage only) [Pp. 176-178]
- Conclusion on pluralism: well-connected groups act as early gatekeepers, but broad multi-group support is necessary for passage. [Pp. 178-179]

## Limitations

- Endorsements may be solicited by a member's office or initiated by the group; letters do not reveal which (footnote 11, p. 170).
- Endorser names were matched to the amicus network and hand-checked; groups absent from the network receive the minimum centrality score, which the authors say understates their influence (footnote 18, p. 173).
- The amicus curiae network is used as a proxy for legislative relationships; the authors assume judicial and legislative relationships persist across branches (footnote 15, p. 172).
- The analysis is restricted to bills that were the subject of Dear Colleague letters, with opposition letters removed (footnote 14, p. 171). Results for all bills appear only in supporting information.
- Hot bill status may be determined after endorsements, which complicates its use as a control (footnote 21, p. 175).
- Some letters are purpose-coded from a hand-coded subsample of 4,656 letters (footnote 9, p. 169).
- Endorsing groups may not interact with one another, so endorsement lists do not establish coordination (p. 171).
- The authors report no systematic per-Congress pattern in the social power and count effects (footnote 22, p. 175).
- Associational results; no causal design. The authors describe the results as complex and open new questions (p. 178).

## Gaps and flags

- No component ingests outside-group support signals on bills (endorsements, coalition letters, Dear Colleague letters). The only bill-level organization data are LDA interaction totals, which are display-only and, under D-021, cannot serve as a weight.
- The legislative pillar's efficiency rate has no control for majority-party sponsorship or bill salience. The paper reports both as passage-model controls (hot bill 2.428, commemorative 1.386, majority 1.353, House 1999-2010) but does not show the efficiency rate is biased.
- The cosponsor forecaster has no endorsement or outside-support feature; its bill-level covariates are bill type, policy area, committee referrals and sponsor majority party.
- No component uses a network of organizations (as opposed to members) to rank the standing of groups. Building one would need an identity-resolved organization graph and D-025-compliant wording.
- Eigenvector centrality in the amicus network is called social power and influence by the authors. If reused in Melange, it is a heuristic model output, never closeness, access or influence (D-025).
- All coefficients are pooled across the 106th-111th Congresses (Congress FE) and both parties. No strata by party or majority status are reported for the social-power or count effects, although majority status is a strong passage predictor. Quote them as UNSTRATIFIED.
- Transferring endorser count as a count of LDA clients lobbying a bill would use LDA as a weight or feature source, which conflicts with D-021. LDA bill mentions carry no position and are not endorsements.
- Solicited versus group-initiated endorsements cannot be distinguished (footnote 11). The direction of association is unresolved.
- Metadata hint says 2018; the article is AJPS 63(1), January 2019, published online in 2018.
- The paper does not argue for dropping regular cosponsorship edges or for per-edge volume normalization, so neither rejected SNA proposal is raised.

Paul has no bibliography entry for this work.
