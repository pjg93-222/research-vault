# Strategic and Sequential Links between Campaign Donations and Lobbying

**In Song Kim (MIT Political Science), Jan Stuckatz (Institute for Advanced Study in Toulouse), Lukas Wolters (MIT Political Science) (2020).** *MIT Political Science Department Research Paper No. 2021-2; SSRN 3937466. Dated October 15, 2020 in the text. The Journal of Politics 88(1), 2026 listing from the metadata hint does not appear anywhere in the PDF text, so it is unverified here..* SSRN 3937466 Theme: [[themes/lobbying|Lobbying]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Kim, Stuckatz and Wolters link LDA bill mentions to FEC donations for U.S. public manufacturing firms (2,703 firms, 979 members of Congress, 2008-2017, 16.4M firm-politician-year rows). They test whether a donation comes before lobbying on bills that the recipient sponsored, cosponsored or handled in committee. In the descriptive 2013 snapshot, only 519 of 11,829 public firms both donated and lobbied, and they account for about 85% of the money. Under firm-politician fixed effects, a first-time donation goes with a 10-12 point higher chance of lobbying a related bill in the same year, against a base rate of about 0.05. Matched difference-in-differences gives about 8.5 points at year t (Senate), falling to about 3 points by t+3. The effect is concentrated in committee-related lobbying and is largest for corporate PACs. The authors read this as access-buying timing, not quid pro quo.
> 
> For Paul's systems, the paper matters mainly because its estimand differs from what the system measured. The FEC-to-LDA bridge (round 3) is a cross-sectional member-by-industry-by-cycle rank correlation. It is dominated by trade associations, credits only sponsors and cosponsors, and controls for finance-committee seats. Its four-control partial of about 0 does not contradict a within-pair, onset-timed, committee-driven effect among public firms. The bridge's outcome lacks the committee-referral channel that Kim's outcome includes.
> 
> The paper also builds its outcome from the same bill-roster join that the system ruled circular. It escapes circularity only because its treatment, the donation, is timed independently of the join. Its identity work (gvkey matching, constrained graph clustering, hand-coded validation) is a method source for Mentat's donor ladder.

## Study

**Question:** Are campaign donations and lobbying activity linked for the same interest group-politician pair, and do donations precede lobbying on bills tied to the donee's committee seats, sponsorship or co-sponsorship (the "access-buying" hypothesis)?

**Theory:** Two camps on campaign money: donations as ideology or electoral competition (Poole and Romer 1985; Poole, Romer and Rosenthal 1987) versus donations as long-term strategic investments for access (Langbein 1986; Austen-Smith 1995; Esterling 2007; Richter and Werner 2017; Barber, Canes-Wrone and Thrower 2017). Lobbying either conveys information (Austen-Smith and Wright 1992; Potters and Van Winden 1992) or serves as a legislative subsidy to allies (Hall and Deardorff 2006). The paper tests the access-buying direction (donation precedes lobbying) and sets aside rewards (fn 9).

**Data:** Unit: firm-politician-year dyad. Sample: all publicly traded U.S. manufacturing firms (NAICS 31-33; 2,703 firms that lobbied or donated at least once) and all 979 members of the House and Senate serving 2008-2018 (text uses 2008-2017 in places). Result: an unbalanced panel of 2,646,237 unique firm-politician dyads and 16,428,834 observations. Sources: Lobbying Disclosure Act reports (over 1.2 million parsed; over 75 million reports plus FEC filings combined since 1999 per abstract; lobbying data from 1999, bill-level quality from 2008 after the HLOGA amendment); FEC itemized contributions (about 53 million individual contributions, four million employer names, 827,000 occupation names, 1980-2018); Compustat gvkey; SOC codes; GovTrack IDs; Volden and Wiseman (2018) politician covariates; Groseclose and Stewart (1998) committee scores. Broader descriptive coverage: over 84,000 interest groups (abstract, p. 2). Descriptive 2013 snapshot: 11,829 public companies and 553 members of Congress (6,541,437 possible dyads). Appendix E: 1999/2000 interest-group comparison.

**Method:** Step 1: descriptive tables and cross-sectional log-log fits (Table 2, Fig 2, Appendix C and E). Step 2: linear probability models with firm, year, politician and firm-politician fixed effects, with controls for party, majority status, committee chair and powerful-committee membership (Table 3, Table F.1; standard errors clustered by firm). Step 3: difference-in-differences combined with matching (Imai, Kim and Wang 2020). Treated pairs are first-time donors defined over L = 3 years. Matched controls are other firms with no donation to the same politician in t to t-3. Matching uses Mahalanobis distance on sales, employees and pre-treatment lobbying, with 10 closest comparators. The DiD estimator (Eq. 4) differences outcome changes between t-1 and t+F for treated pairs versus the matched mean, averaged over pairs. Confidence intervals come from a block bootstrap. Outcome: Y=1 if the firm lobbies a bill that is sponsored, co-sponsored, or assigned to a committee on which the politician serves (each measured separately).

**Scope:** Population: publicly traded U.S. manufacturing firms and House and Senate members, 2008-2017/2018 (main analysis). Institution: U.S. Congress and federal lobbying disclosure. Era: LDA electronic filing era (1999 on; bill-level data from 2008). Excludes private firms, trade and industry associations, ideological groups, unions and nonprofits from the main analysis. Congress is the relevant lobbied institution; agencies are about 3.4% of reports (p. 5).

## Key findings

- Descriptive 2013 snapshot: only 519 of 11,829 public companies (about 4%) both donated and lobbied, yet they account for most money in politics. (Sec 2.1-2.2, Table 2 (printed p. 11, PDF p. 13)). 519 firms; 4% of 11,829; $2.6B of $3.1B total (85%)
- Firm-politician dyads with both donations and bill-related lobbying are extremely rare but carry large money: 13,886 dyads out of 6,541,437 possible. (Sec 2.1, printed p. 13 (PDF p. 15), Table 2 right panel). 13,886 dyads (<0.1% of all possible); more than $520 million, 17% of 2013 spending; 15,420 donation-only dyads, 13,078 of them from employee donations
- Firm-level corporate PAC donations and lobbying spending are positively related for firms doing both. (Sec 2.2, Fig 2 (printed p. 11-12)). Log-log slope beta = 0.88, adjusted R2 about 0.4; text says a 10% PAC increase goes with 8.8% more lobbying, while the Fig 2 caption computes 8.7%
- Other donation types show the same firm-level pattern (all donations, executives, employees, government relations). (Appendix C, Fig C.1 (appendix p. 3)). beta 0.58 (R2 0.29) all donations; 0.58 (0.17) executives; 0.68 (0.23) employees; 0.78 (0.20) government relations; years 2000-2017
- Within firm-politician pairs, a firm's first-time donation raises the probability it lobbies a bill tied to that politician in the same year. (Sec 3.1, Table 3 (printed p. 16)). Donation coefficient 0.102-0.118 (SEs about 0.008-0.009), N = 16,428,834. Firm-politician and year FE (last column) gives 0.118. Baseline probability about 0.05; sponsors 0.002, co-sponsors 0.026, committee members 0.043
- Donation amount matters at the margin: a 10% larger first-time donation goes with about 0.15 percentage points higher probability of lobbying a related bill. (Sec 3.1, Table 3 Models 5-8 (printed p. 17)). Donation Amount coefficient 0.015-0.017 per log unit (SE 0.001); about 0.15 pp per 10%
- Under difference-in-differences with matching, a donation raises the probability the pair lobbies related bills by about 8.5 points in the donation year, falling to about 3 points by three years later. (Sec 3.2, Fig 5 (printed p. 20-21); caption gives 8.5 falling to 3.2 pp). Senate t+0 about 0.085, t+3 about 0.03; House described as 'even stronger' (no number given in text); Fig 5 still shows about 3.2 pp at t+3
- Effects are concentrated in committee-related lobbying. Corporate PAC donations show the largest effects, but CEO and employee donations are also positive. The same-firm sponsor link is positive but short-lived. (Sec 3.2, Fig 6 (printed p. 21-22)). Qualitative: committee effects largest; PAC > CEO and employee; no numeric values in text
- Robustness to firm-level covariates (sales, employees) leaves the donation coefficient essentially unchanged. (Appendix F, Table F.1 (appendix p. 7)). Donation coefficient 0.104-0.123 across eight models; N = 7,189,477 for covariate-controlled models; adjusted R2 0.450-0.544
- The Ansolabehere, Snyder and Tripathi (2002) pattern holds for the 1999/2000 interest-group data, with the strongest PAC-lobbying relation for corporations. (Appendix E, Fig E.1 (appendix p. 6); cube-root scale). beta: corporations 2.0; membership organizations 1.6; trade associations 0.8; labor unions 0.6 (described as not present)
- Illustrative cases: Farenthold (Exxon Mobil PAC) and Lockheed Martin-Bill Nelson show donations followed by related bills and sponsorship. (Sec 3.2, printed p. 22-23). Farenthold received $3,000 from Exxon's PAC in his first year; Nelson received $9,000 from Lockheed's PAC in 2011 and $22,000 from executives and employees in 2012

## Limitations

- Main analysis covers only publicly traded U.S. manufacturing firms; private firms, trade associations, ideological groups and unions are excluded (printed p. 24).
- Donations through industry associations, ideological groups and nonprofits are not captured; the authors say this may understate donation effects on access (printed p. 24).
- Lobbying-to-politician links rely on bills named in LDA reports. Per-bill lobbying dollars are allocated across connected politicians (the paper does not state an even split), which the authors call a highly noisy measure (printed p. 8). Bill success is not modeled (printed p. 24).
- Access-buying is inferred from timing, not from measured private connections; the reverse (rewards) direction is explicitly left for future work (fn 9, printed p. 14-15).
- Name disambiguation is heuristic with hand validation; a graph neural network upgrade is noted as in progress (printed p. 6; fn 6 mentions only the planned GNN).
- Sample period is stated inconsistently: abstract and Sec 2 say 2000 or 1999 onward, p. 2 says 2000-2018, p. 12 says 2008-2018, and the main estimates use 2008-2017. Donation-to-lobbying elasticity is 8.8% in text versus 8.7% in the Fig 2 caption. Fig C.1 caption says cubed USD while the axes are logged.
- Effects are estimated relative to a low base rate (about 0.05), so 8.5 points is large in relative terms but small in levels. The linear probability models rely on parametric assumptions, as the paper acknowledges (printed p. 17). The DiD estimator is conservative and underpowered per Imai, Kim and Wang (2020), per fn 18.
- Estimates are associations within matched designs, not proof of quid pro quo; the paper itself disclaims causal reading of the reverse direction.
- Journal publication is not in the PDF; the 2026 Journal of Politics listing is taken from the metadata hint only.

## Gaps and flags

- The bridge's Y (rebuilt LDA attention) credits only sponsor and cosponsor rosters. The committee-referral channel, which carries Kim's largest effect, is absent from the join even though build_network_2021_rebuild.py parses committee_referrals. The committee control then absorbs whatever committee-driven signal is left.
- There is no within-pair, onset-timed design anywhere in the system. Every FEC-LDA test is cross-sectional and concurrent, so Kim's estimand (first-time donation, then lobbying in the same year, matched DiD) has never been tested locally.
- The system has no measure of how long an access effect lasts. Money enters the flip model and C3 as cycle-level levels; Kim's matched DiD shows the effect largest at donation onset and falling from about 8.5 to about 3 points by t+3.
- There is no firm-level (corporate) identity layer in the lobbying lane. Clients are mostly associations, which Kim excludes and finds weaker for (trade-association slope 0.8 vs corporations 2.0, Appendix E).
- Mentat's identity ladder has no measured precision or recall. Kim's hand-coded cross-validation is the missing step.
- D-021: Kim builds firm-politician edges and weights (Fig 1; per-bill lobbying $ allocated across connected politicians) from LDA bill mentions. Adopting that construction would make LDA an edge and weight source. Use LDA only for identity; any edge would have to come from the donation side or another source.
- The bridge round 3 standing ruling bars presenting FEC exposure as a lobbying predictor, and a different control set on the 2023-2026 window is NOT a successor. Kim's result must not be pasted into Melange surfaces as 'donations predict lobbying'. It is external evidence on a different population and estimand, UNVERIFIED locally (DE-062).
- Pooled figure: the abstract's 8.5 pp is the Senate t+0 DiD point. The House is larger (no number given) and donor types differ (PAC > CEO/employee). Quote it as '+8.5 pp, Senate, first-time donation, year t, public manufacturing firms 2008-2017, base ~0.05'; otherwise label it UNSTRATIFIED.
- 'Access' in the paper is the authors' interpretation of timing. It must not be carried over as an access or closeness probability for any system output (D-025).
- Internal inconsistencies in the source: elasticity 8.8% in the text vs 8.7% in the Fig 2 caption; sample period given variously as 1999, 2000-2018, 2008-2018 and 2008-2017; the Fig C.1 caption says cubed USD while the axes are logged. Paul's annotation cites the work as 'Systemic and sequential links...' (JOP 2026), but this PDF is titled 'Strategic and Sequential Links...' and dated 2020; the JOP listing is unverified from the PDF.
- The paper does not argue for dropping regular cosponsorship edges or for per-edge volume normalization, so neither rejected SNA proposal is implicated.

This partly agrees with Paul's expectation that donations lead lobbying (his fec-lda-bridge position cites this paper). The agreement holds only at the firm-politician dyad, within pair, at donation onset and over a short horizon. The system's bridge null (four-control partial -0.0002) does not make his note wrong, but the note is now underspecified. It needs the estimand distinction: within-pair onset timing versus cross-sectional member-by-industry, corporations versus trade associations, and committee-referral versus sponsor/cosponsor credit. His highlights of gvkey/SOC matching, constrained graph clustering and hand-coded validation (Mentat employer-match and identity-ladder positions) are accurate. His committee-pillar reading (effects strongest in committee activity) matches the paper.
