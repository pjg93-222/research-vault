# Potential and Pitfalls of Audio as Data for Political Research: Alignment, Features, and Classification Models

**Rafael Mestre, Matt Ryan (2026).** *Political Analysis 34: 312-328 (published online 30 January 2026; open access).* [10.1017/pan.2025.10031](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2025.10031) Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Mestre and Ryan (Political Analysis 2026) is a methods paper on audio as data. Its corpus is 45 U.S. presidential and VP debate videos (1960-2020; 47,150 transcript sentences). It runs four demonstrations. (1) Aeneas MFCC/DTW forced alignment, rated by crowd workers on 500 utterances x 5 ratings: percentage agreement 85.26%, distance agreement 95.76%, and about 5% of pairs not aligned at all, mostly short or interrupted sentences. (2) pYIN pitch and RMS energy. Energy is normalized by each debate's loudness, and within-speaker pitch deviation is shown over time. (3) Closed-set speaker identification. A single layer on mean-pooled wav2vec2-base embeddings reaches 0.914 accuracy across 156 speakers; a CNN on MFCCs reaches 0.081. (4) Off-the-shelf emotion models. The authors say such models can encode and amplify gender and racial bias (no bias is measured), and the outputs disagree with expert coding.
> 
> For Melange the paper is less outside evidence than a method source. The speaker-identity research layer already copies it closely: `b5_audio_neural_head.py` reproduces the wav2vec2/Adam/batch-8/patience-3 head (the section 4.3 and footnote 9 citation is in `b5_audio_neural_pilot.py` line 225), and `b5_audio_align_turns.py` uses Aeneas as the paper does. Melange's run of that same method on congressional audio failed. The fresh-21 neural EER was .226, against a bar of 5% or less. That run was source-disjoint (six distinct sources) and used weak caption labels. The paper's 0.914 is in-domain debate audio; the paper does not describe its split, and its confound warning (p.319) concerns cross-speaker and cross-time acoustic comparisons, not classifier accuracy. So the paper's accuracy is not a benchmark Melange should expect to reach. The paper recommends within-speaker pitch deviation as a comparison; it does not mention eGeMAPS or z-scores, and the sex-confound fix is Melange's own (commit 4193f0140). Its warnings about emotion models support keeping audio display-only.

## Study
**Question.** Can audio be made usable as data for political research, given the "alignment crisis" (large text and audio corpora that do not intersect at the utterance level), and which audio features (LLDs, MFCCs, learned embeddings) and classifiers are useful for speech characterization, speaker identification, and emotion/valence measurement? The paper is a methods-and-applications demonstration; it does not test a substantive hypothesis.

**Theory.** No formal theory is tested. The framing draws on the text-as-data literature (Grimmer and Stewart 2013; Wilkerson and Casas 2017) and on the claim that nonverbal audio cues (pitch, energy, intonation) carry political meaning not captured by transcripts (Section 2; Klofstad 2016; Dietrich et al. 2019; Damann, Knox and Lucas 2025). Emotion is treated as socially and politically constructed (Section 4.4, p.325), so classifier outputs are framed as starting points, not measures of emotion.

**Data.** Unit: utterance/sentence within televised U.S. presidential and vice-presidential debates. Period: 1960 to 2020 (first televised debate 1960; 2020 included). Videos: 45 (243,023 s = 4,050 min = 67.5 h). Transcripts: 47,150 sentences (38,649 from candidates; 8,501 from moderators, panelists, or audience), originally from the USElecDeb60To16 dataset (Haddadan, Cabrio and Villata 2020), taken from the Commission on Presidential Debates site and extended to 2020, with some sentence omissions fixed (Section 4, p.317). Participants: 34 candidates (16 Democrat, 15 Republican, 3 independent), 82 moderators/panelists, 59 audience members. Video source: Commission on Presidential Debates YouTube channel. Note for the record: the speaker-identification section (Section 4.3, p.321) reports 44,559 sentences and 156 speakers, which does not reconcile with the 47,150 sentence count in Section 1 (p.314); the paper does not explain the gap. Forced-alignment validation used a random subset of 500 utterances, each rated five times by crowd annotators (p.318). Emotion models were applied to the debates; their training corpora were IEMOCAP (about 12 h of acted and improvised audio, Busso et al. 2008) and MSP-Podcast v1.7 (about 84 h, Lotfian and Busso 2019).

**Method.** Four applications of increasing complexity (Section 4, Table 2). (1) Forced alignment: aeneas, which matches MFCCs of the audio to synthetic-voice MFCCs via dynamic time warping; validated by human ratings on a 1-5 scale. (2) Speech characterization: pYIN pitch (F0) in 32 ms rolling windows and RMS energy per utterance, normalized by each debate's average loudness; within-speaker pitch deviations averaged over 5 s windows (Figure 3c). (3) Speaker identification: a custom CNN on MFCCs (two blocks of convolution, max-pooling and dropout) versus a single-layer neural network on 768-dimensional wav2vec2-base embeddings (Hugging Face facebook/wav2vec2-base); Adam, learning rate 0.001, cross-entropy loss, batch 32 (MFCC) or 8 (wav2vec2), early stopping with patience 3 (footnote 9). (4) Emotion: off-the-shelf models, not retrained: a SpeechBrain wav2vec2 model fine-tuned on IEMOCAP for four classes (anger, happiness, sadness, neutral), and Wagner et al. (2023) dimensional models for arousal, dominance and valence trained on MSP-Podcast. Text-based emotion and sentiment classifiers are compared with audio-based labels as a cross-check (Figure 4d).

**Identification.** None in the causal sense. This is a measurement and feasibility paper. Validation is by human crowd ratings for alignment (Section 4.1), held-out test accuracy for speaker identification (Table 3), and reported third-party benchmark accuracy/CCC for emotion models (Section 4.4). The paper explicitly says cross-speaker comparisons are confounded by microphone placement, compression, and sampling (p.320), and recommends within-speaker shifts or speaker-dependent human validation (p.325).

**Scope.** Population: U.S. presidential and vice-presidential general-election debates, 1960-2020, all televised debate audio available on the CPD YouTube channel. Institution: candidate-moderator debate format, not legislatures. The paper argues the LLD and MFCC features are language-agnostic, but wav2vec2 embeddings and the emotion models are trained on specific languages and corpora and may not generalize across languages, accents, or cultures (Sections 3.3 and 5, pp.316, 326). Alignment and ASR context also draws on parliamentary data (UK Hansard/ParliamentTV, German Bundestag work) only in the literature review.

## Key findings
- MFCC-based forced alignment of audio to transcripts performs well on crowd ratings; about 5% of text-audio pairs were rated not aligned at all, and short or interrupted sentences drove the poor alignment. (500 utterances, 5 annotations each; percentage agreement (PA) 85.26%; distance-based agreement (DA) 95.76%; about 5% rated 1 (not aligned at all).; Section 4.1, p.318, Figure 2)
- Pitch extraction ranks candidates by average F0. Women candidates (Palin, Harris, Clinton) appear among the top five; the bottom five include Reagan, Lieberman, Edwards, Cheney and Dole. Pitch-based comparisons are presented as exploratory and confounded by recording conditions. (34 candidates; top five by average pitch include three of the four women candidates in the sample (Ferraro ranked sixth).; Section 4.2, p.319, Figure 3a)
- Normalized energy differs most across the two debates involving women candidates. Palin and Ferraro had lower average energy than their opponents (Biden, H.W. Bush). (Energy normalized by each debate's average loudness; largest energy gaps appear in the Palin-Biden and Ferraro-Bush debates (no effect size reported).; Section 4.2, p.319, Figure 3b)
- Within-speaker pitch variation is far higher in 2020 than in the 1960 Kennedy-Nixon debate, which the authors link to changing norms and technology but do not test. (Comparison of 1960 and 2020 first debates, normalized to each speaker's own baseline, averaged over 5-s windows; no statistic reported.; Section 4.2, pp.319-320, Figure 3c)
- Wav2vec2 embeddings substantially outperform MFCCs for speaker identification, and the gap widens as the number of speaker classes grows. (Individual debates: MFCC 0.888 (+/-0.085) vs wav2vec2 0.974 (+/-0.024). All 156 speakers: MFCC 0.081 vs wav2vec2 0.914 (table 0.08103 vs 0.913568). Candidates only, 34 labels: MFCC 0.134 vs wav2vec2 0.946.; Section 4.3, p.321, Table 3)
- Off-the-shelf emotion models label a large share of utterances as angry, with most of the rest neutral; sad and happy are rare. The authors say the four-label scheme is too simple. (Reported IEMOCAP four-class test accuracy of 75.3%; no per-debate figures given in text beyond the plotted percentages.; Section 4.4, pp.323-324, Figure 4a)
- Candidates with higher pitch (Clinton, Kemp) also score as more angry, and low-pitch candidates (Cheney, Lieberman) score as calmer. The paper says these contradict expert coding of Trump, who is not among the top angry candidates in the model output. (Bucy et al. (2020) expert coding of the 2016 Trump-Clinton debate: Trump anger/threatening tone 82.6% of interventions vs Clinton 35.3%.; Section 4.4, p.323, Figure 4b; contradiction cites Bucy et al. 2020)
- Dominance-arousal profiles place Clinton, Perot and Anderson at high dominance and high arousal; Lieberman and Cheney are lowest on both. No candidate stands out on valence. (Model CCC for arousal 0.744, dominance 0.655, valence 0.638 (reported for the Wagner et al. 2023 model on MSP-Podcast, p.323).; Section 4.4, pp.323-324, Figure 4c)
- Audio-based emotion labels and text-based labels only partly agree. Text emotion models mostly output neutral, while the audio model is more differentiated. Anger co-occurs with negative sentiment in 32% of cases. (Anger-negative sentiment co-occurrence 32% (as reported in text).; Section 4.4, p.324, Figure 4d)

## Limitations
- Forced alignment is poor for very short or interrupted sentences (Figure 2b; p.318).
- Cross-speaker and cross-time comparisons of pitch and energy are confounded by microphone placement, audio compression, sampling, and recording technology (pp.319-320, 325). The paper explicitly warns against naive interpretation.
- Emotion and valence outputs are not validated against human coding of these debates; the authors call for speaker-dependent human validation (p.325).
- Emotion classifiers can encode and amplify gender, ethnicity and vocal bias, possibly labeling women as angry when using neutral or assertive tones (pp.324-325).
- Four-label emotion categories are simplistic (p.324). Emotion definitions vary across individuals, circumstances and cultures (p.322).
- Training corpora (IEMOCAP, MSP-Podcast) are not debates; out-of-domain generalization is assumed from reported benchmarks, not tested here (p.322).
- Embeddings are language-specific and computationally expensive for typical researchers; MFCC/LLD features are language-agnostic but less accurate for complex tasks (pp.316, 326).
- Only U.S. presidential and VP debates are studied; the authors note the need for comparative work outside the U.S. (p.322).
- Copyright prevents sharing the videos; broadcasters gave contradictory answers on rights, and CBS quoted a licensing fee from $2,500 (footnote 4, p.314).
- Data-protection and copyright concerns apply when audio is sent to external ASR APIs such as Google or YouTube (p.314, footnote 2).
- Sentence count does not reconcile between Section 1 (47,150) and Section 4.3 (44,559); the paper does not explain the difference (pp.314, 321).
- Reported ASR error rates (WER 0.03-0.26) come from prior lab-condition validations that do not account for noise or crosstalk (Proksch, Wratil and Wackerle 2019; p.314).

- "emotion recognition models are not neutral classifiers; they can encode and amplify biases related to gender, ethnicity, and vocal characteristics" (Section 4.4, p.324)

## Gaps and flags
- Melange has no human-rated alignment validation. The paper shows a cheap protocol: 500 utterances x 5 crowd ratings, reported as PA/DA. Melange's ALIGN_MIN_TURN_WORD_MATCH 0.80 and ALIGN_MIN_HEARING_WORD_MATCH 0.70 (fec/audio_rules.py:38-39) have no calibration artifact located in the repo (whether they were ever calibrated, or the 150-clip annotation packet coded, is UNVERIFIED).
- The member eGeMAPS cache (fec/members/b5_audio_build.py:85-100) has no per-recording loudness or channel normalization. The paper normalizes energy by each debate's average loudness before any cross-speaker comparison.
- The cause of the transfer failure is undiagnosed. The paper's wav2vec2 recipe gave 0.914 on in-domain debates and EER .226 on Melange's source-disjoint split. No experiment yet tests whether channel leakage, weak labels or venue mismatch explains the gap. Training and testing within one venue/channel would separate them, but that would be a new look and needs Paul's go.
- Melange has no audio emotion layer. If one is ever proposed, the paper's evidence requires speaker-dependent human validation and a check for gender and pitch bias first. High-pitch candidates scored as more angry, and the model disagreed with Bucy et al.'s expert coding (Trump anger in 82.6% of interventions vs Clinton 35.3%).
- Do not quote the paper's 0.914 (156 speakers) or 0.946 (34 candidates) wav2vec2 accuracy as what Melange should expect. The paper measures closed-set identification within one broadcast series; Melange measures source-disjoint verification. On Melange's own fresh-21 cohort the same recipe failed at EER .226.
- The paper's internal counts do not reconcile: Section 1 gives 47,150 sentences, and the speaker-ID section gives 44,559 sentences and 156 speakers. Name the denominator whenever the speaker-ID figures are quoted.
- The idea that channel leakage explains the gap between the paper's accuracy and Melange's EER is agent analysis and is UNVERIFIED (DE-062).
- No conflict with D-021, D-025 or the rejected SNA proposals. The paper has no network, lobbying or influence content.

The paper mostly agrees with where the system has landed. On prosody, Paul is interested in non-textual channels, and the paper agrees that pitch and energy carry political signal. It also says cross-speaker readings are confounded by recording conditions and that emotion outputs are 'starting points, not definitive measures'. That supports the system's ruling that audio is display-only and that audio-as-Big-5 is closed, and it argues against a stronger use than Paul's interest might suggest. On speaker verification, Paul asked for a framework for 'contextual or auditory constraints'. This paper partly answers that request, and the system has already adopted its alignment and embedding methods. The code implements this paper's head almost exactly, and that method failed to transfer to congressional audio (EER .226).
