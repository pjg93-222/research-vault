# Fouirnaies and Hall: Electoral Incentives and Legislator Behavior

**Alexander Fouirnaies (University of Chicago, Harris School), Andrew B. Hall (Stanford University) (2022).** *American Political Science Review 116(2): 662-676. First published online 27 Sep 2021. Metadata hint confirmed against the PDF's first page; no correction needed..* [10.1017/S0003055421001064](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055421001064) Theme: [[themes/legislative-behavior-effectiveness|Legislative Behavior & Effectiveness]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Fouirnaies and Hall use term limits in 14 U.S. state legislatures (16 chambers, roughly 1991-2016) to ask whether reelection incentives change what legislators do. They compare each incumbent's binding final term with that same person's earlier terms, against same-chamber-year colleagues, using legislator and chamber-year fixed effects. In the final term, a PCA productivity index falls by 0.24 SD (a pooled estimate across 16 chambers, unstratified; N=11,109 legislator-terms). The drop ranges from -0.09 with seniority fixed effects to -0.41 with matched NY/TX controls. Roll-call participation falls 2.7 points from a mean of 90.5, bill sponsorship falls about 6% (-1.63 from a mean of 27.3 bills), and committee activity falls about 8%. Ideology does not move. |W-NOMINATE| changes by +0.02, the party-split estimates are about 0, and interest-group ratings across seven issue areas show no systematic shift. The authors read this as elections shaping visible effort, not positions. They add that legislators who leave to run for another office keep their electoral incentives, so the estimates are a lower bound.
> 
> For Paul's system, the paper matters in four places. (1) The receptivity layer cites it for a 0.60 final-term multiplier on retiring members (the docstring says 'effort/responsiveness'; the retiring list header says 'less responsive to pressure'). The paper measured effort, not responsiveness to pressure. Its null on positions argues against reading the drop as movability, and its lower-bound argument (main text, p.672) contradicts the retiring list, which includes members running for other office. (2) Several measures treat roll-call participation and sponsorship volume as dispositions or costly signals: diligence, the C criterion, the agenda component of Leg% and say-do path A. The paper shows these move with electoral status inside the same person. (3) The paper finds that effort and position separate cleanly, which supports keeping influence and alignment on separate axes. (4) Prospective test H2 cites this paper, but under it, retiring members' reduced activity can produce a correlation with no responsiveness behind it. The evidence comes from state legislatures with exogenous, binding limits. In Congress, retirement is voluntary and self-selected, so the effect transfers only weakly.

## Study

**Question:** Do electoral incentives change how legislators allocate effort to visible activities (bill sponsorship, committee service, floor attendance), and do they change ideological positioning? The paper uses term limits as the source of variation in incentives.

**Theory:** Electoral accountability models (Alt, Bueno de Mesquita and Rose 2011, reviewed in the Appendix): voters cannot observe competence directly and infer it from observable effort, so reelection-minded incumbents should allocate more effort to visible activities. Removing reelection incentives (term-limited final term) should reduce that effort. For ideology, the paper contrasts the marginality hypothesis (Fiorina 1973; Downs-style moderation), ideological shirking (Bender and Lott 1996), pandering (Canes-Wrone, Herron and Shotts 2001) and citizen-candidate models (fixed positions). Null ideological effects are read as consistent with citizen-candidate models and with voter inattention to platforms (p.671-674).

**Data:** Sample: legislator-term panel from 14 U.S. states whose legislatures have term limits of three or more terms (16 chambers: 14 houses plus the AZ and ME senates; Table 1, p.665). Nebraska (unicameral) and staggered-term senates are excluded. Coverage ranges by state (e.g., AZ 1991-2016, OH 2007-2014; Table 1). Outcome samples (Tables 2-4): roll-call attendance N=11,109 legislator-terms, 4,642 legislators; bills N=16,727, 6,207 legislators; committee activity N=16,255, 6,123 legislators (Table 4). Additional controls for Table 3 col 4: New York and Texas (non-term-limited), matched synthetic controls, N=11,416, 1,506 legislators. Scale: about 780,000 bills, over 16 million roll-call votes linked to candidates, about 6,200 legislators, and over 80,000 interest-group ratings (intro and abstract, p.662-663). Data were hand-collected from official state sources, about 100 hours per state (p.665). Election returns from Klarner et al. (2013), cleaned in Fouirnaies and Hall (2018).

**Method:** OLS difference-in-differences at the individual level (eq. 3, p.669): Productivity_ict = beta * TermLimited_ict + alpha_i + delta_ct + eps_ict, with legislator fixed effects and chamber-year (or chamber-party-year) fixed effects; robust SEs clustered by legislator. Outcome is a PCA-based productivity index (first principal component of log bills sponsored, a committee activity index, and percent of floor votes cast; normalized mean 0, SD 1; p.667). Committee index weights: 0 non-member, 1 member, 2 vice chair, 3 chair, summed across committees (eq. 1-2, p.666). Ideology: W-NOMINATE first dimension, absolute value as extremity, and party-split W-NOMINATE (Table 5, p.673). Interest-group ratings (0-100) across abortion, business, education, environment, guns, taxes, labor unions and an "other" category, estimated with legislator-by-group and group-by-chamber-year fixed effects (eq. 4, p.672). Robustness: log seniority, seniority FE, cohort-year FE, and matched NY/TX synthetic controls with match-pair-year FE (Table 3, p.670).

**Identification:** Within-person comparison: each incumbent's change in effort on entering the binding final term is compared with contemporaneous legislators in the same chamber-year who are not yet termed out, which holds fixed time-invariant individual type (Alt et al. selection problem, p.664-665). The authors note that standard leads/pretrend tests are not feasible because treated legislators have few periods (p.669), so they rely on robustness checks instead. Threats addressed: seniority/learning (log seniority, seniority FE, cohort-year FE), anticipation of own future termination and mean reversion (matched NY/TX controls with the same number of terms served, p.670 and fn 13). Heterogeneity predicted by theory (lifetime vs consecutive limits, professionalism, salary) is used as validation (p.671).

**Scope:** Population: state legislators in 14 term-limited states (16 chambers), U.S., roughly 1991-2016, low-salience state legislative elections. Institution: term limits of three or more terms. Authors argue state legislatures resemble others on key attributes (Mooney 2009), so effects may generalize (p.663), but only 14 of 99 legislatures are covered (p.665). They explicitly disclaim normative conclusions about term limits as policy and aggregate legislative effort (p.674).

## Key findings

- Termed-out (final-term) legislators have lower legislative productivity index, the main outcome. The effect is about one-quarter SD and is unchanged with chamber-party-year FE. (Table 2, p.669; text p.669). Term limited = -0.24 (SE 0.03) in both columns; N=11,109; 4,642 legislators; outcome SD 1.00
- Effect is robust to alternative counterfactuals. It shrinks with seniority controls but stays negative and significant. It is larger with matched NY/TX controls, which address both learning and anticipation. (Table 3 and text, p.670). Log(seniority) -0.23 (SE 0.03); seniority FE -0.09 (0.03); cohort-year FE -0.09 (0.03); matched NY/TX -0.41 (0.03), N=11,416, 1,506 legislators
- Floor attendance falls in the final term. Abstention rises from about 10% to 12.7% of votes, a proportional increase of about 27%. The authors say the effect is meaningfully larger than the term-length effect in Titiunik (2016). (Table 4 cols 1-2 and text, p.671). Percentage of floor votes = -2.70 (SE 0.53); -2.63 with party-year FE; outcome mean 90.48
- Bill sponsorship falls in the final term, about 6% in proportional terms. (Table 4 cols 3-4, p.671). Sponsored bills = -1.63 (SE 0.77) and -1.34 (SE 0.70) against mean 27.32; N=16,727; 6,207 legislators
- Committee activity falls in the final term, about 8% in proportional terms. (Table 4 cols 5-6, p.671). Committee activity = -0.31 (SE 0.06) and -0.30 (SE 0.05) against mean 3.84; N=16,255; 6,123 legislators
- Heterogeneity fits theory. Effects are larger under lifetime limits than consecutive limits, in more professionalized legislatures, and where salaries are higher. Effects are also larger in California and Oklahoma, where limits are cumulative. (Appendix Table A.3, summarized in text p.671 (table not in extracted text)). No numeric values in main text
- Estimates are probably a lower bound, because some termed-out legislators run for other offices and keep electoral incentives. Effects remain large in other term-limited states. (Text p.672; fn 14 p.672). No numeric values in main text
- Descriptively, legislators who serve their final term are less productive than legislators in earlier terms. Survivors to a third term are also more productive on average, which the authors treat as selection. (Figure 1, p.668 and fn 11). Figure 1 values are in the figure; no numeric values in text
- Ideological extremity (absolute W-NOMINATE) does not systematically change in the final term. The overall coefficient is marginally significant and small. Party-split coefficients are indistinguishable from zero. (Table 5 and text, p.672-673). |W-NOMINATE| = 0.02 (SE 0.01), N=4,356, outcome mean 0.63; W-NOMINATE Dem column (mean -0.62) = -0.00 (SE 0.01), N=2,192; Rep column (mean 0.52) = -0.01 (SE 0.02), N=2,143. Column identities for the party split are inferred from the outcome means.
- Interest-group ratings show no systematic shift in issue positions for either party across the seven issue areas plus other. (Figure 2, p.674; text p.673-674). Most point estimates fall between 0 and 1 percentage point on a 0-100 scale, and are statistically indistinguishable from zero (figure values not in text)
- Headline interpretation. Elections shift effort toward observable productivity but not ideological positioning. The authors argue the mechanisms are unresolved and could involve voter attention, party or interest-group influence. (Conclusion, p.674-675). None

## Limitations

- Parallel-trend and lead/pretrend diagnostics are not feasible given the small fixed number of periods for treated legislators (p.669).
- Controls are less senior than the treated legislators; if productivity-seniority is concave, estimates overstate the effect (p.668-669). Log-seniority, seniority FE and cohort-year FE partly address this, but at a cost to functional-form assumptions (p.670).
- Control legislators may anticipate their own future term limits, which would bias the comparison (p.670). Mean reversion may also contribute to the within-state estimate (fn 13, p.670).
- Party control over committee assignments and bill coordination means effects may partly reflect party strategy rather than individual choice (p.666).
- Half of the term-limited states cap bill introductions, though not stringently (p.666). Roll-call recording differs across states (p.667), though comparisons are within chamber-term.
- Data collection is manual and labor-intensive (about 100 hours per state), limiting coverage to 14 of 99 legislatures (p.665).
- Termed-out legislators may still face electoral incentives through other offices, which attenuates estimates (p.672).
- W-NOMINATE is coarse and may miss issue-specific shifts. Interest-group ratings only detect systematic shifts, not heterogeneous individual deviations (p.674). Groups select bills for their own agendas (p.672).
- The paper cannot say whether freed-up time goes to leisure or constituent service, or what happens to aggregate legislative effort (p.674). Mechanisms (voter attention, party, interest groups) are not identified (p.675).
- Normative conclusions about term limits are explicitly disclaimed (p.674). Appendix content (Tables A.1, A.3; Figures A.3-A.4) is not in the extracted text and is unverified here.

## Gaps and flags

- Retiring list conflates members leaving office with members seeking other office (header of fec/retiring_members.txt, which is the system's own label); the paper (main text, p.672) says the latter keep electoral incentives. There is no field separating the two.
- No electoral-status control on participation- or volume-based measures (diligence_z votes_cast, the agenda component of Leg%, rank-honors rate per service year, congress-activity counts). A member's final Congress or a statewide campaign mechanically lowers them.
- The 0.60 incent_mult is unestimated, and the paper offers no estimate of responsiveness to lobbying or money for final-term members. It estimates effort only, and finds positions unchanged.
- Retiring status enters susceptibility twice with opposite signs: incent 0.60 lowers pressure, while lower diligence (from more missed votes) raises susc. This interaction is undocumented.
- The system has no within-member, cross-Congress design for effort measures. The paper's identification relies on legislator fixed effects, while all system measures are cross-sectional.
- The prospective H2 contract has no reported stratum for retiring or running-for-other-office members, so activity-volume artefacts can pass as responsiveness.

## Related papers

Bucchianeri's state-level effectiveness scores are the outcome-side counterpart to Fouirnaies & Hall's effort index, and would show whether final-term effort losses reduce effectiveness.
