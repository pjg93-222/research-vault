# Most Reliable Path-finding Algorithm for Maximizing On-Time Arrival Probability

**Bi Yu Chen, Chaoyang Shi, Junlong Zhang, William H. K. Lam, Qingquan Li, Shujin Xiang (2016).** *Transportmetrica B: Transport Dynamics. Corrected from hint: the paper's own "To cite" line gives vol. 5, pp. 253-269, dated 2017 (print); online publication 13 Apr 2016. The hint's "5(3), pp. 248-264" does not appear in the text. Issue number not stated in text.* [10.1080/21680566.2016.1169953](https://doi.org/10.1080/21680566.2016.1169953). Theme: [[themes/path-finding-algorithms|Path-Finding Algorithms]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Chen, Shi, Zhang, Lam, Li and Xiang study a road-routing problem. Each link has a random travel time, and the goal is the path with the highest probability of arriving within a fixed time budget b, P(T <= b). They treat path times as normal, so this means minimizing (b - mean)/SD. Because path SD is the square root of summed variances, the objective is non-additive, and the authors state that Dijkstra-type methods cannot solve it. Their exact two-stage algorithm, MRP-TS, works as follows. A deterministic least-expected-time search first fixes the risk regime. Mean-Budget dominance with multi-criteria label-setting then finds the optimum. They check optimality by matching an independent multi-criteria A* on the same budgets. On Wuhan taxi data (19,306 nodes, 2009 morning peaks), the best path changes with b: a short budget picks a high-variance path and a long budget picks a low-variance one. MRP-TS runs in milliseconds, about 750x faster than the label-correcting benchmark on Chicago. Links are assumed independent, and the authors name correlation as the main open limitation.
> 
> What this means for Paul's system is narrow. The dashboard's recruitment chain (`make_dashboard_html_00.tpl:735-757`) cites this paper as grounding for its max-product path. That path is a different problem: the classical product-of-independent-probabilities path, which is additive in -log p and correctly solved by Dijkstra. This paper is about deadline-reliability, which is the case Dijkstra cannot handle. So the citation supports the word 'reliable', not the method. The paper's useful lessons are elsewhere. Exact optimality claims should be checked against a second algorithm, as Chen et al. do. Products of independent per-hop probabilities overstate certainty when the hops are correlated. And a true deadline or variance penalty on a recruitment chain would need label-setting, not Dijkstra. Nothing here concerns lobbying, legislators or elections.

## Study

**Question:** How can the most reliable path, the path that maximizes the probability of arriving on time for a fixed travel-time budget, be found exactly and efficiently in large road networks with stochastic link travel times? (Abstract; Sec. 1, pp. 3-5.)

**Theory:** Travel-time reliability is defined as the on-time arrival probability, P(T <= b), with path travel times treated as normal, so maximizing reliability is equivalent to minimizing z = (b - mean)/SD. The objective is non-additive, so Dijkstra-type methods do not apply. Solution logic rests on first-order stochastic dominance (FSD, Prop. 1), Mean-Budget (M-B) dominance (Prop. 3, from Chen et al. 2013a), and monotonicity of the objective under path extension (Lemmas 1-3). Risk-taking regimes (risk-averse, neutral, seeking) are set by the z-value of the least expected time path (Prop. 2, p. 8). No behavioral theory is tested empirically.

**Data:** Case study on the Wuhan, China road network: 19,306 nodes and 46,757 links. Link travel times come from floating-car GPS data from 11,248 taxis reporting about every 40 seconds, map-matched with the MDP-MM algorithm, which the authors say reaches over 90% accuracy in prior work. Data cover five weekdays, 14-18 September 2009, 7-10 a.m. (Sec. 4.1, pp. 14-15). Unit of analysis: link and path travel times, and origin-destination (O-D) pairs. Link fits use chi-square tests at 5% across normal, lognormal and gamma distributions. Monte Carlo validation uses 100 randomly selected paths. Computational tests use 100 random O-D pairs per network, averaged over 100 runs, on four additional networks from Chen et al. (2013a): Chicago regional (12,982 nodes, 39,018 links), G1 (2,000 nodes, 7,820 links), G2 (5,000 nodes, 19,700 links), and G3 (10,000 nodes, 39,600 links). The paper does not report a count of O-D pairs for the single numerical example.

**Method:** Two-stage exact algorithm, MRP-TS. Stage 1: a deterministic A* or Dijkstra search finds the least expected time path, which gives an upper or lower bound on the optimal reliability and sets the risk regime (Prop. 2). Stage 2: a multi-criteria label-setting procedure, MRP-RS for risk-seeking (fixed confidence level, Sec. 3.1) and MRP-RA for risk-averse (adaptive confidence level starting at 0.999, z = 3.5, with an admissible heuristic h(i), Sec. 3.2). Risk-neutral cases return the least expected time path directly. Optimality is argued through Props. 4-5 and Lemmas 1-3. Complexity is analyzed in Sec. 3.3. Benchmarks: the authors' improved multi-criteria label-correcting BSP-LC (Nie and Wu 2009 extended with a bi-criteria method, Appendix B, valid only for normal times), and multi-criteria A* from Chen et al. (2013a) used to check that MRP-TS returns the same paths. Implemented in Visual C#, one core of a 2.0 GHz i7 laptop, Windows 7. Identification strategy: not applicable; this is an algorithmic and computational paper with no causal design.

**Identification:** Not applicable. This is an algorithm and computational-performance paper with no causal identification strategy. Its validity rests on model assumptions: independent links, normal path times (checked by Monte Carlo on 100 paths), and the admissibility of the heuristic.

**Scope:** Population: road travelers in a congested city network, modeled as a directed graph with stochastic link times. Institution: none. Era: data from 2009 morning peaks; paper published 2016. Geography: one Chinese city plus four synthetic or benchmark networks. Restrictions: road transport only; link travel times independent; O-D pairs randomly sampled; reliability computed in model terms, not validated against observed arrivals.

## Key findings

- The most reliable path is non-additive, so deterministic shortest-path algorithms cannot solve it; FSD label-correcting (Nie and Wu 2009) generates an exponentially growing number of non-dominated paths. Numbers: none. (Sec. 2, p. 7; Sec. 1, p. 4)
- A two-stage exact algorithm (MRP-TS) first estimates the reliability range from the least expected time path, then applies M-B dominance with multi-criteria label-setting. Optimality is proven under admissible heuristics. Numbers: Adaptive confidence level starts at 0.999 (z = 3.5) for the risk-averse procedure. (Sec. 3, pp. 7-13; Props. 4-5 on pp. 10-13)
- Normal distributions fit only a minority of link travel times on their own, while lognormal fits best overall. No single distribution fits all links. Numbers: Significant fit at 5%: normal 58.85%, lognormal 81.64%, gamma 67.19%. Best-fit shares: lognormal 50.29%, gamma 30.25%, normal 19.46%. (Sec. 4.1, Table 1, p. 15)
- Link travel times in the Wuhan network are highly variable, with 10.25% of links congested (below 20 km/h). Numbers: Average coefficient of variation 0.37; 10.25% of links under 20 km/h. (Sec. 4.1, Fig. 1, p. 15)
- Normal approximation of path travel times is good enough for route guidance. Accuracy is higher at the 10th percentile than the 90th, because of positive skew, and improves with path length. Numbers: Monte Carlo ground truth on 100 paths: 98.3% accuracy at 10th percentile, 94.9% at 90th percentile. (Sec. 4.1, Fig. 2, p. 16; Conclusions p. 20)
- A worked O-D example shows the chosen path changing with the budget b: a short budget selects a high-variance path, a budget equal to the least expected time selects the mean path, and a longer budget selects a low-variance path. Numbers: b = 2301.4 s (Path 1, risk-seeking); b = 2630.9 s (Path 3, least expected time, reliability 50%); b = 2875.5 s (Path 2, risk-averse). (Sec. 4.2, Figs. 3-4, p. 16)
- Runtime falls as the risk regime moves toward risk-neutral, because M-B dominance prunes more paths near alpha = 0.5. Numbers: 275.69 ms at alpha = 0.1 vs 162.47 ms at alpha = 0.4; 18,115 vs 11,087 non-dominated paths generated. (Sec. 4.3, Fig. 5, p. 17)
- MRP-TS returns the same optimal paths as multi-criteria A* on the same budgets, which supports the optimality claim empirically. Risk-averse MRP-RA runs faster than risk-seeking MRP-RS. Numbers: none. (Sec. 4.3, p. 17)
- On the Wuhan network, the BSP-LC benchmark is invariant to budget and generates many unnecessary paths, while MRP-TS is far faster at high reliability. Numbers: BSP-LC 4,743.94 ms in total; MRP-TS 89.27 times faster at alpha = 0.9; BSP-LC generated 225,878 FSD non-dominated paths, of which 95.09% are M-B dominated relative to MRP-TS at alpha = 0.1. (Sec. 4.3, p. 18)
- Scaling test at alpha = 0.9 across five networks: MRP-TS stays in milliseconds while BSP-LC grows steeply with network size. The text reports about 750x on Chicago. Numbers: Chicago 48.26 ms vs 36,251.40 ms (text: ~750x); G1 3.12 vs 201.64 ms; G2 11.31 vs 1,182.10 ms; G3 25.63 vs 3,208.12 ms. Ratios from the table (my arithmetic, not stated in text): ~751x, ~65x, ~105x, ~125x. Chicago MRP-TS time rises ~15.5x as nodes rise ~6.5x from G1; BSP-LC rises ~179.8x. (Sec. 4.3, Table 2, pp. 18-19)

## Limitations

- Link travel-time correlations are omitted for simplicity; the paper proposes a two-level hierarchical model as a future extension (Sec. 5, p. 20).
- Only road transport is modeled; multimodal networks are left to future work (p. 20).
- The method is not extended to en-route traffic information updated during the trip (p. 20).
- Normal approximation of path travel times is only approximate, with weaker accuracy at the 90th percentile due to skew (p. 16). It was validated on only 100 Monte Carlo paths.
- Worst-case complexity is non-polynomial in theory (|P| grows exponentially); efficiency depends on the practical size of the non-dominated set in sparse networks (Sec. 3.3, p. 14).
- The main benchmark, BSP-LC, is the authors' own improved variant (Appendix B, pp. 24-25), valid only for normal path times. Runtime comparisons come from one implementation on one laptop, reported in milliseconds.
- The case study is one city, five weekday morning peaks in 2009, with floating-car data from taxis. Map-matching accuracy (over 90%) is taken from prior work. O-D pairs and budgets are randomly sampled or derived from alpha using A*.
- Optimality of MRP-RA depends on an admissible heuristic; the Euclidean and least-expected-time heuristics are the examples given (pp. 12-13).
- Reliability is an analytic probability under the model. The paper does not compare predicted on-time probabilities with observed arrivals.
- Independence of link times is assumed, and the authors note the correlated-link case is handled only by reference to prior work (p. 5).

## Gaps and flags

- The recruitment chain has no time or deadline dimension. Chen shows that the best path depends on the budget b (risk-seeking at a short budget, risk-averse at a long one), but the system cannot express 'reach the target before markup or floor time' or choose a lower-variance chain when time is ample.
- No second-path check backs the 'PROVABLY-OPTIMAL' recruitment-chain claim. Chen et al. validate MRP-TS against an independent multi-criteria A*; the dashboard has no exact solver to compare against (for example, a Dijkstra on -log p over directed PE with dropped nodes removed).
- Agent finding, UNVERIFIED (DE-062): distToTarget propagates reliability from the target outward along forward edges, while tracePath needs v-to-target reliability. Under asymmetric PE (EMU receiver multipliers, role priors) the chain is not guaranteed optimal. The dropped set is also not applied in distToTarget, so a dropped node can make the walk stall when another path exists.
- chainHold assumes the asks are independent. The source paper lists link-time correlation first among its future research directions, and recruitment asks along one chain are likely correlated, so the product is not a guaranteed floor.
- The cosponsor-route Pareto front is approximate: five lambda scalarizations of a greedy, so non-supported Pareto points are missed. An exact dominance-based labeling is not built.
- Mis-grounding in code: make_dashboard_html_00.tpl:735-739 cites Chen, Shi, Zhang & Lam (Transportmetrica B 2016) as grounding for the max-product Dijkstra-on--log-p chain. That paper solves the non-additive on-time-arrival problem and says Dijkstra cannot solve it. The algorithm the code runs is the classical product-reliability path. The citation should be re-pointed (for example to Frank 1969 or classical reliable-path theory) or reworded. It does not support the method.
- Over-claiming comment: tpl:736-737 calls the chain 'the mathematical certainty ... the exact, maximal probability of the optimal recruitment route'. The UI tooltip correctly says it is scenario output, not an empirical probability (tpl:1119). The comment should match, since chain reliability is a heuristic model output (in the spirit of D-025) built on an unestimated ASKM.
- Bibliographic discrepancy in Paul's annotation: the citation reads vol. 5, no. 3, pp. 248-264, 13 Apr. 2016. The paper's own 'To cite' line gives vol. 5, pp. 253-269, 2017 print, online 13 Apr 2016; the issue number is not stated in the text.
- No conflict with the rejected SNA proposals: the paper argues neither for dropping regular cosponsorship edges nor for per-edge volume normalization. It does not touch LDA (D-021) or reciprocity (D-104..D-108).
