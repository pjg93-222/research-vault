# Moneyed Interests, Information, and Action in Congress: A Survey Experiment

**Alexander C. Furnas, Timothy LaPira, Alexander Hertel-Fernandez, Lee Drutman, Kevin Kosar (2019).** *Prepared for the 115th Annual Meeting of the American Political Science Association (APSA), 2019. Working paper; manuscript header reads "APSR Submission Template". No journal publication is shown in the text..* Theme: [[themes/lobbying|Lobbying]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Furnas, LaPira, Hertel-Fernandez, Drutman and Kosar ran a 4 x 2 x 4 factorial vignette inside the 2017 Congressional Capacity Survey. The sample is 441 DC staffers, a 5.2% response rate (441 of 8,485), with 549 ratings per factor. Each vignette varied who made the request (constituent, campaign donor, consumer-group lobbyist or business lobbyist), what they asked for (propose a bill or stop one) and what evidence they brought (supportive polling, their own analysis, or a center-left or center-right think tank). Effects are AMCEs estimated by weighted OLS with HC3 errors. Figures 2-5 are plots, so the text gives no coefficient values.
> 
> Findings. Requester identity barely changes whether staff will take a meeting, and donors get no more meetings than constituents, which runs against Kalla and Broockman. Staff say they are less likely to use information from donors. Pooled across all staff (unstratified), think-tank evidence does little for whether staff side with the requester; the paper states the baseline inconsistently ('own analysis' in the p.12 prose, 'supportive polling' in the Fig. 4 note). Split by party, the effect appears: staff side with evidence from their own side's think tank, and evidence from the other side costs about twice what aligned evidence gains. Staff in both parties side more often with requests to stop a bill than to propose one. Everything is stated intention about a hypothetical request, and the design is underpowered. After FDR correction, 46 of 11,904 pairwise tests survive.
> 
> For Paul's systems, the paper bears mainly on the partisan source-alignment factor (cpf_pressure 1.7/0.5, whose code comment cites "Furnas/Bakker") and on the flip model's premise that money buys access. It does not calibrate either one. The paper's manipulation is the ideology of an outside requester's evidence as judged by staff, not a co-partisan legislator applying pressure. Its asymmetry also runs the other way from the system's. The system's "money is capped, info is the lever" comment misattributes a money-cap claim the paper does not make (its information half is a loose paraphrase of pp.3 and 18). The brain's "district-relevant information" line is narrower: every information arm in the vignette was constituency-specific, but aligned think-tank evidence beat constituency polling.

>
> This article by Professors Furnas, LaPira, and various others, explores the effects that the identity of a giver of political information has on the receptiveness of the recipient of said political information within the context of legislative advocacy. By using a novel survey of 400 federal congressional staffers, where the identity of the individual or interest, the nature of the request, and the source of the statistics for said recommendation are provided, the authors reveal an “epistemic closure among partisan staffers.” They make clear their recommendation that an interest group should base their findings around evidence from a think-tank that is ideologically aligned with the office they are engaging in. Put simply, the staffers want to hear only what they want to hear and will be psychologically adverse to institutions they perceive as against their interests, and hesitant towards groups that they perceive as pursuing their own goals. The implications for these findings are profound, and can be seen as an explanation for the donation behavior of large dark money funds. By channeling funds toward the building of ideological infrastructure, such groups are not recruiting their own champions, but cultivating them. Finally, in regards to the current research, allocation of venture capital funds to the growth of industry-aligned think tanks should be considered, scouted, and ultimately incorporated into future allocation models.

## Study

**Question:** Whether and how congressional staffers respond to a lobbying-style request depending on (a) the identity of the requester (constituent, donor to the Member's campaign, national consumer-group lobbyist, large national business lobbyist), (b) the action requested (propose a new bill, or stop a bill under consideration), and (c) the supporting information offered (supportive constituency polling, the requester's own analysis, or jobs evidence from a center-left or center-right think tank). Four outcomes: likelihood of taking a meeting, of using the information for the boss's recommendations, of siding with the requester in the recommendation, and of rating the requester as representative of the district or state.

**Theory:** No formal theory section; the paper states motivations and candidate mechanisms. (1) Staff act as gatekeepers and recommenders, so their choices carry weight (p.4). (2) Staff can exert independent effects on bosses' behaviour (p.4). (3) Elites, including senior staff, misperceive constituent opinion in ways that favour concentrated economic interests; the authors ask whether access alone changes staff behaviour (p.2, p.4). (4) Selective attention to information sources, driven by partisanship, may explain why Republicans respond to the well-off and Democrats to the disadvantaged (p.4). (5) Under time constraints, ideological think tanks act as heuristics for credibility and belief confirmation, and may create epistemic closure (p.16, p.18). (6) Status-quo bias and multiple veto points may explain why stop-bill requests succeed more often (p.18). The paper also proposes that donor influence may run through think tanks that fund agendas (p.3, p.17).

**Data:** 2017 Congressional Capacity Survey (CCS), an online Qualtrics survey with an embedded vignette experiment. Fielded August 9 to November 4, 2017 (p.5; Appendix A gives August to December for the survey stage). Sampling frame: LegiStorm census of 8,485 prospective respondents in Washington, DC legislative-branch offices (633 organisational units; personal, committee, leadership and other offices), excluding purely administrative units (p.5, Appendix A). Final N = 441 respondents, response rate 5.2 percent (441 of 8,485), margin of error 4.5 percent at 95 percent (Appendix A). Subsample margins of error: House 5.9 percent, Senate 7.7 percent. Policy-domain blocks: budget 125 of 412 valid (MOE 8.7), health 120 of 412 (MOE 8.9), national security 167 of 412 (MOE 7.5); 6.6 percent missing. Unit of analysis: staffer-vignette rating. Appendix C factor-level counts each total 549 (identity: constituent 131, donor 128, corporation 154, consumer 136; action: propose 279, stop 270; information: left think tank 152, right think tank 136, own analysis 123, polling 138). The text does not explain why 549 exceeds 441 respondents, so respondents presumably rated more than one vignette, but the text does not say so. Party counts for the experimental subsamples are not reported. Post-stratification weights: maximum 4.82, minimum 0.749 (p.7). Stage 1 (Feb to Jun 2017, qualitative, non-random): 52 senior staff interviews, 4 former Members and 7 former staff (Appendix A). Respondent profile: 244 offices (133 House personal, 27 House committee or leadership, 60 Senate personal, 24 Senate committee or leadership); typical respondent is a 25 to 29 year old white male mid-level legislative assistant in a majority-party office with about four years on the Hill (p.5 fn.4).

**Method:** Fully randomised 4 x 2 x 4 factorial vignette (32 possible conditions) with four dependent variables, each a five-point Likert item transformed to numeric. Following Hainmueller, Hopkins and Yamamoto (2014), the authors estimate the Average Marginal Component Effect (AMCE) of each factor level by OLS, using post-stratification weights and HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors (p.9). Subgroup models for all staff, Democratic staff and Republican staff. The preregistered design called for 32-condition pairwise t-tests; the sample was underpowered for that, so these are reported in Appendix B. Appendix B: 11,904 pairwise tests (992 pairs x 4 outcomes, plus partisan subsamples); 828 pairs nominally significant at alpha 0.10; 46 pairs survive Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction at 0.10 (Table 1). Significant pairwise differences appear only for the "recommend the boss side with petitioner" and "representative" outcomes, mostly in the partisan subsamples and mostly between opposite-valence information sources. Figures 2 to 5 plot AMCEs with baseline categories: constituent, supportive polling, propose bill. Weights: post-stratification on chamber, office type and party from the LegiStorm frame; a second weight (psweight_g) conditions on gender because of persistent gender imbalance (Appendix A).

**Identification:** Randomisation of the three vignette factors (identity, action, information) identifies each factor's effect as an AMCE, averaged over the joint distribution of the other factors (p.9). The text gives no separate identification argument. Respondent party is a pre-treatment attribute, so the Democratic versus Republican contrasts are subgroup comparisons, not randomised contrasts; the text makes no explicit claim about this. Outcomes are self-reported likelihoods for hypothetical requests, so they measure stated intention, not observed staff behaviour (the authors flag social desirability; see limitations).

**Scope:** Population: congressional staff in Washington, DC legislative-branch offices who contribute to legislative operations, 2017 (115th Congress). Institution: US House and Senate personal, committee, leadership and other offices. Era: 2017, a period the authors describe as one of rising polarisation and inequality (abstract). The sample is restricted to DC-based staff, and the authors exclude purely administrative offices (p.5 fn.1). The findings are about stated responses to a single hypothetical lobbying scenario (job-creation evidence; one policy domain at a time), not about actual lobbying outcomes.

## Key findings

- Taking a meeting (lowest-stakes outcome): most identity effects are not statistically distinguishable from zero. Democratic staff are less likely to take meetings with donors. Staff are less likely than constituents to take meetings with donors or lobbyists overall, but the authors read this as evidence that donors are not granted meetings more readily than constituents, and they flag social desirability. No relationship with information source or requested action. (Figure 2 gives AMCEs only as a plot; no point estimates in the extracted text. Dependent variable: five-point Likert, numerically transformed.; Results, Figure 2, pp.9-10)
- Using the information provided: staff report being much less likely to use information from donors, especially Democratic staff. The only statistically distinguishable Democratic-Republican gap is for evidence from a center-right think tank. Other identity, action and information effects are not distinguishable from zero. (No point estimates in the text; Democratic-Republican difference reported only qualitatively.; Results, Figure 3, pp.11-12)
- Siding with the requester in the recommendation to the boss (highest commitment): across all staff there is minimal evidence that think-tank information raises the chance of siding with the requester, and staff may be less likely to side with partisan-aligned groups than with individuals offering their own analysis. Disaggregated by party, Democrats are substantially more likely to side with center-left think tanks and less likely with center-right ones; Republicans show the mirror pattern. Offering information from a non-aligned think tank yields roughly twice the penalty of the benefit from an aligned one. ('Roughly twice' is the only ratio given; no coefficient values in the text.; Results, Figure 4, pp.12-14)
- Requests to stop a bill: both Democratic and Republican staff are more likely to side with requesters who want to stop a bill than with those proposing one. The authors offer several explanations (visibility of proposing bills, skepticism about legislation, status-quo bias from veto points, selectivity given many requests) and do not separate them. (Not given in the text.; Results p.14 and Discussion p.18; Figure 4)
- Perceived representativeness: all staff rate donors, business lobbyists and consumer lobbyists as less representative of their district or state than constituents. Exceptions: no distinguishable effect among Republican staff for donors or business lobbyists, and among Democratic staff for consumer lobbyists. Both parties rate presenters of aligned think-tank evidence as more representative than presenters of constituency polling supporting the staffer's own position. Republicans rate center-left presenters as less representative; Democrats' ratings of center-right presenters do not differ from polling presenters. (Figure 5 AMCEs are plotted only; no point estimates in the text.; Results, Figure 5, pp.14-15)
- Staff are more likely to side with donors than to meet with or use information from donors, relative to constituents. The authors say this 'merits further inquiry' and do not resolve whether donors qua donors have direct influence. (Not given in the text.; Discussion, pp.16-17)
- Headline conclusion: to influence Congress, bring policy evidence from an aligned partisan think tank. The authors read this both as evidence that staff want solid analysis, and as a warning of epistemic closure among partisan staff. (Not applicable.; Discussion and Conclusion, p.16)
- Pre-registered pairwise tests (Appendix B): only the recommend-to-side and representativeness outcomes yield significant pairwise differences after FDR correction; differences cluster in partisan subsamples where at least one information source has ideological valence. Authors describe these tests as considerably underpowered. (11,904 pairwise tests; 828 pairs nominally significant at alpha 0.10; 46 pairs significant after FDR correction at alpha 0.10 (Table 1 lists them with adjusted p-values).; Appendix B, pp.33-35, Table 1)
- Donor-meeting result contrasts with Kalla and Broockman (2016b): the authors find donors are not granted meetings more than geographic constituents, consistent with Chin, Bond and Geva (2000) but contrary to Kalla and Broockman. They note the chief of staff is the most likely staffer to grant donor meetings, citing Kalla and Broockman. (Not given in the text.; Results p.9-10; Discussion p.17)

## Limitations

- Low response rate: 5.2 percent (441 of 8,485); margin of error 4.5 percent (Appendix A, p.5).
- Underpowered design: the authors state the sample is substantially under-powered for the preregistered 32-condition pairwise tests and move to AMCEs (pp.8-9); Appendix B tests are described as considerably under-powered (p.33).
- Social desirability: staff may over-report willingness to meet constituents and under-report willingness to meet donors; the authors caution about interpreting the donor result (pp.9-10, p.16).
- Stated, hypothetical intentions rather than observed behaviour (vignette design, pp.7-8); the authors call the outcomes 'likelihood' ratings.
- Non-response bias and imbalance: Table A2 shows significant unweighted and weighted differences on several covariates (e.g., gender, number of offices, office type, some title categories); weights do not remove all of them, and gender imbalance persisted, motivating a second weight (Appendix A).
- Sample-size inconsistency: p.5 says the survey was sent to 8,458 staff, while the same page and Appendix A give 8,485 prospective respondents.
- Unexplained counts: Appendix C factor-level counts sum to 549 per factor, more than the 441 respondents; the text does not explain this (likely repeated vignettes, unstated).
- Party-subgroup counts are not reported in the text, so cell sizes for the Democratic and Republican subsamples cannot be checked.
- Position in office (e.g., chief of staff versus others) is not analysed; deferred to future research (p.10).
- Ideology is signalled through think-tank cues with brand names omitted (p.16); the authors do not test whether effects depend on brand recognition.
- Donor effects: the authors state the experiment does not reveal preferential access to donors qua donors (p.3, p.17).
- Stop-bill explanations (visibility, skepticism, status-quo bias, selectivity) are offered but not tested (p.18).
- Replication data: the Appendix A codebook link is a placeholder ('<DATAVERSE OR OTHER PERMANENT REPOSITORY TO BE MADE AVAILABLE>', p.24); replication data are not yet available in this version.
- Citation issues in the reference list: Kalla and Broockman (2016a) and (2016b) are the same article listed twice; several citations are 'n.d.' or 'in press' (Furnas n.d.; Crosson et al. in press; Furnas et al. in press; Grossmann and Isaac n.d.), so those works cannot be checked from this text.
- Figures 2-5 are plots only in the extracted text; no coefficients, standard errors or confidence intervals are available here.

## Gaps and flags

- No direction-of-request asymmetry. Staff in both parties side more with requests to stop a bill than to propose one (Fig. 4, p.18). The flip, cascade and contested-bills machinery treat converting a target to pro and to anti symmetrically: equal money and peer pull give pflip 0.5 (flip_model.py:9-10). A block-vs-advance mode is absent; fec/lobbying/contested_bills.py is shipped but not wired.
- No staffer or information layer between outside requester and Member. The system's source-alignment factor lives on Member-to-Member edges. The paper's effect sits on the outside requester's evidence as received by staff, and nothing in the engine models what evidence a CU requester brings or how aligned it is.
- No requester-identity term. The paper shows that a trade-association or business lobbyist is rated less representative than a constituent. The engine has no term distinguishing who delivers the CU ask (constituent, PAC, ACU lobbyist).
- No party-stratified calibration of cpf_pressure. The paper's pooled estimate is near null and its effects appear only within party strata, but the 1.7/0.5 constants are uncalibrated and unstratified.
- MISATTRIBUTION: fec/surfaces/templates/make_dashboard_html_00.tpl:742 and brain actors/melange-dashboard-june-capstone.md:88 credit Furnas et al. with 'money is capped, info is the lever'. The paper makes no money-cap claim; the $10k bound is FEC law. The paper tests donor identity, not dollar amounts. The 'info is the lever' half is a loose paraphrase of pp.3 and 18, not a fabrication.
- MISREADING in brain actors/melange-dashboard-june-capstone.md:72, which says staff act on 'co-partisan, district-relevant information' and that 'everything [is] reported at 95% CI ± MOE'. Every information arm in the vignette was constituency-specific, and aligned think-tank evidence outperformed constituency polling on representativeness (Fig. 5). The 4.5% MOE is survey-level and conflated with AMCE intervals, which the text does not report.
- CITATION SCOPE: the kernel docstring (fec/moneyball_core.py:11) and the dashboard align() factor (make_dashboard_html_00.tpl:585) cite Furnas for Member-to-Member co-partisan pressure (x1.7 / x0.5). The paper manipulates the ideology of an outside requester's evidence as judged by staff. Furthermore, the pooled all-staff effect is minimal and appears only within party strata (pooled-over-fault-line rule: quote by stratum or as UNSTRATIFIED). The paper's asymmetry (penalty about 2x gain) is the reverse of the system's (gain 0.7 > penalty 0.5).
- EVIDENCE QUALITY: 5.2% response rate, stated intentions about one hypothetical request, design underpowered by the authors' own account (46 of 11,904 pairwise tests survive FDR), party subgroup N unreported, replication data a placeholder. Any calibration drawn from it rests on text and code reads only (DE-062).
- Contradicts the 'access' half of the flip_model.py:21 comment ('money buys access/effort > votes ... Kalla & Broockman'), which this paper disputes for donor meetings.

Paul's highlights pick out the paper's most careful claims: donors as donors show no preferential access; staff side with evidence from their own side's think tanks; staff side more with requests to stop a bill; and taking a meeting means much less than siding with a petitioner. The built system departs from those highlights in three places. First, the brain and dashboard comments recast the paper as 'money is capped, info is the lever' (the money-cap half is absent from the paper) and 'co-partisan district-relevant information' (a narrower overstatement), claims Paul did not highlight. Second, Paul's flip-model position ('money buys access and returns') is undercut on access by the very passage he highlighted. Third, the stop-bill asymmetry he highlighted twice (pp.14 and 18) has no counterpart in the engine. His annotation entries in the input point to Furnas et al. 2022 (PRQ) and Furnas, Heaney et al. 2019, not to this paper, and record no takeaways. The 2019 working paper therefore has no written takeaway in his notes, only the 48 highlights. The highlights are current and the system-side citations are stale.
