# Who chokes under pressure (Big Five, decision-making)

**Kaileigh A. Byrne, Crina D. Silasi-Mansat, Darrell A. Worthy (2015 (print, vol. 74, pp. 22-28; online 23 Oct 2014)).** *Personality and Individual Differences, 74 (2015) 22-28. Texas A&M University. DOI 10.1016/j.paid.2014.10.009 (from the text's header)..* [10.1016/j.paid.2014.10.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2014.10.009). Theme: [[themes/adjacent|Adjacent]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Byrne, Silasi-Mansat and Worthy ran two lab experiments with Texas A&M undergraduates. Experiment 1 had N = 127, randomised to low or high social pressure. Experiment 2 had 65 recruited and 3 excluded (62 by that count, 61 implied by the regression df; unreconciled), under social plus time pressure, with no control arm. The task was a dynamic two-choice task in which picking the option that pays less now but more later is optimal. Big Five traits came from the self-report BFI. Neuroticism was the pre-specified trait, and it was the only effect that held in both experiments. In Experiment 1 it had no effect at low pressure but predicted worse performance under pressure: condition x N b = .45, p = .02; added R2 for the interaction step = .06. Experiment 2 repeated it (N b = .31, p < .05). Agreeableness was exploratory. Its Experiment 1 interaction was only a trend (p = .08); it was significant only in the uncontrolled Experiment 2 (b = .36, p < .01). The conscientiousness correlation (r = .27 under pressure) did not survive regression and did not replicate. Extraversion was null. The paper itself flags that money and social pressure are confounded, that there was no manipulation check, and that the working-memory mechanism was never measured.
> 
> Why it matters here: this is the citation behind the receptivity layer's susceptibility composite (`.40A + .30N - .20C - .10E`). It reads that composite as how much a legislator moves under lobbying pressure. The paper only partly supports that. Its outcome is worse choices on a delayed-reward task, not giving in to persuasion. Its sample is students, not legislators. N is the only pre-specified trait that replicated, while A, which gets the larger weight, is exploratory and significant only in the uncontrolled Experiment 2. The project's own test of Byrne's mechanism reportedly came back null (H5, N x loss domain, partial r = +0.036, n = 441; a project claim not re-derived in this check). So the N slot is cited to theory with no validation behind it, and should keep the UNVERIFIED label.

## Study

**Question.** Whether the Big Five personality traits predict who thrives or chokes under pressure during dynamic decision-making, i.e., which traits predict performance decrements under social pressure (Exp 1) and under combined social plus time pressure (Exp 2). Neuroticism was the a priori focus; the other four traits were treated as exploratory (Intro, p. 23; Sections 2.2.3 and 4).

**Theory.** Trait activation theory (Tett & Guterman, 2000) plus distraction theory (Lewis & Linder, 1997; Beilock & Carr, 2005; Beilock et al., 2004): pressure consumes working-memory resources; neurotic individuals experience more anxiety and intrusive thoughts, so they are more likely to choose the immediately rewarding option.

**Data.** Experiment 1: N = 127 undergraduates at Texas A&M (76 female, 51 male), course credit. Low-pressure n = 63; high-pressure n = 64 (random assignment, Section 2.1.1). Unit: individual participant, each completing 250 trials. Experiment 2: N = 65 undergraduates (37 female, 28 male), course credit (Section 3.1.1). Three participants were excluded for completion time more than 2 SD from the mean (Section 3.2.1), so the analysed N is about 62. The reported regression df F(5,55) implies N = 61; the paper does not reconcile this. Period: not stated (Psychtoolbox for Matlab v2.5 on PCs).

**Method.** Lab experiments with a dynamic two-option decision task (Gureckis & Love, 2009; Worthy et al., 2011, 2012). The Increasing option pays less immediately but raises both options' future payoffs, so it is optimal. The Decreasing option pays more immediately but lowers future payoffs. Trial-by-trial foregone payoffs are shown. Dependent variable: average proportion of Increasing-option selections over 250 trials. Personality was measured with the 44-item Big Five Inventory. Experiment 1 analysis: Pearson correlations within each condition (Table 2); a two-step hierarchical regression (Step 1: condition dummy and five traits; Step 2: condition x trait interactions), then separate regressions within each condition to locate the interaction (Section 2.2.3; Table 3). Balance check: t-tests on the five traits by condition, Bonferroni-corrected, none significant at p > .01 (Section 2.2.1). Experiment 2: same task plus a 1705 s time limit with live On Pace / Behind Pace feedback; correlations with completion time and performance (Table 4) and a multiple regression of the five traits on performance (Table 5). There was no low-pressure arm in Experiment 2.

**Identification.** Experiment 1 identifies the pressure effect by random assignment to pressure condition (social pressure: a fictitious partner and an $8 shared bonus contingent on reaching 16,000 points). Personality is measured, not manipulated, so trait effects are associations, not causal effects. Experiment 2 has no concurrent control: it is compared to the Experiment 1 control sample (Section 3.3, p. 26), and combines social and time pressure, so its trait effects are not randomized. The authors frame Experiment 2 as a replication test of the Experiment 1 neuroticism effect.

**Scope.** Population: undergraduates at one US university (Texas A&M), recruited for course credit. Era: 2014 data collection (exact dates not stated). Institution: university laboratory. Domain: individual (not group) decision-making in one laboratory task. The authors say results may be limited to the decision-making domain and may not extend to other high-pressure situations (Section 4.1).

## Key findings

- Exp 1 condition x neuroticism interaction: neuroticism had no significant relation to performance in the low-pressure condition but negatively predicted performance under social pressure. (Interaction DR2 = .06, F(11,115) = 1.96, p = .04; condition x N b = .45, p = .02; high-pressure N b = .50, p < .01; low-pressure N not significant.; Section 2.2.3; Table 3; Section 4)
- Exp 1 main effects: pressure lowered performance; neuroticism negatively predicted performance in the first-step model. The first-step model only approached significance. (Step 1 R2 = .10, F(6,120) = 2.14, p = .054; condition b = .43, p = .02; neuroticism b = .25, p = .01; conscientiousness b = .16, p = .09 (trend).; Section 2.2.3; Table 3)
- Exp 1 correlations within condition: no trait correlated significantly with performance in the low-pressure condition; under pressure, conscientiousness and neuroticism were negatively correlated with performance. The conscientiousness link did not survive the regression and was not replicated in Exp 2. (High pressure: C r = .27, p < .05; N r = .34, p < .01. Low pressure: none significant.; Section 2.2.2; Table 2; Sections 2.3 and 3.3)
- Exp 1 agreeableness interaction was a non-significant trend. Higher agreeableness was associated with lower performance under pressure, but not significantly. (Condition x A b = .33, p = .08 (trend); high-pressure A b = .18, p = .17 (ns).; Section 2.2.3; Section 2.3)
- Exp 2 (social plus time pressure) replicated the neuroticism effect. Neuroticism and agreeableness both negatively predicted performance; the overall model was significant. Conscientiousness did not predict performance under time pressure. (Model R2 = .19, F(5,55) = 2.61, p < .05; N b = .31, p < .05; A b = .36, p < .01; O, C and E not significant (Table 5 reports b = .16, .16, .06 without significance marks).; Section 3.2.2; Table 5; Section 3.3)
- Exp 2 correlations: agreeableness negatively correlated with performance; neuroticism and conscientiousness were negative but non-significant; completion time correlated strongly with performance. (A r = .29, p < .05; N r = .16 (ns); C r = .16 (ns); completion time x performance r = .62, p < .01.; Section 3.2.1; Table 4)
- Exp 1 post-task importance ratings did not differ significantly by condition; the low-pressure group rated performance importance slightly lower. (Low M = 4.68 (SD 2.42); high M = 5.17 (SD 2.14); not significant.; Section 2.2.1, footnote 1)
- Authors' overall conclusion: neuroticism is consistently linked to choking under pressure in this task; agreeableness is an unexpected exploratory finding; the conscientiousness result is not supported. (No new numbers; summarizes the above.; Section 4 (general discussion, pp. 26-27))

## Limitations

- Social pressure included a monetary incentive absent from the low-pressure arm, so monetary and social pressure are confounded (Section 4.1).
- Neuroticism group means differed between Experiment 1 conditions (low-pressure higher); the authors say this could have skewed results (Section 4.1).
- No pressure manipulation check, anxiety or mood measure, or working-memory capacity measure; the working-memory mechanism is inferred, not tested (Section 4.1).
- Between-subjects design with no within-subject control of group differences (Section 4.1).
- Self-reported pressure did not differ significantly between conditions, and the low-pressure mean was higher than in earlier studies (Section 2.2.1, footnote 1).
- Experiment 2 has no concurrent control; it is compared to the Experiment 1 control sample (Section 3.3).
- The conscientiousness effect appeared in Experiment 1 correlations but not in the regression, and did not replicate in Experiment 2 (Sections 3.3 and 4).
- The agreeableness finding is unanticipated and labelled exploratory (Section 4); its Experiment 1 interaction was not significant.
- Sample: undergraduates at one university, individual decisions only; generalization limited (Section 4.1).
- Reporting inconsistencies: Experiment 2 mean completion time is 1699 s in the text but 1670 s (SD 55.73) in Table 4; the text's 2 SD cutoff of 292 s does not match Table 4's SD. The Experiment 2 regression df F(5,55) implies N = 61, not the about 62 implied by excluding 3 of 65.
- Footnote 2: the 1705 s limit required responses about 25% faster (about 500 ms per trial) than the Experiment 1 average of about 680 ms (Section 3.1, footnote 2).

## Gaps and flags

- The paper's construct is worse decisions under pressure, not being moved by a persuader. The system has no evidence connecting the two. The prospective H2 contract (Spearman of pressure_mult vs |tier movement|) is still the only planned test, and F5 found it underpowered (72 of 511 new-event members).
- No anxiety or stress-state measure exists for members at the time they are lobbied. Byrne's effect appears only under pressure. The system applies trait N to every member all the time; the loss-domain flag is the only situational switch, and H5 found no N x loss interaction.
- The ±30% susc_mult range and the .40/.30/.20/.10 weights have no anchor in effect sizes. The paper's trait effects are small (added R2 = .06; model R2 .10 to .19), and the ordering is set by owner framing, not evidence.
- The paper's single replicated finding is N. The system's default behavioural basis puts its largest weight (.40) on cooperation_v2, which nearly duplicates bpf (rho .888). bpf already enters the targeting product, so the slot that carries the most weight has the least Byrne support.
- Citation mislabel at fec/members/receptivity_layer.py:7-8. The docstring calls the agreeableness effect 'their Exp-2 interaction'. Exp 2 had no low-pressure arm, so no interaction could be tested. The only A interaction was in Exp 1, and it was a non-significant trend (p = .08).
- Ordering conflict. The paper's evidence ranks N (pre-specified, replicated) above A (exploratory, uncontrolled). The composite weights A .40 above N .30 on owner framing. That is a legitimate owner choice, but the Byrne label at :189 and the provenance string at :233 suggest the paper backs it.
- N-slot status: the project's Byrne-mechanism test (H5, n = 441) was NULL, and genre transfer failed. Any statement that receptivity 'implements Byrne' must carry the UNVERIFIED label (DE-062), and must not be described as validated susceptibility.
- Coefficient signs in the extracted paper text are printed without minus signs (for example 'N b = .25'). Direction here is taken from the authors' prose ('negatively predicted'). Check against the PDF before quoting a sign.

Paul did not annotate this paper. The code-comment framing of the receptivity layer (not a stated Paul position) is that psychological traits should adjust how a member responds to pressure, as edge parameters. Byrne agrees with that idea in principle: a trait-by-situation interaction exists for N. It disagrees on three specifics. Third, the C and E dampeners are not Byrne findings, and the C evidence runs the opposite way.
