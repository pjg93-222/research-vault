# Feasibility of Big Data Analytics to Assess Personality Based on Voice Analysis

**Víctor J. Rubio, David Aguado, Doroteo T. Toledano, María Pilar Fernández-Gallego (2024).** *Sensors 24(22), 7151 (MDPI, Basel). Received 30 Sep 2024; accepted 4 Nov 2024; published 7 Nov 2024. Open access, CC BY 4.0..* [10.3390/s24227151](https://doi.org/10.3390/s24227151). Theme: [[themes/personality-psychometrics|Personality & Psychometrics]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Rubio, Aguado, Toledano and Fernandez-Gallego (Sensors 2024) recorded about 7-minute mock job interviews with Spanish women, nearly all students. They extracted 1,941 OpenSMILE AVEC-2011 acoustic functionals and correlated them with Big Five scores from three sources: NEO-FFI self-report, close-other ratings and zero-acquaintance expert ratings. In Sample 1 (n=78), the best single feature reached r of about 0.30-0.48 per trait. Expert ratings tracked voice most closely (E 0.48, N -0.47). The paper also says N=100, but its two samples add to 122.
> 
> The key result is negative: the top features did not replicate in Sample 2 (n=44). The authors say two different microphones may have contributed, alongside noise, colds and fatigue. For prediction, the top five features fed a random forest that sorted people into low, medium or high on each trait, scored by leave-one-out. Self-report accuracy was 43-53%, mostly at or below the 50% you get by always guessing "medium" (Agreeableness at 53% is slightly above it). Expert ratings did best, at 53-60%. The paper never says whether the top five features were chosen inside each fold, so even those figures may be optimistic. The Discussion's numbers also contradict the Results (0.38/0.40 vs 0.47/0.48).
> 
> For Melange, the paper backs the audio lane's existing verdict rather than reopening it. The lane uses a related toolkit (eGeMAPS v02 from openSMILE), and its audio Neuroticism (N) pilot was a MISS. Audio is display-only, and the engine never reads it. The paper adds two concrete cautions. First, feature-level findings did not replicate across two samples recorded on different microphones. Second, a sample of one sex hides the sex confound in pitch variability. Melange measured that confound, but the fix is on a side branch, not main (see flags).

## Study

**Question:** Three questions stated on p.5 (Introduction, lines 266-286): (1) Are there voice features related to self-reported Big Five scores (externalization)? (2) Are the voice features related to externalization the same as those related to the accuracy of close-other and expert judgments? (3) Can personality dimensions be predicted from voice recorded with ordinary devices in controlled but not restricted speech, so the approach could fit Asynchronous Video Interviews and personnel selection?

**Theory:** Brunswick lens model as adapted by Scherer (1978): traits are externalized through objectively measurable speech variables (distal cues), perceived by listeners as proximal cues that ground attributions (p.2, lines 80-86). Brown and Bradshaw (1985) three facets: accuracy, externalization, attribution; this study tests externalization and accuracy (p.5, lines 258-265). Big Five framework; the authors also draw on the Cannata et al. (2022) call to join psychological personality-judgment research and personality computing (p.3, lines 156-163). Authors argue speech reveals the self (Allport and Cantril 1934; Allport 1937) (p.5, lines 238-239). No formal hypothesis is stated; the authors describe the study as exploratory.

**Data:** Unit: individual participant (interview recording). N = 100 female participants, split into Sample 1 (n = 78 female psychology students, aged 18-27, M = 20.18) and Sample 2 (n = 44 female participants, aged 18-54, M = 29.42) (p.5, lines 289-292). NOTE: the two sample sizes sum to 122, not 100; the text does not reconcile this. Recruitment: the first author's School of Psychology participant pool at Universidad Autonoma de Madrid, students registering for extra course credit (p.5, lines 296-299). Recording period: not stated; the only date is the IRB approval of June 2016 (p.17, lines 1311-1313). Procedure: group session (60-item NEO-FFI, Spanish adaptation), individual interview in the following two weeks, about 7 min per session (p.7, lines 402-410). Audio: WAV, 16 kHz, mono, 16-bit; manually selected open-answer segments, mean 172.8 s (SD 102 s), range 49 s to 15 min; total 4 h 48 min (p.7, lines 414-430). Two different microphones were used for Sample 1 and Sample 2 (p.16). Data available only on request, for privacy reasons (p.17, line 1315). Expert rating subset: N = 20 videos for inter-rater ICC (p.7).

**Method:** Voice features: the AVEC 2011 emotion feature set extracted with OpenSMILE 2.3, 1941 features (25 energy/spectral LLDs x 42 functionals, 6 voicing LLDs x 32, 25 delta energy/spectral x 23, 6 delta voicing x 19, 10 voiced/unvoiced durations) (p.6, lines 311-323). Analysis 1 (externalization and accuracy): Pearson-type correlation of all 1941 features with each Big Five score from three criteria (self-report, close-other rubric, expert VAS), then reporting the top features per trait in Sample 1 (Table 4). Replication: the top-correlated self-report feature per trait was re-examined in Sample 2 (Section 3.1.6). Analysis 2 (prediction): for each of five traits, the five highest-correlated features were used; scores were binned into low/medium/high at the 25th and 75th percentiles of training data; random forest classifier (scikit-learn, n_estimators = 20, other defaults); leave-one-out with 100 folds (99 train / 1 test). SVM and multiple linear regression were tried but not reported because random forests did better (p.13, lines 1005-1010). Software: Python with Pandas, SciPy, scikit-learn (p.8). The text does not say whether the five features were selected inside each LOO fold or on the full sample; this is a reviewer note, not a stated fact, and it bears on whether the reported accuracies are out-of-sample. The text also does not name the correlation type or any multiple-comparison correction across 1941 features.

**Identification:** None in the causal sense. The design is cross-sectional: correlations between acoustic features and three personality criteria (Sample 1, with Sample 2 as a replication for self-report), then leave-one-out random-forest classification on N = 100. No instrument, quasi-experiment or manipulation. Out-of-sample validity depends on whether feature selection was inside each fold, which the text does not state.

**Scope:** Population: adult female Spanish participants recruited at one Madrid university; Sample 1 is mostly students aged 18-27. Institution: Universidad Autonoma de Madrid (School of Psychology and Polytechnic School of Engineering). Era: collection date not stated; published 2024. Setting: semi-structured interview simulating a job interview (warm-up questions, then self-description, career, and a 15-year future question; p.7 lines 418-424). Sex: all participants are female, which the authors treat as a sample restriction (p.16). Constructs: Big Five self-report, close-other judgments, and zero-acquaintance expert ratings from video plus audio.

## Key findings

- Close-other ratings correlate with self-report on all Big Five dimensions except Openness. Expert ratings match self-report only for Conscientiousness and Extraversion. Numbers: Close-other vs self, significant range 0.32 (Neuroticism) to 0.51 (Extraversion). Expert vs self: 'Responsibility' 0.34 and Extraversion 0.31; both significant, the rest not. (Section 3.1, Table 3, p.8-9 (lines 444-451))
- Single voice features correlate moderately with personality ratings in Sample 1. The top correlation per trait and criterion is given in Table 4. Numbers: Self: Extraversion 0.47 (mfcc_sma[3]_lpgain), Neuroticism 0.37, Openness 0.36, Agreeableness -0.33, Conscientiousness 0.30. Experts: Extraversion 0.48, Neuroticism -0.47, Conscientiousness 0.44, Openness -0.36, Agreeableness 0.35. Close others: Openness -0.41, Agreeableness -0.41, Neuroticism 0.32, Extraversion 0.40 (text says 0.40 in one sentence and 0.41 in the next), Conscientiousness -0.30. (Section 3.1 and Table 4, p.8-12 (lines 452-462, 806-867))
- Expert ratings showed the strongest voice correlates; the authors read this as expert judgments tracking voice cues more than close-other judgments do. Numbers: Largest correlations with acoustic features came from the non-acquainted expert. Expert Extraversion accuracy 60% was the best all-cases cell (Table 6); Expert Neuroticism medium-trait accuracy at 81.1% is the highest single cell. (Discussion, p.14-16 (lines 1155-1160, 1225-1228))
- The feature-level correlations did not replicate across samples. Sample 2 (self-report only) gave different features, although of similar magnitude. Numbers: Sample 2 top features: Neuroticism mfcc_sma[1]_percentile99.0 r=0.52; Extraversion F0final_sma_quartile3 r=0.46 (p=0.002); Openness mfcc_sma[3]_kurtosis r=0.52; Agreeableness mfcc_sma[2]_skewness r=-0.46 (p=0.002); Conscientiousness mfcc_sma_de[7]_kurtosis r=-0.53. Authors say the results 'did not coincide'. (Section 3.1.6, p.12-13 (lines 981-998, 991-993))
- Three-class (low/medium/high) classification from the top-5 voice features gives overall accuracy above the 33% chance rate for all 15 criterion-trait cells, but mostly not above the 50% majority-class baseline for self-report (Agreeableness at 53% is above it). Numbers: All-cases accuracy (%): Self N 50, E 46, O 46, A 53, C 43. Expert N 57, E 60, O 57, A 56, C 53. Close others N 54, E 57, O 45, A 52, C 54. Chance 33%; a constant central-class prediction gives 50% because the central band holds 50% of cases (p.14). (Section 3.2, Table 6, p.13-14 (lines 1009-1020, 1109-1120))
- Within-class accuracy is highest for medium-trait cases and lower for low and high cases. Some cells are extreme: the Conscientiousness high-trait cell for self-report is 4.1%. Numbers: Medium-trait accuracy ranges 47.4% to 81.1%; examples: close-other Neuroticism medium 76.4%, expert Neuroticism medium 81.1%. Self-report Conscientiousness high 4.1%, Extraversion high 18.2%. Close-other Conscientiousness low 0%, high 64.3%. (Table 6, p.13; text p.14 (lines 1121-1127))
- Expert zero-acquaintance rating reliability was moderate to strong on four dimensions and low on Openness. This rating is the criterion that the authors say voice predicts best. Numbers: ICC on N=20 videos: Emotional Adjustment 0.70, Extraversion 0.73, Agreeableness 0.74, Conscientiousness 0.82, Openness 0.23. Experts discussed disagreements and re-rated Openness. (Section 2.2.2, p.7 (lines 392-401))
- The Discussion's numbers do not match the Results. It says the highest trait correlations were Neuroticism 0.38 and Extraversion 0.40, and that the predictions range 0.3 to 0.4. Results and Table 4 report 0.47 to 0.48 for Extraversion. Numbers: Discussion: N 0.38, E 0.40. Results: E 0.47 (self), 0.48 (expert). Text claims all correlations p<0.001 with a maximum of 0.50, but Table 4 shows p = 0.0013, 0.002, 0.004 and 0.01 for some cells and a maximum of 0.48. (Abstract p.1 (lines 45-47); Discussion p.14 (lines 1129-1132); Results p.8-10 (lines 459-460, 466, 475))
- The authors compare the voice results with facial-expression work and conclude voice alone is more limited; they call personality prediction a multimodal task. Numbers: Voice best case about 60% accuracy vs over 75% for facial-expression work by Gavrilescu (ref 56, cited as [56]). (Discussion, p.14 (lines 1138-1144))

## Limitations

- Sample: 100 participants, all female, mostly psychology students (age 18-27 in Sample 1). Authors say the group is homogeneous in age and personality, so extreme trait values are unlikely, limiting external validity (p.16).
- Effect sizes are moderate and may not hold in larger or more varied samples; the authors say they should be read cautiously (p.16).
- No cross-cultural sampling; the authors say cultural norms may change how traits are externalized through voice (p.16).
- Confounders: noise in some recordings; two different microphones for Sample 1 and Sample 2, which the authors say may explain the lack of replication; colds, fatigue and emotional state could act as latent variables (p.16).
- Only vocal aspects were analyzed. Fluency (pauses, discontinuities), morphology/syntax, conversational behavior, facial expression, body language and movement were not (p.17).
- Feature interpretability is weak: most top features are MFCC or LPC coefficients whose meaning is unclear, and the top features did not replicate across samples (pp.12-15).
- No explicit power analysis. The authors say N = 100 gives 80% power for correlations near 0.2 (p.5, lines 300-304). Many features (1941) raise the risk of spurious correlation; the authors used two samples to check consistency (p.5).
- Expert Openness reliability was low (ICC 0.23 on 20 videos) (p.7).
- Internal inconsistencies found in the text, not reconciled by the authors: Sample sizes 78 + 44 = 122 vs N = 100; Discussion correlation values (0.38/0.40) vs Results (0.47/0.48); Extraversion close-other r given as both 0.40 and 0.41; 'all p < 0.001' vs Table 4 p-values up to 0.01; 'one of two experts' vs both experts rating videos (p.7).
- Data are available only on request for privacy reasons, so the results cannot be reproduced from the paper alone (p.17).
- Prediction set-up: the text does not say whether feature selection (top-5 by correlation) was done inside each leave-one-out fold; this must be checked before accepting the 43-60% accuracies as out-of-sample.

## Gaps and flags

- The sex-confound fix for pitch_variability_raw (stddevNorm*amean, commit 4193f0140, 2026-08-21) is on branch claude/melange-speech-recognition-cfa049, not on main. fec/members/b5_audio_descriptors_candidate.py:217 still emits stddevNorm alone, a metric Melange itself measured as sex-loaded (r=-0.266). Verification confirmed this by git ancestry and a file read.
- There is no explicit channel or microphone stratum. Rubio's non-replication across two microphones suggests eGeMAPS features should be checked for stability within member across recording channels, or z-scored within venue. The brain lists channel as uncontrolled.
- Melange has no test of whether vocal descriptors match how listeners perceive members, as opposed to self-reported traits. That is where Rubio found the strongest signal (expert ratings). The system has no rater panel for audio-perceived persona.
- Do not cite Rubio's 43-60% accuracies as evidence that voice predicts personality. For self-report they mostly do not beat the 50% majority baseline. Fold placement of feature selection is unstated. There is no multiple-comparison correction across 1,941 features.
- The paper is internally inconsistent: N=100 vs 78+44=122; Discussion r 0.38/0.40 vs Results 0.47/0.48; 'all p<0.001' vs Table 4 p values up to 0.01. Quote figures only from the Results tables, with the sample named.
- Every figure comes from a homogeneous Spanish sample of 100 women in mock job interviews, with a recording date not stated. Mark any transfer to members of Congress as UNSTRATIFIED by sex and channel.
- The audio N route is closed (look spent 2026-08-25). This paper does not justify reopening it, and a new audio-trait look would need a fresh pre-registration under the single-look discipline.
- The missing-fix finding (4193f0140 not an ancestor of HEAD) is reported here, not repaired: the repo is read-only for this task.

Voice alone reached only about 60% at best, and feature-level correlations did not replicate across the two samples. Overall, the evidence sides with the system's conclusion (audio is display-only; the audio N pilot MISSed) more than with an expectation that voice will yield trait scores.
