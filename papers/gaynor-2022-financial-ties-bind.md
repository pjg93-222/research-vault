# The (Financial) Ties That Bind: Social Networks of Intraparty Caucuses

**SoRelle Wyckoff Gaynor (2022).** *Legislative Studies Quarterly 47(4), November 2022, pp. 885-920 (metadata hint 885- corrected; end page from running headers, Supporting Information note printed on p.920).* DOI: [10.1111/lsq.12360](https://doi.org/10.1111/lsq.12360). Theme: [[themes/social-network-analysis|Social Network Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Gaynor builds the member-to-member Leadership PAC (LPAC) donation network for every House member in the 115th Congress (450 nodes, 12,870 directed donation edges) and the 116th Congress (collected partway through the session). Each party is modelled as its own network, because cross-party LPAC giving is almost zero: one cross-party donor in the 115th. Ties are binary and the models are ERGMs. Within each party, same-caucus ties are more likely for some caucuses but not others. In Model A, Freedom Caucus is +1.208*** and Tea Party +0.686*** (Table 2), and Progressive is +0.381 (Table 3, significant only at p<0.1). Tuesday Group (-1.033***) and Liberty (-0.389*) are negative, and New Dems and Blue Dog are not significant. In the Republican network, members outside a caucus withhold money from Freedom and Tea Party members. Republican leaders give to 86% of members but to only about 30% of Freedom Caucus members (Table 4). Democratic leaders still give to every caucus. Across both parties, vulnerable members (Cook PVI 'likely', October 2018) are about 70-75% more likely to receive a tie, and announced retirees about 40-45% less likely (the paper's own text conversions from log-odds, pp.903-904, not table coefficients). The design is observational. Weights are ignored, and the text and tables disagree in places: the DW-NOMINATE result in Table 4 versus the p.907 text, and the 116th-Congress replication.
>
> For Paul's systems, this paper describes where money goes. It is not evidence that money moves anyone. It supports Cook PVI as the vulnerability measure that donors act on. It does not support the system's reciprocity claim (D-104..D-108) that a dollar buys more in a competitive seat. Its clearest lesson is structural. Melange treats all co-partisans the same and has no data on the main ideological intraparty caucuses. The scraper's caucus list covers CU-relevant policy caucuses and scores leaders only. The caucus research experiment has membership data for Problem Solvers only (it also has prior-Congress leadership features for eight caucuses from official lists). Mentat has code that reads FEC leadership-PAC sponsor and candidate-committee link files, but its default cycles (2022, 2024, 2026) do not cover the 2018 and 2020 cycles Gaynor's windows need, and whether the bulk files are on disk was not checked. Any such network would be a donation-tie layer, not a measure of closeness or influence (D-025).

> This article outlines the connection between campaign donations by members of Congress to members of various caucuses, whether shared or not. It applies an Exponential Random Graph Model to a Social Network Analysis. The data collection methods are highly valuable for presenting the research through the following methods: overlaying caucus partisanship using the mean DW-Nominate score of all members within said caucus, using Cook's Partisan Voting Index to measure electoral vulnerability, and using LPAC donations as a directed edge relationship. This article offers several important insights into how to structure the edges, nodes, and data infrastructure for the present research, particularly in representing the members themselves as nodes using DW-Nominate. The main weaknesses within this article are gaps in applying this method to committee action, voting, and bill co-sponsorship, all of which are directly relevant to the current research.

## Study

**Question.** Do intraparty caucus memberships shape member-to-member Leadership PAC (LPAC) donations in the US House, and how do party leaders' LPAC giving decisions relate to caucus membership?

**Theory.** Intraparty caucuses give rank-and-file members ideological branding and bargaining leverage against party leaders (pp.888-889). Caucuses also build relationships that make financial ties more likely (pp.889, 892). H1: members are more likely to donate to members of a shared caucus and less likely to donate to outsiders; nonmembers are less likely to donate to caucus members (pp.891-892). H2: congressional leaders are less likely to give to members who join an intraparty caucus, controlling for vulnerability (p.892). Null hypotheses: no monetary difference between caucus members and nonmembers, and no leader-caucus relationship (p.892). Existing LPAC theory (Currinder 2003) predicts giving by majority or minority status, not caucus membership (pp.888, 891).

**Data.** Original dataset of every House member in the 115th Congress (2017-2019) and 116th Congress (2019-2021). Unit of analysis: member-to-member LPAC donation dyad, from OpenSecrets.org compiled from FEC records. 450 nodes in the 115th Congress, of which 417 are connected by at least one LPAC tie (38 unattached). In the 116th Congress 398 members are connected, including 101 new members; its LPAC data was collected January 27, 2020, midway through the session (fn.5). 115th Congress has 12,870 unique directed edges. Only edges between two members (or a member and a member LPAC) are kept; 282 members headed an LPAC and 373 received money from one (p.890). Caucus membership: Congressional Yellow Book archives (self-reported; fn.6). Eight active intraparty caucuses (five Republican, three Democratic): Freedom 26, Liberty 10, Tea Party 24, RSC 153, Tuesday Group 14; Progressive 72, New Democrat Coalition 57, Blue Dog 18 (Table 1, p.894). Vulnerability: Cook PVI 'likely' category as of October 2018, dichotomous (fn.8). Retirement: announced retirement dummy. Party-leader model adds DW-NOMINATE distance. Models use 238 connected Republican nodes and 168 connected Democratic nodes for 115th (Tables 2-3 notes); 175 R and 188 D nodes for 116th (Tables A1-A4).

**Method.** Descriptive social network analysis (raw and weighted degree, transitivity; Figures 1-2 Fruchterman-Reingold layouts) on each party separately (pp.895-899). Exponential Random Graph Models (ERGM) estimated with MCMC in the R ergm package (Hunter et al. 2008; Cranmer and Desmarais 2011; Snijders et al. 2006), treating ties as binary and ignoring edge weight (p.899, fn.17). Member-to-member models (Tables 2-3) use four specifications: A both caucus members; B both noncaucus; C donor in caucus, recipient not; D recipient in caucus, donor not; each caucus is entered separately and multi-caucus members get one term per caucus (pp.900-903). Party-leader models (Tables 4-5) are directed ERGMs on leaders' outward ties, with DW-NOMINATE distance from the leader mean (pp.899, 905-907). Mutual reciprocity term included. Only Tables 2-3 are described as undirected analysis (p.900-901).

**Identification.** No causal identification claimed. Associations are conditional on covariates (caucus dummies, leadership, vulnerability, retirement), an endogenous reciprocity term, and separation of parties into two networks to limit partisan homophily and ERGM degeneracy (pp.899-901). Robustness: 116th Congress (p.908, Tables A1-A4), shared home state check (fn.19), GWESP triad controls infeasible (fn.20), leader models with and without DW-NOMINATE distance (p.907, p.908-909).

**Scope.** US House of Representatives only (chamber where caucuses are more prominent than in the Senate, p.893). Population is every House member in two consecutive Congresses (115th-116th), including retirees and resignees. Era: post-1995 centralization of party power (pp.888-889). LPAC giving only, not party committee or PAC giving. Cross-party giving is near zero: one cross-party donor in the 115th Congress (fn.10).

## Limitations

- Binary ERGM ignores donation amounts; the author flags XERGM as an unused alternative (p.899, fn.17).
- Caucus membership relies on self-reported Yellow Book entries; non-reporting members may be misclassified (fn.6).
- Liberty and Tea Party caucuses may be defunct after 2014 per Clarke (2020), though members reported membership through 2020 and 2018 (fn.7).
- Vulnerability is a dichotomous 'likely' PVI indicator measured in October 2018 only (fn.8).
- GWESP triad controls were computationally infeasible; degeneracy handled by party split and reciprocity term (fn.20).
- 116th Congress tables omit vulnerability and retirement controls and use data collected January 27, 2020, midway through the session (Tables A1-A4 notes; fn.5).
- Text claims 116th results hold across all models, but Table A3 Freedom leader effect is not significant and Table A2 Blue Dog Model A is -1.315***; consistency is partial.
- Text on p.907 says Republicans are less likely to donate to members who veer from party center; Table 4 DW-Nom distance is -0.838 with no significance marker and p.909 says no impact; the two statements conflict.
- Table 5 retirement coefficient -0.055 is marked *** with SE 0.102, which is internally inconsistent.
- Tables 2-3 are labeled undirected analysis, while the data section describes a directed edgelist (p.894-900).
- Design is observational; ties are conditional associations, not causal effects of caucus membership. Effects on electoral outcomes are not estimated; the $3,682.19 figure is a converted average donation, not an electoral effect.
- Only two Congresses and only LPAC giving are analyzed; the author notes party committee and cross-chamber ties are out of scope (p.911).
- Caucus-level sample sizes are small (Liberty 10, Tuesday Group 14 members) and some caucus terms are not significant.

## Gaps and flags

- There is no dated roster of ideological intraparty caucuses (Freedom, RSC, Tuesday Group/Main Street, Progressive, New Dems, Blue Dog) anywhere in the system. The cosponsor_institutions experiment's membership block is Problem Solvers only: 54 named people for the 117th and 63 for the 118th (outputs/cosponsor_institutions_20260905/REPORT.md:13). Adding caucus evidence was inconclusive there (-9 correct selections, REPORT.md:96), so the faction hypothesis this paper supports has not yet been tested in the system.
- The influence kernel has no intraparty faction structure. Every co-partisan gets cpf_pressure 1.7, but the paper shows Republican financial ties split along faction lines and leaders punish the Freedom Caucus.
- No member-to-member LPAC donation layer exists. Mentat has code that reads leadership-PAC sponsor and candidate-committee link files, but its default cycles (2022, 2024, 2026) miss the 2018 and 2020 cycles the paper's windows need, and bulk file presence was not checked.
- Two vulnerability measures (Ballotpedia Election_Average for leverage, Cook PVI for c) feed the engine side by side, and nothing tests which one tracks the giving behaviour the paper documents.
- D-025: Paul highlighted the line that LPAC data captures 'financial information for what it is: relationships'. If an LPAC layer is ever built, its ties and any ranks derived from them are donation records and heuristic model outputs. They must not be read as closeness, access or introduction probabilities.
- D-104..D-108: The paper shows that vulnerable members receive more co-partisan money. It does not show that dollars buy more in competitive seats. Citing it as support for the reciprocity assumption in COMPET_MULT would be wrong, and reciprocity stays NOT validated. The ERGM's mutual-reciprocity term is a network control (A gives to B, B gives to A). It is a different concept from the system's money-reciprocity claim.
- Paul's FreeText note on p.4 asks 'How does this translate into an edge?'. In the paper, an edge is any member-to-member LPAC donation in one Congress, directed and binary, with amounts ignored (p.899, fn.17). It records a gift, not influence. Building it would be new analysis.
- Internal inconsistencies to cite with care. The DW-NOMINATE distance coefficient in Table 4 (-0.838, no significance marker) conflicts with the p.907 text. The Table 5 retirement coefficient is marked *** with SE 0.102. The text says the 116th replication holds 'across all models', but Table A3 has Freedom n.s. and Table A2 has Blue Dog Model A at -1.315***. Tables 2-3 are labelled undirected although the edgelist is directed.
- All coefficients are stratified by party. No pooled cross-party figure should be quoted. Every figure must name its window (115th or 116th House, LPAC giving only) and denominator (e.g. 238 R / 168 D connected nodes for the 115th).
- The paper does not argue for either rejected SNA proposal (dropping regular cosponsorship edges; per-edge volume normalization). It ignores weights by binarizing and recommends exploring amounts in future work. No conflict.
- LDA is not involved; nothing here touches D-021.
