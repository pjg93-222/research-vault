# Political DEBATE: Efficient Zero-shot and Few-shot Classifiers for Political Text

**Michael Burnham, Kayla Kahn, Ryan Yang Wang, Rachel X. Peng (2024).** *arXiv (arXiv:2409.02078v1, 3 Sep 2024; cs.CL).* Theme: [[themes/machine-learning-sentiment-analysis|Machine Learning & Sentiment Analysis]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Burnham, Kahn, Wang and Peng (arXiv 2024) release Political DEBATE: DeBERTa V3 base (86M) and large (304M) NLI classifiers, further trained on PolNLI. PolNLI holds 201,691 political documents and 852 hypotheses across stance, topic, hate-speech and event tasks, and its sources include congressional newsletters. On test hypotheses unseen in training, both models beat general NLI models, Llama 3.1 8B and Claude 3.5 Sonnet on zero-shot MCC. On the hardest stance set (quote tweets), Claude scores MCC 0.29, base 0.62 and large 0.88. In few-shot training, 10 to 25 random labeled documents are often enough. The base model, however, failed the bespoke COVID threat-minimization task and could not match a supervised classifier (MCC 0.66) even at 100 samples, while the large model matched it at 10 (0.67). The authors therefore recommend the large model whenever feasible, short single-condition hypotheses, and supervised training when labels need meta-knowledge or a bespoke concept. The labels were validated with GPT-4o, so GPT-4o is excluded from the benchmark. For Paul, this is the actual checkpoint behind the Melange CU press stance reader. The system fine-tunes the base model on 552 labeled releases. Its own history follows the paper's pattern: base zero-shot reached 53% accuracy with oppose F1 0.08, and fine-tuning raised it to 87% agreement with Claude. Its valence-hop bottleneck matches the paper's warning about bespoke concepts. The bearings are on the model choice (base, not large), hypothesis design (a compound neutral hypothesis), the evaluation metric (accuracy and kappa against an LLM reference rather than MCC against humans), and the planned ACU speaker-mode branching. Nothing here touches the SNA or LDA rules.

## Study

**Question:** Can small, domain-adapted natural language inference (NLI) encoder models trained on political text match or beat zero-shot large language models and supervised classifiers for political text classification, and can they be few-shot trained from 10-25 labeled documents, while staying open and efficient? (p.1-2)

**Theory:** No formal theory. Argument: NLI framing turns any classification into a document-hypothesis entailment task, and narrowing a small pretrained encoder to one domain reduces the model size needed for high performance; transfer from general NLI models speeds adaptation (p.2-4, p.8).

**Data:** PolNLI: 201,691 political documents, 852 unique entailment hypotheses, 22 datasets in four tasks: stance 11 datasets, 361 hypotheses, 66,581 documents; topic 5, 278, 62,005; hate-speech/toxicity 2, 177, 41,871; event extraction 4, 36, 31,234 (Table 1, p.5). Sources include tweets, news, congressional newsletters, legislation, party platforms, State of the Union speeches, Supreme Court case summaries, ACLED and SCAD event records, and crowd-sourced arguments (Appendix A, p.23). Unit: document paired with a hypothesis, labeled entail or not entail. After augmentation there are 2,834 unique entailment phrasings (p.7). Split: test and validation sets of about 15,000 documents each, split by hypothesis so test hypotheses are unseen in training; validation is about 10,000 unseen-hypothesis and 5,000 seen-hypothesis documents (p.7-8). Few-shot test cases: Mood of the Nation open-text democracy answers (liberty and rights) and COVID-19 threat-minimization tweets, about 2,000 tweets in Block Jr. et al. (p.12-14). Timing: 5,000 PolNLI test documents (p.14).

**Method:** Fine-tunes DeBERTa V3 base (86M parameters) and large (304M) models that were already trained for general NLI (Laurer et al. 2023), using the Transformers library (p.2, p.8). Label cleaning removes source metadata; hypotheses are written per label (p.5-6). Labels are validated by GPT-4o; documents where human and LLM labels disagree are dropped (p.6-7). Hypotheses are augmented with GPT-4o synonyms and manual review (p.7). Checkpoints chosen by training loss, validation loss, MCC, F1, accuracy and per-task consistency, plus qualitative tests with punctuation, capitalization and wording edits (p.8). Zero-shot benchmark against DeBERTa base and large NLI (Laurer et al. 2023), Llama 3.1 8B and Claude 3.5 Sonnet; GPT-4o not benchmarked (p.9). Few-shot: simple random samples of 10, 25, 50 and 100 documents, 10 repetitions each, 95% confidence intervals, default learning rate, 5 epochs, no hyperparameter or hypothesis search (p.12). Timing: same 5,000 documents with hypothesis 'This text is about politics' on RTX 3090, Tesla T4, M3 Max and Ryzen 9 5900x (p.14).

**Identification:** Not causal. Generalization is judged by splitting test documents on unseen hypotheses (p.7-8) and by few-shot learning curves at sample sizes 10, 25, 50 and 100 (p.12).

**Scope:** Population: English-language political text from social media, news, newsletters, legislation, party platforms, speeches, court summaries, event reports and crowd-sourced arguments (Appendix A, p.23). Language: English only (p.17). Era: sources span several years; references were accessed in June 2024 (p.19-22). Tasks: stance, topic, hate-speech and toxicity, and event classification (p.4).

## Key findings

- PolNLI test set, all tasks combined: DEBATE models are more performative than DeBERTa base and large NLI, Llama 3.1 8B and Claude 3.5 Sonnet in zero-shot MCC; per-model values appear only in Fig. 1 (p.9-10).
- Stance: DEBATE models and Claude 3.5 Sonnet are significantly better than the other models on stance detection; event extraction is comparable between DEBATE and the two LLMs (p.9).
- Hate-speech: DEBATE models are significantly better than the other models; the authors attribute this to subjective definitions that the models were tuned to (p.9-10).
- Polistance Quote Tweets (stance, hardest set): Claude 3.5 MCC 0.29; DEBATE base MCC 0.62 and large MCC 0.88; DeBERTa Large had negative correlation with the correct label on this dataset (p.10).
- Few-shot, Mood of the Nation liberty and rights: Llama 3.1 is best zero-shot (reported MCC 0.74, accuracy 88%); after 10 samples both DEBATE models jump and do not differ significantly from Llama; at 25 the large model significantly beats Llama; at 50 the base model matches it (p.13).
- Few-shot, COVID-19 threat minimization: base model largely fails zero-shot and does not match the supervised Electra classifier (MCC 0.66 on 2,000 tweets) at 100 samples; large model matches it at 10 samples (MCC 0.67, accuracy 87%) and exceeds it at 25 (MCC 0.69, accuracy 88%) (p.13-14).
- Efficiency: DEBATE models give large speed advantages over Llama 3.1 8B on all four tested hardware types; a laptop GPU (M3 Max) or free cloud GPU (Tesla T4) classifies documents at a brisk pace; exact timings are in Fig. 5 only (p.14-15).
- Training: base model learning rate 2e-5, batch 8, 20 epochs; large model learning rate 9e-6, batch 4 with gradient accumulation 4, 20 epochs; checkpoint per epoch (Appendix C, p.25-26).
- Recommendation: use the large model for zero-shot and most few-shot work; the base model for simple few-shot or supervised work (p.15-16).
- Recommendation: keep NLI hypotheses short and template-like; use few-shot training when a task needs long hypotheses with several conditions, or is far from the PolNLI sources (p.16-17).
- Stated limitation with a finding: the authors do not expect DEBATE to beat proprietary LLMs on all tasks; LLMs are expected to generalize better to tasks far from PolNLI (p.17).
- Validation: GPT-4o labels matched manual review on 92.5% of 400 sampled documents (Cohen's kappa 0.85); 14 of 400 (3.5%) were judged wrong LLM labels (p.7).

## Limitations

- Trained and evaluated only on English documents; performance on other languages is unknown (p.17).
- Not expected to outperform proprietary LLMs on every task; LLMs expected to generalize better to tasks far from PolNLI (p.17).
- NLI hypotheses must be short; no clear measure of task simplicity exists, only qualitative judgments (p.16).
- Labels for topic and event data were positive-only; negatives were created by random reassignment, and multi-topic documents are a known concern addressed only by validation (p.6-7).
- Authors discourage benchmarking OpenAI models on PolNLI because GPT-4o was used in label validation (p.10).
- Few-shot runs used no hyperparameter or hypothesis search, by design, so the results are not tuned (p.12).

## Gaps and flags

- No head-to-head of DEBATE base against DEBATE large, fine-tuned on the same 552 rows and scored on the member-grouped human gold and the adversarial frame.
- No MCC (overall or per class) is reported for the stance reader. The headline 87.2% is agreement with the Claude reference, not with human labels.
- The compound neutral hypothesis has not been tested against split hypotheses (not about CUs, versus neutral) or against a binary per-label setup.
- Training epochs (3, against 5 in the paper's few-shot runs) and the lede-only 1600-character input have no recorded sensitivity check on the human gold.
- The ACU speaker-mode plan has no test of base against large on two-voice (advocacy-group-about-member) texts.
- No conflict with the brain rules: the paper has no SNA, LDA, reciprocity or influence-edge content.
- The headline 87.2% / kappa 0.771 is agreement with an LLM (Claude) pre-labeller. By the paper's own reasoning about GPT-4o, it should not be quoted as human accuracy; quote the member-grouped human-gold figures and name their denominators.
- The paper's comparisons are author-run on author-built data whose labels were LLM-validated. Per-model aggregate MCC values appear only in figures and were not extracted.
- The paper's results come from binary entailment tasks. The system's 3-class CU stance is outside the reported test conditions, so the transfer is UNVERIFIED until a base-versus-large run on CU gold reproduces it.

Paul has no entry for this work, though the deployed CU stance reader is its base checkpoint. His DEBATE position cites Kim, Birkenmaier, Peng and Taherdoost, not Burnham, so it is stale. He calls SVM outdated next to LLMs. The paper partly agrees, but mainly for the large model: base lost to a supervised classifier on a bespoke task. The system's figures fit that (DEBATE 0.796 vs stack 0.837; LinearSVC 83.9% vs transformer 0.757). His noted weakness with ambiguous statements matches the hard two-voice quote-tweet result.
