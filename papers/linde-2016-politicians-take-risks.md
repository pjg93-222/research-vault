# Do Politicians Take Risks Like the Rest of Us? An Experimental Test of Prospect Theory Under MPs

**Jona Linde, Barbara Vis (2017).** *Political Psychology, Vol. 38, No. 1, pp. 101-117 (copyright line 2016).* [10.1111/pops.12335](https://doi.org/10.1111/pops.12335). Theme: [[themes/adjacent|Adjacent]].

> [!abstract] Summary
> Linde and Vis ran an incentivized online experiment with 46 sitting Dutch MPs in 2014-2015, about 30% of the Tweede Kamer. They asked whether politicians depart from expected utility theory the way prospect theory predicts.
> 
> **What holds up**
> - **Reflection effect in the paid lottery task.** The risky option was chosen by 39.1% under a gain frame and 76.1% under a loss frame (p = 0.0002, n = 46).
> - **Asian disease problem.** The risky option was chosen by 88.5% under a loss frame and 30% under a gain frame (odds ratio 14.6).
> 
> **What does not hold up**
> - **Political scenarios.** Reflection appears only in the scenario where votes and economic outcomes are both at stake: 85% vs 69%, Fisher p = 0.30. It reaches 5% significance only when all four scenarios are pooled (odds ratio 8.68, p = 0.033, n = 164 rather than the expected 184, which is unexplained). The regression covers all four scenarios; two (economic, votes) show no effect and the Asian disease scenario is strongly significant. The 8.68 is the scenario-specific loss-frame interaction for votes plus economic outcomes, not a single pooled loss-frame odds ratio. On this basis the authors say, as suggestive evidence, that votes rather than policy set politicians' reference point.
> - **Other effects.** No common ratio effect was found. The lottery-valuation tasks (n = 28) show nothing significant. Neither sample shows loss aversion (fn 4, p.105). Age, tenure, sex and ideology predict nothing.
> - **"Smaller than other people."** The authors ran a student comparison sample (n=176, Appendix E, not in the file) and cite it in the main text, along with earlier literature. No formal MP-versus-student test is reported in the available text.
> 
> **Why it matters here**
> Melange cites this paper twice:
> - **Receptivity layer.** Members in a live race are put in a "loss domain" with pt_mult 1.15, and safe members get 0.92.
> - **Money response.** LOSSFRAME = 1.10 makes competitor money move a member's lean more than equal CU money. The comment describes this as loss aversion "elite-attenuated per Linde-Vis".
> 
> The paper supports the reflection effect itself. It does not support:
> - loss aversion, which it did not find;
> - treating seat insecurity as a loss frame, since framing was randomly manipulated (and, from outside knowledge not stated in the paper, Dutch MPs are elected from national party lists, not single-member seats);
> - equating risk-seeking (preferring higher-variance outcomes) with being easier to move by money;
> - any of the constants 1.15, 0.92 or 1.10.

## Study

**Question:** (1) Does prospect theory describe decisions by politicians as it does those of other people, or are politicians unlike the rest of us? (2) When outcomes span several dimensions (votes and economic policy), which dimension serves as the reference point for politicians?

**Theory:** Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky 1979; cumulative version Tversky & Kahneman 1992) versus expected utility theory. Tested regularities: probability weighting (overweighting of small probabilities, underweighting of large ones), the common ratio effect (Allais), the reflection effect (risk seeking for losses, risk aversion for gains, via probability weighting and diminishing sensitivity), loss aversion, and the reference-point dependence of risk attitudes. Risk is defined as outcome variance (p.104).

**Data:** Sample: 46 sitting Dutch MPs in the lower chamber (Tweede Kamer), about 30% of parliament (p.103, p.107). Unit: individual MP. Period: sessions from September 2014 to January 2015, with no elections or major political events in that window (p.107). Sample traits: 28% female, 81% university-educated, mean age 43.5, 76.2% describe themselves as (very) right-wing (p.107). Lottery-valuation tasks use n = 28 (Table 2, p.109); the pooled political-scenario logistic regression uses n = 164 (Table 5, p.112). The text does not explain why 164 rather than 46 x 4 = 184. Validation comparison: student sample n = 176 (Appendix E, not in file; also n = 102 for the incentivized subset of the student lottery-valuation tasks).

**Method:** Lab-style online experiment, individually completed with at least one researcher present (p.107). Thirteen decision tasks in three parts, with part order and task order within parts randomized (p.107). Part I: six lottery-choice tasks (common ratio tasks 1-2, reflection tasks 5-6, and others) testing EUT deviations. Part II: three lottery-valuation tasks using a multiple-price list (p.109). Part III: four hypothetical political scenarios, each with a gain or loss frame (p.111). Task-related incentives: one randomly selected task (from the lottery-choice or lottery-valuation part) determines a donation to one of seven charities (p.107). For loss frames, the maximum possible loss is added to the donation so that gain and loss frames have identical payoffs (p.109). Analysis: binomial tests (Tables 1 and 3), Fisher's exact tests (Table 4), and logistic regression with robust standard errors clustered by individual (Table 5, p.112).

**Identification:** Within-subject randomized framing. For each political scenario the gain or loss frame was randomly assigned, and the frame and scenario order were randomized, so one MP could see different frames across scenarios (p.111). Reflection tasks 5 and 6 hold final outcomes equal by adding EUR 60 to task 6 (p.110). Political scenarios use earlier expectations so that absolute outcomes match across frames (p.111). Identification of the reference point rests on comparing which frame affects choices in each scenario (economic, votes, votes plus economic). The authors state the trade-off explicitly: control over experimental realism (p.110).

**Scope:** Population: members of the lower chamber of the Dutch parliament, one country and one period (2014-2015). The authors argue the results generalize to other Western democracies (p.103) but do not test this. Setting: a controlled, incentivized experiment plus hypothetical advice-to-caucus scenarios about votes on a proposal. Individual decisions only (fn 12, p.110). Student sample is a validation sample, not a comparison group for the main conclusions in the main text.

## Key findings

- Strong reflection effect in the incentivized lottery-choice task: the risky option is chosen far more often when outcomes are framed as losses. (Table 3, p.110; text p.110) Numbers: 39.13% (gain frame, task 5) vs 76.09% (loss frame, task 6); difference 36.96 pp; binomial p = 0.0002; n = 46
- No common ratio effect for politicians, which the authors read as a departure from prospect theory's probability-weighting account and a similarity to other people in this respect. (Table 1, p.108; conclusion p.114) Numbers: 63.04% (task 1) vs 71.74% (task 2); difference 8.70 pp; binomial p = 0.4545; n = 46
- Lottery valuations show the predicted direction of probability weighting and the reflection pattern, but no differences are statistically significant, and the authors call the probability-weighting evidence weak. (Table 2, p.109; text p.109-110) Numbers: Average valuation minus expected value, gain treatment: EUR +0.16 (10%), +0.28 (50%), -0.41 (90%); loss treatment: EUR -0.25 (10%), +0.75 (50%), +1.13 (90%); n = 28; no significant differences
- Asian disease scenario (classic reflection problem) shows a strong, significant reflection effect in the political sample. (Table 4, p.112; text p.111) Numbers: Risky option: 88.46% under loss frame vs 30% under gain frame; Fisher's exact p < 0.001; logistic loss-frame odds ratio 14.633 (p = 0.001), Table 5
- Hypothetical political scenarios: reflection effect appears only in the votes-plus-economic scenario, and only when pooled with the other scenarios at the 5% level; the single-scenario gap is not significant. (Table 4 p.112; Table 5 p.112; text p.111) Numbers: Economic outcomes: 45% (loss) vs 50% (gain), Fisher p = 0.774. Votes: 45.83% vs 40.91%, p = 0.774. Votes and economic (loss in votes): 85% vs 69.23%, p = 0.302; scenario-specific loss-frame interaction odds ratio 8.684 from a logistic regression over all four scenarios, p = 0.033 (n = 164).
- Reference point: votes appear to be the dimension that drives politicians' reflection effect when votes and economic outcomes are both at stake, with the effect weaker than in the single-dimension lottery tasks. (Abstract p.101; text p.113-114; conclusion p.114) Numbers: Votes-plus-economic scenario: loss frame 85% vs gain 69.23% risky; authors call the evidence 'less strong but nevertheless suggestive'
- Loss aversion not found in the MP sample (nor in the student sample), which the authors attribute possibly to the validity of the loss-aversion tasks (house-money effect). (p.105 and fn 4, p.105) Numbers: No numbers reported in the main text for loss aversion
- Probability-weighting and political-scenario findings are generally in the direction predicted by prospect theory, but politicians' deviations are described as somewhat smaller than those of other people, a claim based on the authors' student comparison sample (n=176, Appendix E, not in the file) and prior literature; no formal MP-vs-student test is reported in the available text. (Abstract p.101; p.108 (EUT-consistency remark); conclusion p.114) Numbers: No direct MP-vs-student deviation test reported in the main text
- Background covariates (age, time in parliament, sex, left/right ideology) do not significantly predict risky choices. (Table 5 p.112; text p.108 and p.112) Numbers: Time in parliament OR 0.995 (p = 0.580); age OR 1.033 (p = 0.259); man OR 2.077 (p = 0.177); right-wing OR 1.510 (p = 0.500); left-wing OR 0.290 (p = 0.173)

## Limitations

- Small sample (46 MPs, 28 for valuations); authors state the valuation tasks lack power and no valuation difference is significant (p.109).
- Loss-aversion tasks may lack validity; participants lose house money, not their own (fn 4, p.105); loss aversion not found in either sample.
- Hypothetical political scenarios trade realism for control; the authors acknowledge this and state the scenarios are a simplification (p.110).
- Alternative explanations for the votes-scenario result are not separated: socially desirable answers (MPs avoid admitting electoral motives) and task familiarity (p.112-114).
- Probabilities are known (risk, not ambiguity), which the authors justify by citing similar behavior under ambiguity (p.107).
- Politicians cannot be paid, so incentives are a charity donation; the political scenarios are not incentivized (p.107).
- Sample is 76.2% right-wing, so the study may be a conservative test of prospect theory, but the sample is too small to test ideology effects directly (p.114).
- Student sample and incentive details are in the online supporting information (Appendix E), not in the text; the main-text claim that politicians deviate less than other people is not tested directly.
- Generalization to other Western democracies is asserted, not tested (p.103).
- The text does not explain why the pooled regression has n = 164 rather than 184 (46 x 4 scenarios).

## Gaps and flags

- No measured reference point for members. The paper says the reference point is electoral (votes) and depends on earlier expectations. The system uses seat security or an electoral play as a fixed proxy for 'loss domain' and never models expectations, such as a member's prior margin or forecast swing.
- The system has no evidence that risk-seeking in the paper's sense (choosing higher-variance outcomes) means being more responsive to money or pressure. pt_mult assumes that link, and nothing in the system tests it.
- Domain-specific framing is missing. The paper finds reflection only when votes are at stake. The system applies one pt_mult to every CU issue, whether or not the issue carries electoral consequences for the member.
- There is no ablation of the Linde-Vis constants. The sensitivity harness lacks a pt-off arm and a LOSSFRAME-off arm, so no one has checked whether these unsupported constants change targeting decisions.
- Competitiveness is counted several times. Seat security drives incent_mult (neutralized under COMPET_MULT), the pt loss test (security<0.45 or a defend/contest play), and, per D-104, the Cook-PVI multiplier c. The paper gives no support for stacking an electoral-risk effect on top of a competitiveness effect.
- Misattribution in code: receptivity_layer.py:23 says politicians are 'loss-averse overall', and LOSSFRAME 1.10 (money_response.py:28-31, receptivity_layer.py:26-28) cites loss aversion 'elite-attenuated per Linde-Vis'. The paper found NO loss aversion in either sample (fn 4, p.105). audit_full.py:62-63 locks the asymmetry in as 'by design' with this citation.
- The 'MPs' framing effects are smaller than students'' premise (receptivity_layer.py:28) is not formally tested in the available text. It rests on the authors' student comparison sample (n=176, Appendix E, not in the file) and earlier literature. The paid-lottery reflection effect among MPs is large: 36.96 pp.
- Any quote of the OR 8.684 (p=0.033) must say it is the scenario-specific loss-frame interaction for votes plus economic outcomes in a regression over all four scenarios. Two of the four scenarios show no effect and the Asian disease scenario is strongly significant. The n is 164, not 184, and the paper does not explain why.
- Transfer limit: the sample is 46 Dutch list-PR MPs (76.2% right-wing) answering hypothetical scenarios that were not incentivized. Mapping their results onto US House and Senate seat security is an inference the paper does not test.
- The paper does not argue for either of the rejected SNA proposals, and it makes no reciprocity, closeness or access claims. The brain wording rules are not triggered.
