Alexander, Raquel Meyer, et al. “Measuring rates of return for lobbying expenditures: An empirical case study of tax breaks for multinational corporations.” *SSRN Electronic Journal*, 2009, [https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.1375082](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.1375082).  
  
“*We find firms lobbying for this provision have a return in excess of $220 for every $1 spent on lobbying, or* ***22,000%****. Repatriating firms are more profitable overall, but surprisingly, profitability is not a predictor of repatriation amount. Rather, industry and firm size are most predictive of repatriation.*” While this study is an outlier in terms of the size of its findings, it is a dramatic illustration of the ROI that political lobbying brings to companies that successfully pass legislation beneficial to their industry. Additionally, it addresses the question of scale and company size as factors in achieving such success, highlighting a significant positive correlation between the two variables.

Bickmore, Elizabeth, et al. “Corporate lobbying, political action committees, and market concentration.” *Public Choice*, 17 Oct. 2025, [https://doi.org/10.1007/s11127-025-01337-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11127-025-01337-7).

A recent study that robustly tests the idea of political lobbying as a means of enhancing company market share, using various linear and multivariate tests and controlling for industry fixed effects. The authors find that companies that deploy lobbying firms exhibit a **7.5% to 10.5%** higher market concentration than companies of comparable size in the same industry.

Huneeus, Federico, and In Song Kim. “The effects of firms’ lobbying on resource misallocation.” *SSRN Electronic Journal*, 2018, [https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3275097](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3275097).

“*We find that a 10 percent increase in lobbying expenditure leads to a 3 percent gain in revenue.*” This article examines both the macro and micro-level effects of company spending on lobbying, showing a robust increase in individual company revenue, if at the expense of the broader market production, not industry-specific, but broader economy as a whole.

Kalla, Joshua L., and David E. Broockman. “Campaign contributions facilitate access to Congressional officials: A randomized field experiment.” *American Journal of Political Science*, vol. 60, no. 3, 2 Apr. 2015, pp. 545–558, [https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12180](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12180).

This article by Professors Furnas, LaPira, and others explores how the identity of a political information giver affects the recipient's receptiveness to that information in the context of legislative advocacy. By using a novel survey of 400 federal congressional staffers, where the identity of the individual or interest, the nature of the request, and the source of the statistics for said recommendation are provided, the authors reveal an “*epistemic closure among partisan staffers*.” They make clear their recommendation that an interest group should base its findings on evidence from a think tank that is ideologically aligned with the office it is engaging with. Put simply, the staffers want to hear only what they want to hear, will be psychologically averse to institutions they perceive as against their interests, and will be hesitant towards groups they perceive as pursuing their own goals. The implications of these findings are profound and can be seen as an explanation for the donation behavior of large dark-money funds. By channeling funds toward the building of ideological infrastructure, such groups are not recruiting their own champions, but cultivating them. Finally, regarding the current research, allocating venture capital funds to the growth of industry-aligned think tanks should be considered, scouted, and ultimately incorporated into future allocation models.

Kim, In Song, et al. “Systemic and sequential links between campaign donations and lobbying.” *The Journal of Politics*, vol. 88, no. 1, 1 Jan. 2026, pp. 110–124, [https://doi.org/10.1086/734531](https://doi.org/10.1086/734531).

“*Specifically, we find that previous donations are associated with an increase of* ***8–11 percentage points*** *in the likelihood of the targeted politician’s involvement in lobbied legislative bills. The estimated effects are large, increase over time, and are particularly pronounced for committee-related activities.*” This article is very pertinent to the present discussion as it directly tests the likelihood that a representative is to be influenced by an advocacy group under the pretext of past political contributions. Importantly, the author describes the observed phenomena as something that compounds but quickly fades, as the observed longevity of such favor granted by a political donation is pronounced for only **6 months**, but then fades soon after, unless renewed which then such favor is compounded upon.

Kim, Jiseon, et al. *Measuring Interest Group Positions on Legislation: An AI-Driven Analysis of Lobbying Reports*. 2025.

Professors from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology use Large Language Models and Graph Neural Networks to analyze and underpin the correlation between lobbying firms, the direction and shape of policy preferences, and ultimately derive insights regarding lobbying strategies and firm behavior. This article is foundational to the current research, as it provides a novel, large-scale, and pre-built data pipeline that can be directly implemented on a social network analysis vector graph, yielding insights into the behavior of competing firms. There are three main findings uncovered by the authors: A strong correlation between the bills' place and the legislative process and the positions taken by the interest group; the firm's size plays a key role in the adoption of monitoring positions; and industries align their efforts on issue-specific stakes. Finally, there are two main weaknesses relevant to the current research. First, this method is reactive rather than predictive due to the time lag between the release of filing reports and current industry positions. Finally, this method doesn’t explicitly highlight conflict between industries over a specific bill, which is vital for political intelligence.
