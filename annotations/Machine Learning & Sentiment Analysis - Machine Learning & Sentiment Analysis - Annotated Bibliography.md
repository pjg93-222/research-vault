Alkazzi, Jean-Marc, and Keisuke Okumura. “A comprehensive review on leveraging machine learning for multi-agent path finding.” *IEEE Access*, vol. 12, 22 Apr. 2024, pp. 57390–57409, [https://doi.org/10.1109/access.2024.3392305](https://doi.org/10.1109/access.2024.3392305).

Birkenmaier, Lukas, and Clemens Lechner. “Measuring politicians’ public personality traits using computational text analysis: A multimethod feasibility study for agency and Communion.” *Political Analysis*, 2 Dec. 2025, pp. 1–19, [https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2025.10025](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2025.10025).

This article by German Professors Lukas Birkenmaier and Clemens Lechner explores the use of computational text analysis of public statements to derive the personality traits of political figures using third-generation AI and both supervised and semi-supervised machine learning. The article delves into the utilization and validity of cues, how to properly distinguish between them, and the domain-specific traits of agency and communion. This article is highly relevant to the design of nodes and is applicable to other avenues within the present research, as it delves into acquiring politically relevant personality traits, observing them, and pipelining them. Methods such as various codebooks will be necessary for continued research and are pre-built into the article, providing easy access. Finally, two weaknesses in the article are as follows: how to convert an individual's findings into a node in a social network analysis graph, and how to disentangle ambiguous statements.

Kim, Jison, et al. *Measuring Interest Group Positions on Legislation: An AI-Driven Analysis of Lobbying Reports*, 23 Apr. 2025.

Professors from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology use Large Language Models and Graph Neural Networks to analyze and underpin the correlation between lobbying firms, the direction and shape of policy preferences, and ultimately derive insights regarding lobbying strategies and firm behavior. This article is foundational to the current research, as it provides a novel, large-scale, and pre-built data pipeline that can be directly implemented on a social network analysis vector graph, yielding insights into the behavior of competing firms. There are three main findings uncovered by the authors: A strong correlation between the bills' place and the legislative process and the positions taken by the interest group; the firm's size plays a key role in the adoption of monitoring positions; and industries align their efforts on issue-specific stakes. Finally, there are two main weaknesses relevant to the current research. First, this method is reactive rather than predictive due to the time lag between the release of filing reports and current industry positions. Finally, this method doesn’t explicitly highlight conflict between industries over a specific bill, which is vital for political intelligence.

Peng, Sancheng, et al. “A survey on Deep Learning for textual emotion analysis in social networks.” *Digital Communications and Networks*, vol. 8, no. 5, Oct. 2022, pp. 745–762, [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcan.2021.10.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcan.2021.10.003).

This article, by a group of Chinese and Australian professors, provides an extensive overview of the current state of Deep Learning as it applies to Textual Emotional Analysis. It provides a comprehensive overview of model training as it pertains to the construction and definition of emotions and codebooks. The following models are of interest to the current research: Emoji2Vec, Context2Vec, KUBWE, Emo2Vec, DeepMoji, CCTSenEmb, and DisSent. The main obstacle for most of these models is long-distance dependency; however, the authors detail how careful algorithmic stacking can reduce, but not eliminate, this obstacle. The main weakness of this article is that it provides little guidance on how to process and convert the output of these algorithms into actionable data and nodes that can be added to a vector graph. Finally, a gap exists in the reader's technical understanding of what is being fully discussed. An additional review of the article, after gaining a deeper understanding of the topic, would be optimal.

Ramey, Adam, et al. “Talking heads: Measuring elite personality using speech.” *SSRN Electronic Journal*, 2015, [https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2605644](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2605644).

This article, while dated, is foundational for examining elite personality through congressional speeches using the Big Five personality traits. While this article is essentially a recap from more recent studies, it is necessary to look back at the original methodology for the sake of understanding what they are examining, how they code to examine it, and how they ultimately apply the data, which is convenient for this article as the authors use a case study of the data. The main source of the data comes from the auditory and textual transcripts of congressional floor speeches. The case study provides a wealth of insight into future political strategy for fine-tuning policy proposals to be most appealing to the MOC being lobbied. Finally, the main remaining weakness is the lack of a pipeline for other venues of textual analysis not covered in the present article. Additionally, beyond the pipeline itself, a framework specific to the format's contextual or auditory constraints is needed to accurately capture the psychological dimensions—see other articles in the collected corpus.
