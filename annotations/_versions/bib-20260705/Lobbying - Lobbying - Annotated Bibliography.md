Aref, Samin, and Zachary P. Neal. “Identifying hidden coalitions in the US House of Representatives by optimally partitioning signed networks based on generalized balance.” *Scientific Reports*, vol. 11, no. 1, 7 Oct. 2021, [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-98139-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-98139-w).

This article by Stanford Professors Aref and Neal examines the use of three-cluster partitioned networks to identify hidden and unofficial partnerships and coalitions within the House of Representatives, highlighting highly effective legislative groups and duos whose actions otherwise avoid typical headlines. The two main models utilized are an optimal k-partition and k-clusterability index. These methods, when used in tandem, allow for frustrated edges, a result of at times 30,000 variables, to be smoothed over. This methodology is highly valuable to the present research as it both presents an alternative layer that can be substituted or added into future network models, but more importantly allows for massive sets of data to be made computationally efficient, something that will become necessary as additional pipelines are worked into future models. Additionally, the main finding of this paper was a third general coalition within the House of Representatives, one whose median ideology is highly unstable yet highly effective. This coalition can be seen as the truly vulnerable or responsive seats, those like Brian Fitzpatrick who are forced to swing ideologically toward their constituents because their districts are so competitive. Finally, the fundamental weakness of this article is that there is no path-finding algorithm as of yet, and the authors did not measure the shifts of this coalition over time to see how this coalition's ideology responded to changes in national mood.

Bacik, Karol A, et al. *Measuring the Dynamical Evolution of the United States Lobbying Network*, 6 Feb. 2026.

This article by Massachusetts Institute of Technology professors, spearheaded by Professors Karol A. Bacik and In Song Kim, explores the evolution of the congressional lobbying ecosystem, while also presenting a novel database called LobbyView that is highly relevant to the present research and is a fundamental source of data points in models being outlined within the present research. It should be noted that the researchers use case studies to derive empirical observations, such as preferential attachment as a key trait in lobbyists' careers, the role of the upcoming election cycle in terms of who and what is being targeted, and the value of adaptation in response to emerging political events. However, the magnum opus of this article is the data set itself, which can be used to create probabilistic models. These models should be reviewed and dissected to gain a fuller understanding of how they function and can ultimately be augmented. One such route of augmentation is the incorporation of news cycles, social media feeds, and press statements, analyzed via sentiment analysis, as determinants of the likelihood of change within the current pathway and dynamic.

Furnas, Alexander C., Timothy M LaPira, Alexander Hertel-Fernandez, et al. “More than mere access: An experiment on moneyed interests, information provision, and legislative action in Congress.” *Political Research Quarterly*, vol. 76, no. 1, 13 May 2022, pp. 348–364, [https://doi.org/10.1177/10659129221098743](https://doi.org/10.1177/10659129221098743).

This article by Professors Furnas, LaPira, and various others explores the effects that the identity of a giver of political information has on the receptiveness of the recipient of said political information within the context of legislative advocacy. By using a novel survey of 400 federal congressional staffers, where the identity of the individual or interest, the nature of the request, and the source of the statistics for said recommendation are provided, the authors reveal an “epistemic closure among partisan staffers.” They make clear their recommendation that an interest group should base their findings around evidence from a think-tank that is ideologically aligned with the office they are engaging in. Put simply, the staffers want to hear only what they want to hear and will be psychologically adverse to institutions they perceive as against their interests, and hesitant towards groups that they perceive as pursuing their own goals. The implications for these findings are profound, and can be seen as an explanation for the donation behavior of large dark money funds. By channeling funds toward the building of ideological infrastructure, such groups are not recruiting their own champions, but cultivating them. Finally, in regards to the current research, allocation of venture capital funds to the growth of industry-aligned think tanks should be considered, scouted, and ultimately incorporated into future allocation models.

Harrison, Elizabeth, et al. “An application of data mining techniques to explore congressional lobbying records for patterns in pediatric special interest expenditures prior to the Affordable Care Act.” *Frontiers in Big Data*, vol. 1, 20 Aug. 2018, [https://doi.org/10.3389/fdata.2018.00003](https://doi.org/10.3389/fdata.2018.00003).

This article by professors from the University of Virginia, Charlottesville explores the use of data-mining in lobbying records to measure the influence of special interest groups in the legislative process of congressional bills, most notably with the Affordable Care Act (Obamacare). It provides a cursory study into how to construct the models necessary for the pipelining of such information, through the use of Python pandas, where to obtain such information, such as the LDA, and how to transform said data into actionable information. It is relatively important to the current research in that it provides a foundational basis for the processing of such data and the do’s and don’ts of such methods. However, the results from this data are lack-luster and this is likely a result of the authors limitations in-regards to their technical ability, and other articles, such as Professor Kim’s, provide a database of a far greater magnitude. Still, for an introductory overview of the pipelining of such data, this article proves adequate.

Huneeus, Federico, and In Song Kim. “The effects of firms’ lobbying on Resource Misallocation.” *SSRN Electronic Journal*, 12 Nov. 2018, [https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3275097](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3275097).

This article by Massachusetts Institute of Technology Professor In Song Kim and Princeton Professor Federico Huneeus examines the optimization, and more specifically misallocation, of company revenue on political lobbying. The main finding is that a 10% increase in lobbying expenditures leads to a 3% gain in revenue. Additionally, the article introduces a novel methodology to capture this finding, employing a general equilibrium firm model with endogenous variables. The fundamental application of this paper to the present research is the ability to set fundraising goals and use current models as a basis to inform appropriate amounts for future targets and institutions. However, there are several gaps within the literature as they pertain to the present research. First, the model does not distinguish campaign donations from other activities, such as events and meetings. Second, the models make no distinction between the representatives; however, their state layer can provide insight into who should be targeted based on general industry presence. In all, Professor Kim delivers another key step toward the optimization and empirical application of mathematical models and principles in the realm of political advocacy, serving as a basis for business-applicable models.
