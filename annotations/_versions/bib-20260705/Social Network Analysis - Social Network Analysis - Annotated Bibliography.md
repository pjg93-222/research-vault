Ardekani, Iman, et al. “Computational and Graph-Theoretic Analysis of Legislative Networks: New Zealand’s Mental Health Act as a Case Study.” *MDPI*, Multidisciplinary Digital Publishing Institute, 5 Feb. 2026, [www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/17/2/161](http://www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/17/2/161).

The article presents a case study of a computational framework used to analyze legislative citations for a single act in the New Zealand Parliament. This framework can be applied to the present research regarding how they mathematically compute the various nodes, vertices, and vectors. Additionally, machine learning methods used in the article, such as Named Entity Recognition (NER) or IE, can be applied to data extraction and processing in the applied product. Finally, the combination of structural graph analytics and semantic representation offers a potential layer to the ultimate analysis. The main drawback of this article is that it focuses on the legal citations of a bill and does not track key elements of lobbying and consulting, such as co-sponsorships, monetary congestion, or other variables. More thought will be needed as to how this can be practically applied to the intended product. However, the article goes into depth on the Louvian method, which can be used by communities and coalitions. Additional research into different forms of centrality is needed.

Borg, Riikka, et al. “Social Capital and governance: A social network analysis of forest biodiversity collaboration in Central Finland.” Forest Policy and Economics, vol. 50, Jan. 2015, pp. 90–97, [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forpol.2014.06.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forpol.2014.06.008).

This article explores the use of social network analysis to facilitate collaboration among different political actors, drawing on both qualitative and quantitative evidence.

Battaglini, Marco, and Eleonora Patacchini. Influencing Connected Legislators, Oct. 2016, [https://doi.org/10.3386/w22739](https://doi.org/10.3386/w22739).

Bonica, Adam. “Mapping the Ideological Marketplace.” *American Journal of Political Science*, John Wiley & Sons, 1 Jan. 1970, ideas.repec.org/a/wly/amposc/v58y2014i2p367-386.html.

Fink, Christian G., et al. “A Congressional twitter network dataset quantifying pairwise probability of influence.” *Data in Brief*, vol. 50, 28 Aug. 2023, p. 109521, [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dib.2023.109521](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dib.2023.109521).

This article presents a novel dataset highlighting the probabilities of influence among members of Congress, derived from Twitter’s API.  The data is presented as a social network, with edge weights representing the probabilities of influence, composed of quotes, retweets, mentions, and replies. It provides a fundamental, pre-built framework for pipelining the data required for the present research. The Viral Centrality Algorithm should be interrogated further, both for its ability to reduce compute time and for its ability to simulate scenarios that, by design, are similar to a Monte Carlo simulation. The two main weaknesses of the article are as follows: whether it quantifies a reply or mention as negative; how it quantifies agreement between two or more actors within the ecosystem when the sentiment is negative toward another member; and the necessity for temporal weighting to capture shifting relationships. Nonetheless, it is a fundamental framework for capturing the psychological and relational characteristics of actors within the ecosystem and among them.

Gaynor, SoRelle Wyckoff. “The (financial) ties that bind: Social networks of intraparty caucuses.” *Legislative Studies Quarterly*, vol. 47, no. 4, 22 Sept. 2021, pp. 885–920, [https://doi.org/10.1111/lsq.12360](https://doi.org/10.1111/lsq.12360).

This article outlines the connection between campaign donations by members of Congress to members of various caucuses, whether shared or not. It applies an Exponential Random Graph Model to a Social Network Analysis. The data collection methods are highly valuable for presenting the research through the following methods: overlaying caucus partisanship using the mean DW-Nominate score of all members within said caucus, using Cook's Partisan Voting Index to measure electoral vulnerability, and using LPAC donations as a directed edge relationship. This article offers several important insights into how to structure the edges, nodes, and data infrastructure for the present research, particularly in representing the members themselves as nodes using DW-Nominate. Additionally, the article reveals the effect that party leadership has on general giving and hints at the idea that increased leadership results in greater insulation from the broader ecosystem, a factor that must be taken into account in the present research. The main weaknesses within this article are gaps in applying this method to committee action, voting, and bill co-sponsorship, all of which are directly relevant to the current research.

Haythornthwaite, Caroline. “Social Network Analysis: An approach and technique for the study of Information Exchange.” *Library &amp; Information Science Research*, vol. 18, no. 4, Sept. 1996, pp. 323–342, [https://doi.org/10.1016/s0740-8188(96)90003-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0740-8188(96)90003-1).

This article is the foundational study of the Social Network Analysis method, providing an original overview of its methodology, reasoning, and empirical workings. By dissecting the method of how it works, various relationships ultimately leading to a causal outcome, Social Network Analysis can be applied empirically, with an almost unlimited variation of data collection, to different domains. When applying the method across domains, the article notes that one must consider how the general availability of information affects the ultimate validity of the structure, which is particularly important in the field of politics, where word of mouth and behind-closed-doors dealings are common. Finally, it emphasizes the directionality and structure of the ultimate social network as paramount when examining emerging leaders, trends, and overall findings. While the article is excellent at viewing social networks from a theoretical perspective, it is thin on how to construct graphs and build data pipelines, likely due to its age. Diagnosis: Priority – Medium – Theory

Kim, In Song, and Dmitriy Kunisky. “Mapping political communities: A statistical analysis of lobbying networks in Legislative Politics.” *Political Analysis*, vol. 29, no. 3, 23 Nov. 2020, pp. 317–336, [https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2020.29](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2020.29).

This article by MIT professors In Song Kim and Dimitriy Kuinsky applies the theory of Social Network Analysis to the lobbying industry and members of Congress, using a novel Bipartite Link Community Model that captures mixed membership and representative congestion within an MOC. On the topic of “mixed interactions observed between actors,” what this means specifically is that, within the model, one can see that different interactions between the same two actors as a result of one of the actors, such as the Chamber of Commerce, taking on multiple, each distinct, issues. Regarding the present research, it is crucial for both data pipelines to capture congestion, as well as to identify champions for rival or ally industries and measure the level of commitment those champions have toward those industries. Its unique lobbying database is incredibly extensive and utilizes textual extraction from lobbying reports using machine learning. A weakness of this article as it pertains to the present research, is how to transform the bipartite nature of the current model into a graph format, where path-finding algorithms can be run. Regardless, the current model provides a useful tool for identifying champions and connections across rival industries.

Malaterre, Christophe, and Francis Lareau. “Inferring social networks from unstructured text data: A proof of concept detection of hidden communities of interest.” *Data &amp*; Policy, vol. 6, 2024, [https://doi.org/10.1017/dap.2023.48](https://doi.org/10.1017/dap.2023.48).

This article focuses on the methodological approach to turning unstructured data, such as long policy memos, into refined data that can be input as vectors and edges within a social network analysis graph. The main theory emphasizes the use of shared semantic content between seemingly unrelated groups using a fitted topic model. It provides a good foundational overview of how Social Network Analysis operates and how to craft your own framework. One potential extension of the current research is to infer the level of closeness between two congressional offices based on mapping their co-occurrences within texts or on general textual similarities—requiring a temporal element. Additionally, this topic model could be domain-specific to the trade association or consulting partner assigned to it. This also mentions Louvian community detection. Finally, note how they slimmed down the articles to increase computational efficiency. The author explicitly mentions this method in its application to advocacy. Diagnosis: Priority – Very High – Foundational

Mason, Porter, and Peter Mucha. A Network Analysis of Committees in the U.S. House of Representatives | *PNAS*, 5 Apr. 2005, [www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0500191102](http://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0500191102).

This article, conducted by Professors Mason Porter, Peter Mucha, M.E. Newman, and Casey Warmbrand, examines the United States House of Representatives, and more specifically its subcommittees, through the lens of Social Network Analysis, revealing strong linkages within certain committees and a general hierarchical structure. The network consists of two nodes: a two-mode committee network and a one-mode representative network, with edges representing the common overlap between the two. The multi-layering of this social network is prevalent to the current research by directly outlining the entire ecosystem of the U.S House of Representatives on the committee level, and details of the correlations between the overall partisan alignment and committee selection. The main weaknesses are as follows: First for correlation between partisan alignment and committee selection, what came first, the chicken or the egg? Second, which pertains to the present research, is how to display both the members and the committees on the same graph and whether that can be done and/or is feasible.
